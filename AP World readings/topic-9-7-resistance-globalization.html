1. What were "structural adjustment programs" and why did they generate protest?
Structural adjustment programs (SAPs) were conditions imposed by the IMF and World Bank on developing countries in exchange for loans and debt relief. Typically they required: reducing government spending (cutting subsidies on food and fuel, reducing public sector employment), privatizing state-owned enterprises, eliminating trade barriers (opening markets to foreign competition), and deregulating industries. The theory was that these market-oriented reforms would promote growth and allow countries to repay their debts. In practice, critics argued they consistently harmed the poor: cutting food subsidies raised prices for the most vulnerable; privatization of public utilities raised prices for basic services; opening markets to foreign competition destroyed local industries; reduced social spending eliminated healthcare and education for low-income populations. Countries like Mexico, Argentina, and many African nations implemented SAPs and experienced economic crises, unemployment, and social unrest. The 1994 Zapatista uprising in Mexico partly responded to NAFTA and SAP-era economic policies. Anti-SAP riots occurred across Latin America and Africa in the 1980s–90s.
2. What happened at the 1999 WTO Summit in Seattle and why was it significant?
The 1999 WTO Ministerial Conference in Seattle became the defining moment of the anti-globalization movement. Tens of thousands of protesters — including labor unions, environmental groups, human rights activists, and anarchists — converged on Seattle to oppose the WTO's agenda. The protests disrupted the conference sufficiently that negotiations collapsed. The "Battle of Seattle" was significant for several reasons: (1) It united an unlikely coalition of groups — labor unions (worried about job offshoring), environmentalists (worried about WTO rules overriding environmental regulations), and developing-nation advocates (worried about unfair trade rules) — who rarely agreed on much. (2) It demonstrated that globalization faced organized, sustained popular opposition, not just elite-level disagreement. (3) It inaugurated a decade of protests at G8 summits, IMF meetings, and WTO conferences worldwide. (4) It forced global trade institutions to acknowledge that their policies had legitimacy problems and needed to address concerns about inequality and democratic accountability.
3. How does China's Weibo represent resistance to cultural/digital globalization?
Weibo (Chinese: "microblog") is a social media platform launched in 2009 by Sina Corporation, often described as China's equivalent of Twitter. Its existence represents a complex form of resistance to Western digital globalization: (1) China blocked Facebook and Twitter (and other Google, Meta, and Alphabet products) through the "Great Firewall" — partly for censorship/political control, but also reflecting China's broader assertion of digital sovereignty. (2) The development of domestic alternatives (Weibo for Twitter, WeChat for WhatsApp/Facebook, Baidu for Google, Alibaba for Amazon) demonstrated that China could develop its own technology ecosystem rather than depending on Western platforms. (3) This created a massive alternative digital cultural space — serving over 1 billion users — that operated outside Western-dominated platforms. Whether this is best understood as resistance to cultural globalization or as state censorship masquerading as cultural assertion is genuinely contested — both things are true simultaneously. The CED lists it as an example of "advent of locally developed social media" as a response to economic globalization.

Key Vocabulary
Anti-Globalization Movement
Definition: A broad coalition of activists opposing the policies and institutions of economic globalization, particularly the IMF, World Bank, and WTO. Significance: Peaked in the late 1990s–early 2000s with major protests at international summits; represented organized popular resistance to the free-market global order.
IMF (International Monetary Fund)
Definition: International organization providing loans to countries facing financial crises, typically requiring free-market "structural adjustment" reforms as conditions. Significance: CED illustrative example of resistance target; critics argued IMF conditions harmed poor populations and served wealthy-nation interests.
World Bank
Definition: International institution providing development loans to poorer nations, requiring market-oriented policy reforms. Significance: CED illustrative example of resistance target; like the IMF, was a major target of anti-globalization protests for imposing conditions critics viewed as serving corporate and wealthy-nation interests.
Structural Adjustment
Definition: IMF/World Bank-required policies (cutting government spending, privatization, trade liberalization) imposed on developing nations in exchange for loans. Significance: A major cause of anti-globalization protest; critics argued these policies harmed the poor while benefiting foreign investors and corporations.
Weibo
Definition: A Chinese social media platform, similar to Twitter, developed domestically as Western platforms were blocked in China. Significance: CED illustrative example of "locally developed social media"; represents China's assertion of digital sovereignty and a form of resistance to Western-dominated digital globalization.
Cultural Sovereignty
Definition: A nation's or community's ability to maintain and develop its own cultural identity independent of external cultural influences. Significance: A driving concern behind many forms of resistance to cultural globalization — the fear that local cultures would be overwhelmed by more powerful (often American/Western) cultural products.

🌐 Why Did Globalization Generate Resistance? (KC-6.3.IV.iv)

The AP CED presents globalization as a contested process: "Responses to rising cultural and economic globalization took a variety of forms." To understand these responses, it helps to understand what people were resisting — and why the gains of globalization did not translate into universal acceptance.

Economic globalization produced real costs alongside its real benefits. In wealthy nations, manufacturing workers lost jobs as production moved to lower-wage countries. Communities built around steel mills, textile factories, and automobile plants were devastated. In developing nations, IMF and World Bank-required "structural adjustment" policies — cutting social services, privatizing public utilities, opening markets to foreign competition — often harmed the poor while benefiting foreign investors. Small farmers couldn't compete with subsidized agricultural imports from wealthy nations. Local industries were undercut by cheaper foreign goods flooding newly opened markets.

Cultural globalization also generated anxiety and resistance. The dominance of American films, music, and consumer brands felt to many communities like a threat to local traditions, languages, and identities. Religious communities across traditions worried that Western secular consumer values were eroding moral frameworks. Nationalists resisted the apparent erosion of sovereignty as international institutions (WTO, IMF) increasingly constrained what national governments could do. All of these grievances, sometimes in combination, produced the varied forms of resistance the CED describes.

💡 Fun Fact
The 1999 Seattle WTO protests became famous partly because of an unlikely coalition: Teamsters and Turtles — labor union members (Teamsters) and environmental activists (symbolized by people in sea turtle costumes protesting WTO rules that weakened environmental protections) marching together. The unexpected alliance demonstrated the breadth of anti-globalization sentiment: people from very different backgrounds — workers worried about jobs, environmentalists worried about regulations, human rights advocates worried about developing-nation exploitation — could unite against the same institutions. The slogan "No justice, no peace, no WTO" captured the movement's energy.

🏦 Anti-IMF and Anti-World Bank Activism (KC-6.3.IV.iv)

The most prominent form of economic globalization resistance was organized activism against the international financial institutions — particularly the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank — that the CED explicitly names as illustrative examples.

Both institutions were created at the 1944 Bretton Woods Conference to stabilize the global economy after WWII. The IMF was designed to provide short-term loans to countries facing balance-of-payments crises; the World Bank to provide longer-term development finance. Over time, both became major vectors of economic globalization by requiring free-market "structural adjustment" policies as conditions for their loans. Nations that needed IMF assistance had to agree to: cut government spending (reducing subsidies on food, fuel, and healthcare; slashing public sector employment); privatize state-owned enterprises; eliminate trade barriers; and deregulate financial markets. The theory was that these market reforms would promote growth. Critics argued the actual effects were consistent: cuts to social programs harmed the poor; privatization of utilities raised prices for basic services; trade liberalization exposed domestic industries to competition they couldn't survive; and the resulting economic disruption caused unemployment and social unrest.

Anti-IMF activism escalated dramatically in the 1990s. Protests accompanied virtually every major IMF, World Bank, and WTO summit. The 1999 WTO Ministerial Conference in Seattle ("the Battle of Seattle") drew tens of thousands of protesters and effectively shut down the negotiations. The 2001 G8 Summit in Genoa, Italy saw hundreds of thousands of protesters and violent confrontations that left one protester dead. Organizations like Jubilee 2000 campaigned successfully for debt cancellation for the world's poorest nations, arguing that IMF-imposed structural adjustment conditions on indebted nations perpetuated poverty rather than alleviating it.

The intellectual framework of anti-IMF activism drew on economists like Joseph Stiglitz — himself a former World Bank chief economist — who wrote Globalization and Its Discontents (2002), arguing from inside the system that IMF policies were economically flawed and often worsened the crises they were supposed to fix. The East Asian financial crisis of 1997–98, where IMF austerity measures imposed on Thailand, Indonesia, and South Korea were widely seen as deepening and extending economic pain, was a major catalyst for this critique.

💻 Locally Developed Social Media: Digital Resistance (KC-6.3.IV.iv)

The CED identifies a distinctive form of cultural resistance to globalization: the "advent of locally developed social media", using Weibo in China as the illustrative example. This development represents the intersection of technological nationalism, state censorship, and cultural assertion.

When Western social media platforms — Facebook, Twitter, YouTube, Google — became global phenomena in the 2000s, China blocked them all through the "Great Firewall" — China's sophisticated internet censorship and filtering system. In their place, Chinese companies developed domestic equivalents: Weibo (a microblogging platform similar to Twitter), WeChat (messaging and social network similar to WhatsApp and Facebook combined), Baidu (search engine comparable to Google), and Alibaba/Taobao (e-commerce comparable to Amazon). By 2020, Weibo had over 500 million registered users and WeChat had 1.2 billion — creating a massive alternative digital ecosystem that operated entirely outside Western-dominated platforms.

The Chinese case was complex: it was simultaneously state censorship (the government could monitor and censor content on domestic platforms more easily than on foreign ones) and technological self-sufficiency (China developed world-class technology without dependence on Western corporations). Whether to interpret it primarily as political repression or as cultural/technological resistance to Western digital dominance is genuinely contested — both are true. But in the CED's framework, it illustrates that even in the digital sphere, globalization was not total: nations could and did develop alternatives to Western-dominated platforms.

Other regions also saw the development of locally rooted social media alternatives: VK (Russia's social network), various platforms in South Korea, and regional platforms across the Global South. The rise of TikTok — owned by Chinese company ByteDance but achieving genuine global popularity — further complicated the narrative, as a Chinese-origin platform became a global cultural force, reversing the typical direction of digital cultural flow.

💡 Fun Fact
When China's "Great Firewall" blocked Google in 2010, Baidu — China's domestic search engine — became the world's second most visited website after Google itself, serving over 1 billion Chinese internet users. This made Baidu one of the world's most used websites that most non-Chinese people had never heard of. China's internet — home to the world's largest online population — effectively became a separate internet: similar in technology, completely different in content and governance. This "splinternet" phenomenon represents one of globalization's most significant limits.

🌍 A Spectrum of Responses

The CED notes that responses to globalization "took a variety of forms" — and this variety is genuinely broad. Beyond the two specific examples (anti-IMF activism and locally developed social media), resistance to globalization took many additional forms:

  • Economic protectionism — many nations and political movements pushed for tariffs, subsidies, and trade barriers to protect domestic industries. Even enthusiastic free-trade nations maintained protections in strategically important sectors.
  • Cultural protectionism — France famously required a minimum percentage of French-language content on television and radio to protect French culture from English-language dominance; many nations restricted foreign ownership of media; some governments supported indigenous-language broadcasting.
  • Religious revivalism — from Islamic fundamentalism to Hindu nationalism to Christian evangelicalism, religious movements worldwide partly represented reactions against the secular consumer values spread by cultural globalization.
  • Nationalist populism — political movements from the 1990s onward exploited resentment of globalization's costs (job losses, cultural change, perceived loss of sovereignty) to build electoral coalitions opposing international integration.
  • Alternative globalization — some activists didn't want to stop globalization but to make it fairer — through fair trade, debt cancellation, stronger labor and environmental standards in trade agreements, and greater developing-nation representation in international institutions.

1. Why did the IMF and World Bank become major targets of anti-globalization activism in the late 20th century?

The IMF and World Bank became targets because they were the most visible enforcers of the free-market global economic order. When countries faced financial crises and needed loans, these institutions attached conditions requiring fundamental restructuring of economic policy — cutting social programs, privatizing public services, opening markets to foreign competition. Critics, including former World Bank chief economist Joseph Stiglitz, argued that these policies consistently harmed the poor: cutting food and fuel subsidies raised prices for the most vulnerable; privatizing utilities made basic services unaffordable; opening markets destroyed domestic industries. Countries that implemented IMF programs often experienced severe economic pain — the East Asian crisis of 1997–98, where IMF austerity worsened recessions in Thailand, Indonesia, and South Korea, was particularly damaging to the IMF's credibility. Anti-globalization activists saw these institutions as imposing wealthy-nation interests on poor nations under the guise of economic expertise, making them powerful symbols of unjust global power structures.

2. How does China's development of Weibo represent a form of resistance to globalization? What are the limitations of this interpretation?

China's development of Weibo and other domestic platforms represents resistance in several senses: it asserted technological and cultural sovereignty by creating a domestic alternative to Western-dominated platforms; it prevented Western corporations from dominating China's information infrastructure; and it created a large digital ecosystem — serving over a billion people — that operated entirely outside Western control. From this perspective, China built a genuinely alternative digital world rather than being absorbed into Western digital globalization. However, the interpretation has real limitations: the primary motivation for China's Great Firewall was political control — the CCP could more easily monitor and censor content on domestic platforms than on foreign ones. This was censorship enabling resistance to government critics, not primarily cultural resistance to Western influence. Additionally, China's digital platforms adopted the same fundamental business model (advertising-driven, data-harvesting) as Western ones, just censored and state-aligned. The "resistance" framing obscures that China was both limiting Western influence AND tightening authoritarian control simultaneously.

3. How were the various responses to globalization (anti-IMF activism, locally developed social media, cultural protectionism, religious revivalism) connected? What did they share?

Despite their surface diversity — street protests, technology policy, broadcast regulations, religious movements — these responses shared several underlying concerns. All represented assertions of some form of sovereignty (economic, cultural, digital, moral) against perceived erosion by forces from outside. All reflected the experience of globalization as a loss — of jobs, of cultural distinctiveness, of political autonomy, of traditional values — rather than as a gain. All challenged the narrative that globalization was a neutral, technically driven process equally beneficial for all participants, rather than a political project that served some interests more than others. And all revealed that globalization generated real losers who had real reasons to resist it. What varied was the form of resistance — peaceful advocacy vs. protest vs. state policy vs. religious community-building — and the specific target of resistance, reflecting the different dimensions (economic, cultural, digital, moral) of globalization that different groups experienced as threatening.

4. To what extent was anti-globalization resistance successful? Did it change or limit globalization?

The record is mixed. Anti-IMF activism achieved some significant results: the Jubilee 2000 debt cancellation campaign secured substantial debt relief for the world's poorest nations; growing criticism of structural adjustment forced the IMF to modify some of its most rigid prescriptions; and the political credibility damage from the Seattle protests contributed to the eventual failure of the Doha Round of WTO trade negotiations (2001–2016). However, economic globalization continued — global trade kept growing, MNCs expanded, and free-market policies remained dominant. The 2008 financial crisis temporarily strengthened critics' arguments, but recovery was led by the same institutions and policies they had criticized. China's Great Firewall successfully maintained digital sovereignty, but did not prevent China from deeply integrating into global economic networks. The most significant long-term effect may have been the rise of nationalist populism — particularly in wealthy nations — that eventually disrupted key pillars of the free-trade order through protectionist policies, Brexit, and NAFTA renegotiation. But this populist backlash raised as many new problems as it solved.

✍️ Short Answer Questions

Use the following claim and your knowledge of world history to answer parts A, B, and C.

"Resistance to globalization in the late 20th century demonstrated that the process was contested and generated real losers, not merely inevitable progress."

A. Provide ONE piece of evidence from the period 1900 to the present that supports this claim.

B. Provide ONE piece of evidence that complicates this claim by showing globalization had genuine benefits that resistance sometimes overlooked.

C. Explain how anti-IMF/World Bank activism represented a challenge to the dominant free-market economic order of the late 20th century.

Sample Answer:
A. The 1999 "Battle of Seattle" — mass protests that disrupted the WTO Ministerial Conference — demonstrated that globalization was contested and had generated real losers. The diverse coalition that converged on Seattle (labor unions, environmentalists, developing-nation advocates) represented real grievances: manufacturing workers who had lost jobs to offshoring, communities whose environmental regulations had been challenged as WTO violations, and developing nations whose agricultural markets had been devastated by wealthy-nation subsidized exports. The fact that the protests effectively shut down the WTO negotiations showed that globalization faced organized, sustained, and politically significant opposition, not mere inevitable progress that all rational people would accept.

B. China's integration into global trade networks from the 1980s onward produced genuine, dramatic benefits that complicate simple anti-globalization narratives. An estimated 800 million Chinese people were lifted out of extreme poverty in four decades of economic opening — one of history's most dramatic reductions in human suffering. Workers in Chinese factories may have earned low wages by Western standards, but those wages were far above what subsistence agriculture had offered. The benefits of globalization were real for hundreds of millions of people in the developing world, even when the costs were real for others. This suggests that resistance to globalization needed to address how to make it fairer, not necessarily how to stop it.

C. Anti-IMF and World Bank activism challenged the dominant free-market order by questioning whether these institutions' policies actually served their stated goals of development and poverty reduction. Activists, supported by economists like Joseph Stiglitz, argued that structural adjustment programs — while presented as neutral economic expertise — systematically benefited wealthy nations and international corporations at the expense of developing-nation populations. This critique challenged the ideological framework that presented free-market reforms as universally beneficial technocratic policy rather than as political choices that served specific interests. By demanding debt cancellation (achieved through Jubilee 2000), policy reform, and greater democratic accountability for these institutions, activists challenged the assumption that expert-driven financial institutions operating outside democratic accountability were legitimate authorities over national economic policy.
📄 Long Essay Question

Evaluate the extent to which resistance to globalization changed or limited globalization in the period 1900 to the present.

Model Thesis: While resistance to globalization — including anti-IMF activism, cultural protectionism, and the development of alternative digital platforms — achieved some specific reforms and demonstrated the contested nature of the globalizing project, it largely failed to fundamentally alter or reverse the broader trajectory of economic and cultural globalization, which continued to integrate world markets, spread consumer culture, and constrain national sovereignty throughout the late 20th and early 21st centuries.
