2.3 Market Equilibrium and Price

Equilibrium is the price where the amount buyers want to buy exactly matches the amount sellers want to sell. When demand or supply shifts — because of a new trend, a new technology, a change in income, or a change in costs — the equilibrium price and quantity move to a new point. Learning Objectives
  • Explain how equilibrium price and quantity are determined in a competitive market.
  • Predict how a shift in demand or supply changes equilibrium price and quantity.
  • Distinguish between a shift in a curve and a movement along a curve.
The Reading

Imagine a food truck selling tacos outside your school. If the truck charges $20 a taco, almost nobody buys one — there's a huge surplus of unsold tacos sitting under the heat lamp. If the truck charges $1 a taco, the line wraps around the block and they sell out in ten minutes — a shortage, with plenty of hungry customers left unsatisfied. Somewhere between those two extremes is a price where the number of tacos the truck wants to sell matches the number of tacos people want to buy, with no long line and nothing left over. Economists call that point equilibrium.

Equilibrium is where the demand curve (which slopes downward — people buy more when price is lower) and the supply curve (which slopes upward — sellers offer more when price is higher) cross on a graph. At any price above equilibrium, sellers want to sell more than buyers want to buy, creating a surplus, which pushes prices back down as sellers compete for customers. At any price below equilibrium, buyers want more than sellers are offering, creating a shortage, which pushes prices back up as buyers compete for the limited supply. Left alone, a competitive market tends to settle at this equilibrium point almost automatically — no one has to plan it.

But equilibrium isn't permanent. It only holds as long as the underlying conditions behind supply and demand stay the same. If something changes buyers' willingness to purchase at every price — a new health trend, a change in income, a popular food-influencer video — the entire demand curve shifts, and a new equilibrium forms. Likewise, if something changes sellers' costs or willingness to sell at every price — a spike in the cost of tortillas, a new competitor food truck, a new food-safety regulation — the entire supply curve shifts. It's worth being precise about the difference between a shift and a movement: a change in the taco's own price moves you along the existing curves, but a change in anything else (income, tastes, input costs, number of sellers, expectations about the future) shifts the curves themselves to a new position, landing on a new equilibrium price and quantity.

This single idea — equilibrium responding to shifts in supply and demand — explains an enormous amount of everyday economic life: why gas prices jump after a hurricane disrupts oil refineries (a supply decrease), why concert-ticket prices spike right before a sold-out show (a demand increase relative to a fixed supply), and why the price of a hot new video-game console often falls a year after release once more factories are producing it (a supply increase). Key Vocabulary (tap a term)
  • The price at which the quantity buyers want to purchase equals the quantity sellers want to sell — the price where the market "clears."
  • The amount bought and sold at the equilibrium price.
  • A situation where quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied at the current price — usually because the price is set below equilibrium.
  • A situation where quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded at the current price — usually because the price is set above equilibrium.
  • A "shift" moves an entire supply or demand curve (caused by something other than the good's own price, like income or costs). A "movement" is a change in quantity along the same curve, caused only by a change in the good's own price.
The word "equilibrium" comes from Latin for "equal weight" — the same root used to describe a balanced scale, which is a pretty good visual for what's happening at the market-clearing price.

Discussion Questions
Model answer: Demand increases (the whole demand curve shifts right) because more people want to buy the item at every price. With supply unchanged in the short run, the new intersection point sits at both a higher equilibrium price and a higher equilibrium quantity than before.
Check Your Understanding
At the equilibrium price, which of the following is true?
  • Quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded
  • Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied
  • Quantity supplied equals quantity demanded
  • Price is always at its lowest possible level
Equilibrium is defined as the price at which the quantity sellers want to supply exactly equals the quantity buyers want to purchase — the market "clears" with no shortage or surplus.
If the price of a good is set above its equilibrium price, the result is most likely a
  • shortage
  • surplus
  • shift in demand
  • shift in supply
A price above equilibrium means sellers want to sell more than buyers want to buy at that price, creating a surplus of unsold goods.
An increase in the cost of raw materials used to make a good will most directly cause
  • a rightward shift of the demand curve
  • a rightward shift of the supply curve
  • a leftward shift of the supply curve
  • a movement along the demand curve only
Higher input costs make sellers willing to supply less at every price, shifting the entire supply curve to the left (a decrease in supply).
Which of the following would cause a movement along the demand curve rather than a shift of the entire curve?
  • A rise in consumer income
  • A change in the good's own price
  • A change in consumer tastes
  • A change in the price of a substitute good
Only a change in the good's own price causes a movement along an existing demand curve. Income, tastes, and the price of related goods all shift the entire curve to a new position.
If both demand and supply increase (shift right) at the same time, what happens to equilibrium quantity and price?
  • Quantity definitely rises; price change is ambiguous without more information
  • Price definitely rises; quantity change is ambiguous without more information
  • Both quantity and price definitely fall
  • Neither changes
When both curves shift right, equilibrium quantity unambiguously increases, but the effect on price depends on the relative size of the two shifts — it could rise, fall, or stay about the same.
Free-Response Practice
A new highway makes it much cheaper and faster to ship strawberries from farms to your local grocery store. Using a supply-and-demand model, explain what happens to the equilibrium price and quantity of strawberries at that store, and explain your reasoning.
Cheaper, faster shipping lowers the store's cost of getting strawberries to shelves, which increases supply — the supply curve shifts to the right, since sellers are now willing to offer more strawberries at every price. With demand unchanged, the new equilibrium sits at a lower price and a higher quantity: shoppers pay less for strawberries and more strawberries are sold than before the highway was built.
