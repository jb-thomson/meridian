9.3 Technological Advances: Debates About the Environment After 1900

🌍 Humans & Environment🌿 Deforestation🌡️ Climate Change💧 Resource Competition
  • LO-A: Human activity degraded the environment at scale: Industrial growth, agricultural expansion, urbanization, and population growth led to deforestation, desertification, declining air quality, and freshwater depletion on a global scale. Competition over scarce resources intensified between nations and communities. Greenhouse gases fueled climate debates: The burning of fossil fuels released carbon dioxide and other gases that trapped heat in the atmosphere. Scientific consensus emerged that this was causing global warming and climate change. But political and economic interests made international action difficult, the "debates about the nature and causes of climate change" were as much political as scientific. Environmental damage was global but unequal: The richest nations produced the most pollution and consumed the most resources, but the worst impacts often fell on the poorest communities and nations, island nations threatened by rising seas, sub-Saharan Africa threatened by desertification, poor urban communities breathing polluted air. International responses emerged but were limited: Treaties like the Kyoto Protocol (1997) and the Paris Agreement (2015) attempted to coordinate global responses to climate change, but were weakened by non-participation by major emitters and the difficulty of balancing economic development with environmental protection.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. What is desertification and why did it accelerate in the 20th century?
Desertification is the process by which fertile land becomes desert, typically through a combination of overgrazing, deforestation, poor agricultural practices, and drought. It accelerated in the 20th century for several reasons: (1) Population growth pushed agriculture into marginal lands that couldn't sustain it. (2) Commercial farming removed ground cover that held soil together. (3) Overgrazing by increasing livestock populations stripped vegetation. (4) Deforestation removed trees whose roots held moisture and soil. (5) Climate change reduced rainfall in already-dry regions. The Sahel region of Africa (south of the Sahara) experienced severe desertification, threatening food security for millions. The Dust Bowl in the American Great Plains in the 1930s was a smaller-scale version, created partly by poor agricultural practices, that displaced 3.5 million people.
2. What is the greenhouse effect and how do human activities intensify it?
The greenhouse effect is a natural process: certain gases in Earth's atmosphere (CO2, methane, water vapor) trap heat from the sun, warming the planet. Without any greenhouse effect, Earth would be about 33°C colder and largely uninhabitable. The problem is human intensification of this natural process. Burning fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) for energy releases enormous amounts of CO2 that had been stored underground for millions of years. Deforestation removes trees that absorb CO2. Industrial agriculture releases methane (from livestock and rice paddies) and nitrous oxide (from fertilizers). Together, these activities have raised atmospheric CO2 from about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 ppm today, the highest in at least 800,000 years. The result is enhanced greenhouse warming, climate change.
3. Why was international cooperation on climate change so difficult to achieve?
Several factors made climate cooperation difficult: (1) Economic interests, fossil fuel industries and the industries dependent on cheap energy resisted regulation. (2) National sovereignty, nations were reluctant to limit their own economic activities based on an international agreement. (3) Equity disputes, developing nations argued they should not be restricted from using fossil fuels the way wealthy nations had built their economies on, while wealthy nations didn't want to bear costs alone. (4) Free-rider problems, any nation that reduced emissions benefited from global action whether or not it contributed, creating incentives to let others bear the cost. (5) Political short-termism, climate change operates over decades and centuries, but politicians face re-election every few years, making it hard to impose near-term costs for long-term benefits. The US refused to ratify the Kyoto Protocol; China and India were exempt from its major restrictions.

The Reading

🌲 Deforestation and Desertification: Land Under Pressure (KC-6.1.II.A)

The 20th century brought unprecedented pressure on the world's land resources. The AP CED identifies a key development: "As human activity contributed to deforestation, desertification, a decline in air quality, and increased consumption of the world's supply of fresh water, humans competed over these and other resources more intensely than ever before."

Deforestation accelerated dramatically in the 20th century, driven by agricultural expansion, commercial logging, and urbanization. The Amazon rainforest, the world's largest tropical forest, lost nearly 20% of its total area between 1970 and 2020, cleared primarily for cattle ranching and soybean cultivation. Southeast Asian forests were logged for timber and palm oil. African forests were cleared for small-scale farming by growing populations. The consequences were profound: deforestation destroyed biodiversity (tropical forests contain roughly half of all species on Earth), accelerated soil erosion (trees hold soil together), disrupted water cycles (forests generate rainfall through transpiration), and released vast stores of carbon, accelerating climate change.

Desertification converted an estimated 12 million hectares of productive land to desert each year by the late 20th century. In Africa's Sahel, the semi-arid zone south of the Sahara stretching across a dozen countries, desertification caused recurring famine and displaced millions of people. Overgrazing by growing livestock populations stripped vegetation; deforestation removed trees whose roots held moisture; declining rainfall (partly caused by deforestation itself) killed remaining plants. The Sahel famines of the 1970s and 1980s, which killed hundreds of thousands, were partly consequences of desertification combined with drought.

💡 Fun Fact
The Aral Sea, once the fourth-largest lake in the world, in Central Asia, virtually disappeared in the late 20th century due to Soviet-era irrigation projects that diverted its feeder rivers to grow cotton in the desert. By 2007, it had shrunk to 10% of its original size. Fishing boats sit abandoned in the middle of what is now desert. The Aral Sea disaster is one of history's worst environmental catastrophes, a graphic illustration of how intensive human water use can destroy ecosystems that took thousands of years to form.

💧 Freshwater: The Contested Resource (KC-6.1.II.A)

Fresh water, essential for drinking, agriculture, and industry, became an increasingly scarce and contested resource as the 20th century progressed. Although water covers 71% of Earth's surface, only about 2.5% is fresh water, and most of that is locked in glaciers and ice caps. Less than 1% of Earth's water is accessible for human use. As the global population grew from 1.6 billion in 1900 to over 8 billion by 2024, and as agricultural, industrial, and urban water demand increased even faster, competition over freshwater intensified dramatically.

Underground aquifers, vast stores of groundwater accumulated over thousands of years, were depleted at rates far exceeding natural recharge. The Ogallala Aquifer in the American Great Plains, which supports much of US grain and beef production, was being depleted by irrigation many times faster than it was being replenished. In South Asia, water tables were falling beneath major agricultural regions in India and Pakistan. Glacier retreat in the Himalayas threatened the freshwater supply of hundreds of millions of people in South and Southeast Asia whose rivers were fed by glacial melt. By the 21st century, water scarcity affected over 2 billion people and was a growing source of international tension, some analysts predicted "water wars" in the Middle East and other water-stressed regions.

🌡️ Greenhouse Gases and the Climate Debate (KC-6.1.II.B)

The most politically contentious environmental issue of the late 20th and early 21st centuries was climate change. The science was clear: burning fossil fuels released carbon dioxide that had been stored underground for millions of years, rapidly increasing atmospheric CO2 concentrations and warming the planet. The AP CED describes this carefully: "The release of greenhouse gases and pollutants into the atmosphere contributed to debates about the nature and causes of climate change."

The scientific consensus was established by the late 1980s: the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), founded in 1988, synthesized thousands of studies and concluded that human greenhouse gas emissions were warming the planet, with potentially catastrophic consequences, rising sea levels, more extreme weather events, disruption of agricultural systems, and threats to biodiversity.

But the debates the CED references were real and consequential. While the scientific consensus was strong, political and economic opposition was fierce. Fossil fuel industries spent billions funding organizations that cast doubt on climate science. Governments of major emitters, the US, China, India, resisted mandatory emission reductions that they feared would slow economic growth. The Kyoto Protocol (1997), the first binding international climate agreement, was never ratified by the United States (the world's largest historical emitter) and exempted rapidly developing China and India. The Paris Agreement (2015) achieved broader participation but relied on voluntary, nationally determined targets rather than binding obligations.

💡 Fun Fact
The first scientific warning of the greenhouse effect and its potential impact on global temperatures was published in 1896 by Swedish chemist Svante Arrhenius. He calculated correctly that doubling atmospheric CO2 would raise global temperatures by 5–6°C, remarkably close to modern estimates. He thought this would take thousands of years. In fact, atmospheric CO2 has risen more than 50% since industrialization in less than 200 years. The science was right; the timeline was just much faster than anyone expected.

🌍 Environmental Inequality: Who Bears the Cost?

One of the most significant dimensions of the environmental crisis was its deeply unequal character. The nations that produced the most greenhouse gas emissions and consumed the most resources were primarily wealthy industrialized nations. But the nations and communities that suffered most severely from the consequences were often the world's poorest.

Low-lying island nations, Tuvalu, Kiribati, the Maldives, faced potential complete inundation from rising sea levels caused by climate change produced primarily by wealthy nations' emissions. Sub-Saharan Africa, which had contributed negligibly to global greenhouse gas emissions, faced severe threats from changing rainfall patterns, desertification, and agricultural disruption. Bangladesh, one of the world's most densely populated and poorest nations, faced increasing flood risk from both sea level rise and intensified monsoons. Meanwhile, wealthy nations had far more resources to build sea walls, develop drought-resistant crops, and adapt to changing conditions.

This inequity became a major source of tension in international climate negotiations. Developing nations argued that they had a right to develop their economies the way wealthy nations had, using cheap fossil fuels, and that wealthy nations should bear the costs of the climate damage their historical emissions had caused. The debates the CED references were thus not only about scientific facts but about justice, history, and the distribution of costs and benefits in a globalized world.

Use the following claim and your knowledge of world history to answer parts A, B, and C.
A. The burning of fossil fuels, coal, oil, and natural gas, to power industrial economies dramatically increased atmospheric CO2 concentrations from pre-industrial levels of ~280 parts per million to over 420 ppm by the 21st century. This rise in greenhouse gases, produced almost entirely by wealthy industrialized nations' economic activities, drove global warming. The connection between economic modernization and environmental degradation is direct: the industrial revolution that raised living standards in Europe and North America also created the greenhouse gas problem that now threatens global climate stability. B. Environmental degradation also occurred in non-industrial contexts, complicating the modernization narrative. Deforestation and desertification in sub-Saharan Africa were driven partly by population growth and subsistence farming pressure, not primarily by industrial development. Poor communities clearing forests for fuel and farmland, or overgrazing cattle as their herds grew, caused significant environmental damage through traditional, non-industrial activities. This suggests that poverty itself, not just industrial modernization, can drive environmental degradation, complicating simple narratives about who bears responsibility. C. Climate debates became a major arena of developed-developing nation tension. Developing nations, especially China, India, and African states, argued that they had a right to develop their economies using cheap fossil fuels the same way wealthy nations had for two centuries. They pointed out that wealthy nations' historical emissions had created the problem, while they had contributed minimally. They also demanded compensation to cover adaptation costs. Wealthy nations, conversely, argued that without major developing nation participation, any agreement was futile as their emissions would soon dwarf historical levels. This stalemate, rooted in historical inequity, significantly limited the effectiveness of international climate agreements throughout the late 20th and early 21st centuries.
Evaluate the extent to which human activity was responsible for environmental degradation in the period 1900 to the present.
Human activity was the primary driver of environmental degradation in the period 1900 to the present, industrial fossil fuel use, agricultural expansion, and urbanization caused deforestation, desertification, freshwater depletion, and greenhouse gas accumulation on a global scale, though the distribution of both environmental harm and responsibility was deeply unequal, with wealthy industrialized nations producing most pollution while poorer nations often suffered the worst consequences.
Model answer: The CED identifies human activity as the primary cause of deforestation, desertification, air quality decline, and freshwater depletion. These were directly connected to modernization in multiple ways: (1) Industrial growth required vast amounts of raw materials (logging) and energy (fossil fuels), directly causing deforestation and air pollution. (2) Agricultural expansion to feed a growing population, intensified by the Green Revolution's commercialization of farming, cleared forests and drained aquifers. (3) Urbanization concentrated populations and consumed land. (4) Rising living standards increased per-capita consumption of goods, energy, and water. The same forces that raised living standards, industrialization, agricultural intensification, urbanization, generated the environmental pressures the CED describes. This is a classic pattern of unintended consequences: the means of achieving human progress also threatened the natural systems on which human life depends.
Model answer: The science of greenhouse gases and climate change was well established by the 1980s–90s and represented a rare example of near-universal scientific consensus. But translating that consensus into international policy proved extraordinarily difficult. Politically, the core challenge was that reducing greenhouse gas emissions required restricting the burning of fossil fuels, the foundation of modern industrial economies. For any government, imposing such restrictions meant higher energy prices and potential economic slowdown, which translated into political costs. Economically, industries built on fossil fuels, oil companies, coal miners, car manufacturers, airlines, had enormous resources to lobby against regulation and fund doubt campaigns. Internationally, equity disputes between developed and developing nations stalled agreements. The CED's careful phrasing, "debates about the nature and causes of climate change", acknowledges that these debates were real and consequential, even if the underlying science was clear.
Model answer: Environmental degradation both reflected and reinforced global inequality in several ways. Wealthy nations produced most greenhouse gas emissions and consumed most resources, their industrialization and high-consumption lifestyles drove global environmental change. But poor nations, communities, and people often bore the worst consequences: desertification threatened food security in Africa; rising sea levels threatened island nations; air pollution concentrated in poor urban neighborhoods; freshwater scarcity hit poor agricultural communities hardest. Additionally, wealthier nations had far greater adaptive capacity, resources to build sea walls, develop drought-resistant crops, relocate vulnerable populations. This meant that the same historical processes that created wealth in some parts of the world (industrialization, fossil fuel use) created environmental burdens that fell disproportionately on the world's poorest. Environmental justice, the principle that environmental burdens and benefits should be distributed fairly, became a major social and political movement as a result.
Model answer: The Sahel illustrates how human pressures can accelerate and interact with natural environmental variation to create catastrophic outcomes. The region was naturally semi-arid and experienced periodic droughts, but 20th-century pressures dramatically worsened its vulnerability. Population growth increased pressure on land: more people needed more food, leading to expansion of farming onto marginal land and overgrazing by growing livestock herds. Deforestation removed vegetation that held soil and contributed to local rainfall through transpiration. Colonial agricultural policies had disrupted traditional drought-coping strategies. When natural droughts hit in the 1970s and 1980s, the land was far less resilient than before. The result was catastrophic famine, desertification, and displacement. The Sahel demonstrates that environmental disasters are rarely purely natural. They typically result from the interaction of natural variability with human choices that have increased vulnerability. This is directly relevant to current climate change debates, where human emissions are amplifying natural climate variability.
