7.7 Conducting World War II

💀 Total War⚙️ Technology📣 Propaganda⚖️ Democracy vs. Totalitarianism
  • LO-A: WWII was the second total war — bigger and deadlier than WWI — All the methods of WWI total war (propaganda, conscription, industrial mobilization, colonial recruitment) returned in WWII at even greater scale. The U.S. alone converted its entire auto industry to tank and plane production within months of Pearl Harbor. Women entered industrial workforce in large numbers (Rosie the Riveter). The war killed approximately 70–85 million people — more than any conflict in history. AP exam comparison: democratic vs. totalitarian mobilization — The CED's illustrative examples are: western democracies (Britain under Churchill, U.S. under Roosevelt) vs. totalitarian states (Germany under Hitler, USSR under Stalin). Key differences: democracies used propaganda and voluntary enlistment supplemented by conscription; totalitarian states used ideology (fascism/communism) plus terror to enforce participation, extending control into all aspects of daily life. New military technology: atomic bomb changes everything — WWII introduced strategic bombing of civilian cities (Blitz, Dresden, Tokyo firebombing), the atomic bomb (Hiroshima and Nagasaki, August 1945), radar, jet engines, V-2 rockets, and advances in amphibious warfare. The atomic bomb specifically represents a qualitative leap: for the first time, one weapon could destroy an entire city, making total war potentially civilization-ending. Ideology as a weapon: fascism and communism mobilize total states — Totalitarian states didn't just conscript armies — they used fascism (Germany) and communism (USSR) to mobilize all state resources, justified repression of any dissent, and erased the line between military and civilian life. Nazi Germany used antisemitic ideology to justify Holocaust; the USSR used communism to demand total sacrifice, including accepting enormous casualties (26 million Soviet dead). This topic connects to the Total War, Technology, Propaganda, and Democracy vs. Totalitarianism themes in AP World History.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. The AP CED asks you to explain "similarities and differences in how governments used a variety of methods to conduct war" in WWII. What is the most important similarity and the most important difference between democratic and totalitarian states' mobilization?
Most important similarity: both democratic and totalitarian governments used propaganda, conscription, industrial mobilization, and ideological appeals to mobilize entire societies for war. Total war required total mobilization regardless of political system — Churchill's Britain and Hitler's Germany both rationed food, directed industrial production, and organized propaganda campaigns to maintain morale. Most important difference: totalitarian states used ideology (fascism, communism) as an organizing principle that extended state power into all aspects of life and justified repression of any dissent as necessary for the war effort. Nazi Germany used the war to accelerate the Holocaust; the Soviet Union used wartime necessity to justify purges, forced labor, and the demand for suicidal military sacrifice. Democratic governments used propaganda and emergency powers but operated under constitutional constraints — Churchill could not simply execute political opponents; Roosevelt could not abolish elections (the 1944 election proceeded normally even while the war continued).
2. The atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki (August 1945) killed approximately 130,000–226,000 people and ended the Pacific War. Were they morally justified?
This is one of history's most debated ethical questions; historians and philosophers genuinely disagree. The primary justification: an invasion of the Japanese home islands was projected to cost hundreds of thousands of Allied casualties and potentially millions of Japanese casualties (military and civilian) — the atomic bombs may have prevented a more destructive war. Japan showed no signs of surrendering; the firebombing of Tokyo (March 1945) had already killed approximately 100,000 people in one night with conventional bombs. The primary critique: the bombs deliberately targeted civilian populations — Hiroshima and Nagasaki were chosen partly because they had not been heavily bombed previously, making the damage assessable. The distinction between combatants and civilians, a fundamental principle of international law, was systematically violated. Many historians also argue Japan was already near surrender due to Soviet entry into the war and could have been induced to surrender without the bombs. For AP purposes, understand that the atomic bombs represent the ultimate expression of total war logic — when the entire enemy nation is considered a legitimate military target, cities become targets — and that this raises profound moral questions that historians continue to debate.
3. Churchill mobilized Britain through speeches ("We shall fight on the beaches") while Hitler mobilized Germany through Nuremberg rallies. Both were propaganda. What specific differences in these propaganda styles reflected the different political systems?
Both Churchill and Hitler used rhetoric, symbols, and spectacle to build popular commitment to war. But the propaganda contexts were fundamentally different. Churchill's speeches operated within a democratic system where opposition was permitted — he had to actually persuade; he couldn't simply order people to agree. His rhetoric appealed to British tradition, democratic values, and voluntary sacrifice ("blood, toil, tears and sweat"). It was designed to build consent in a system where consent was genuinely required. Hitler's Nuremberg rallies operated in a system where dissent was punishable by imprisonment or death. The rallies were carefully choreographed to create the psychological experience of collective unity — torchlight processions, synchronized crowds, overwhelming scale — designed less to persuade individuals than to create an emotional experience of total belonging to the Volk (the people/nation). The distinction: Churchill's propaganda sought to build voluntary support; Hitler's propaganda sought to overwhelm individual judgment and subsume the individual into the collective, supported by terror against anyone who resisted.
4. The Soviet Union suffered approximately 26–27 million deaths in WWII — more than any other country, and more than the entire death toll of WWI. How did Stalin's communist ideology shape the USSR's conduct of the war?
Soviet communist ideology shaped WWII conduct in several ways. First, the Stalinist system demanded total submission to state directives — soldiers who retreated without orders were shot by NKVD (secret police) units positioned behind front lines ("Order 227: Not One Step Back"). This produced enormous casualties as Soviet forces made suicidal stands that military rationality would not have demanded. Second, the communist state's control of information and media enabled manipulation of casualty figures and military failures in ways that democratic states couldn't sustain — Soviet soldiers often didn't know the true state of the military situation. Third, communist ideology framed the war as a class struggle against fascism and capitalist imperialism, which provided genuine motivation for soldiers defending their country against an invader that was literally enslaving and exterminating Soviet populations. Fourth, the Soviet command economy — built by Stalin's Five Year Plans — could redirect industrial production to war with a speed and totality that market economies found difficult; the USSR relocated 1,500 factories to the Ural Mountains in 1941–42, beyond German bombing range. The ideology enabled both extraordinary sacrifice and extraordinary repression.
5. Strategic bombing of enemy cities — the Blitz, Dresden, Tokyo firebombing — deliberately targeted civilian populations. How does this practice reflect the logic of total war?
Strategic bombing of civilians follows directly from total war logic. If the enemy's entire economy is directed toward war production, and if that economy requires civilian workers, then targeting civilian industrial workers is militarily rational by total war reasoning. Destroying a munitions factory and killing the workers inside it both reduce enemy military capacity. The "area bombing" strategy (used by Britain against Germany, and the U.S. against Japan) went further: it deliberately targeted civilian residential areas to destroy morale and provoke civilian pressure on governments to surrender. This represents the complete dissolution of the civilian/combatant distinction that traditional laws of war attempted to maintain. Total war logic — if you need to mobilize your entire society, the enemy's entire society is a legitimate target — produces strategic bombing as an almost inevitable conclusion. The atomic bomb was the logical endpoint of this reasoning: one weapon to destroy one entire city.

The Reading

🌍 The Biggest War in Human History

World War II was the deadliest conflict in human history — killing an estimated 70–85 million people (about 3% of the world's 1940 population), the majority of them civilians. It was fought simultaneously on multiple continents: Europe, North Africa, East Asia, Southeast Asia, and the Pacific. It mobilized entire economies for war production, introduced weapons of unprecedented destructive power, and ended with the first and only use of nuclear weapons in warfare. Understanding how governments conducted this war requires examining both the methods they shared and the critical differences between democratic and totalitarian approaches.

📊 Total War: Similarities Across All Governments

As in WWI, WWII was a total war requiring the mobilization of entire societies. All major belligerents — democratic and totalitarian alike — used similar methods:

  • Conscription: All major powers implemented mass conscription. The U.S. — historically resistant to peacetime conscription — drafted 10 million men after Pearl Harbor.
  • Industrial mobilization: Economies were redirected to war production. The U.S. converted its entire auto industry to military production within months; the Soviet Union relocated 1,500 factories to the Ural Mountains to protect them from German bombing.
  • Women in the workforce: All major powers recruited women into industrial production. In the U.S., "Rosie the Riveter" became the symbol of women building planes and ships; in the USSR, women served in combat roles (Soviet female snipers and pilots were highly effective).
  • Rationing: All governments rationed food, fuel, and consumer goods to direct resources to war production.
  • Propaganda: All governments used media — radio, film, posters, newspapers — to maintain public support and demonize the enemy.
  • Colonial mobilization: Britain again recruited from India, Africa, and other colonies; France from West Africa; Japan conscripted Korean and Taiwanese subjects.
💡 Fun Fact
During WWII, the United States built more military equipment than all the other major powers combined. Between 1941 and 1945, the U.S. produced: 86,000 tanks, 297,000 aircraft, 8,800 naval vessels, and 2.4 million military trucks. The USSR received 400,000 of those trucks through Lend-Lease, which Soviet commanders credited as essential to their ability to pursue the retreating German army after Stalingrad — American trucks moved Soviet soldiers faster than their legs could carry them. The industrial mobilization of the U.S. economy was arguably as decisive as any battlefield victory.

🗳️ Democratic States: Churchill, Roosevelt, and Consent

The CED's illustrative examples for democratic states are Great Britain under Winston Churchill and the United States under Franklin Roosevelt. Both operated within constitutional frameworks that constrained their wartime powers, even as they dramatically expanded government authority.

Churchill became Prime Minister in May 1940, as Germany was overrunning France. His famous speeches — "We shall fight on the beaches," "Their finest hour" — used rhetoric to build popular will to continue fighting when surrender might have seemed rational. Churchill understood that Britain could not win without American support, so he cultivated Roosevelt's alliance carefully, presenting Britain as a democracy defending civilization against barbarism.

Roosevelt faced the challenge of mobilizing an isolationist America for a war that had not yet directly attacked the U.S. Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941) resolved that challenge by bringing the U.S. into the war — Roosevelt called it "a date which will live in infamy." Even in wartime, democratic constraints operated: the 1942 and 1944 elections proceeded normally; Congress continued to debate war policy; the press published critical reporting (though with some military censorship). The significant exception: the internment of 120,000 Japanese Americans in concentration camps — a violation of civil liberties that illustrates how wartime emergency powers can be abused even in democracies.

☭ Totalitarian States: Hitler, Stalin, and Total Control

The CED's illustrative examples for totalitarian states are Germany under Hitler and the USSR under Stalin. Both used their respective ideologies — fascism and communism — to mobilize all state resources for war and to repress any dissent as treason.

Hitler's Germany used Nazi ideology to motivate and justify: the war was framed as a racial struggle for German survival against Jewish Bolshevism. Propaganda Minister Joseph Goebbels controlled all media; the Gestapo (secret police) monitored for dissent; defeatism was punishable by death. The war was conducted alongside — and in some ways as part of — the Holocaust: SS units followed the Wehrmacht into the Soviet Union, murdering Jews and communist officials. The racial ideology that caused the war was implemented through genocide during the war.

Stalin's USSR faced the catastrophic opening of Operation Barbarossa (June 1941), in which Germany advanced 1,000 miles in six months and the Red Army suffered devastating losses. Stalin's response combined genuine inspiration (framing the war as the "Great Patriotic War" defending the Russian motherland) with brutal coercion: "Order 227" ("Not One Step Back") stationed NKVD units behind Soviet lines to shoot soldiers who retreated without orders. Political commissars monitored military officers for ideological loyalty. Suspected collaborators were executed or sent to the Gulag. The USSR lost approximately 26–27 million people — but ultimately provided the primary military force that defeated Germany on the Eastern Front.

💣 New Technology: The Atomic Age Begins

WWII introduced several significant military technologies, but the most consequential by far was the atomic bomb. The AP CED specifically identifies the atomic bomb and fire-bombing as new military technologies that "led to increased levels of wartime casualties."

Strategic bombing of cities had begun in WWI but reached industrial scale in WWII. The German Blitz killed 40,000–43,000 British civilians. Allied bombing of German cities (including the destruction of Dresden in February 1945, killing approximately 25,000) was even more devastating. In the Pacific, the U.S. firebombing of Tokyo (March 9–10, 1945) killed approximately 80,000–100,000 people in a single night — more immediately than either atomic bomb.

The atomic bomb — produced by the Manhattan Project — represented a qualitative leap. On August 6, 1945, the U.S. dropped "Little Boy" on Hiroshima, destroying 4–5 square miles of the city and killing an estimated 70,000–80,000 people immediately (with tens of thousands more dying later from radiation). On August 9, "Fat Man" was dropped on Nagasaki, killing 40,000–80,000 people. Japan surrendered on August 15, 1945, ending WWII. The atomic bomb had ended one war — but it began the nuclear age, in which humanity possessed the capacity to destroy civilization itself.

💡 Fun Fact
Many of the key scientists of the Manhattan Project were European Jews who had fled Nazi persecution — Albert Einstein, Leo Szilard, Edward Teller, Enrico Fermi (Italian, not Jewish, but a political refugee). Einstein and Szilard wrote the famous 1939 letter to Roosevelt warning that Germany might be developing nuclear weapons and urging the U.S. to begin its own program. There is a profound irony: Hitler's antisemitic persecution drove from Europe the scientists whose knowledge produced the weapon that ended the war against Japan. The Holocaust thus contributed — perversely — to the weapon that finished WWII.

According to the AP CED, both fascism and communism were used by totalitarian states to mobilize all their state resources for war in WWII. What does this shared function of very different ideologies suggest?
  • Both ideologies provided frameworks for demanding total submission to state authority, regardless of their different economic or political theories
  • Fascism and communism were essentially the same ideology with different names
  • Both ideologies required international cooperation to sustain military operations
  • The choice of ideology was less important than the personality of the individual leader
Fascism and communism are ideologically very different (capitalism vs. communism; nationalism vs. internationalism; racial hierarchy vs. class struggle). But both provided totalitarian states with frameworks that demanded total submission to state authority — fascism through racial/national obligation; communism through class solidarity and historical destiny. Both justified repression of dissent as necessary for the collective good. Their shared totalitarian political structure enabled similar methods of mobilization, despite different ideological content.
The atomic bombs dropped on Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945 are classified under the AP CED as "new military technology" that "led to increased levels of wartime casualties." More broadly, the atomic bomb represented a qualitative change in warfare because
  • it was the first weapon to be dropped from an airplane, combining air power with explosive technology
  • its radiation effects could spread to neutral countries, making it impossible to use without violating neutrality agreements
  • one weapon could destroy an entire city, making total war potentially civilization-ending and transforming international relations permanently
  • it eliminated the need for ground armies, since nuclear weapons could defeat any enemy without infantry combat
The atomic bomb's qualitative significance is that one weapon could destroy an entire city — a level of destructive efficiency that made total war potentially civilization-ending. All previous weapons required large numbers of units (bombs, shells, soldiers) to achieve destruction at this scale. The atomic bomb changed international relations (the Cold War nuclear deterrence structure, the Non-Proliferation Treaty) precisely because states with nuclear weapons could threaten each other's entire populations with a small number of deliveries.
Britain under Churchill and the United States under Roosevelt are compared in the AP CED as examples of democratic states mobilizing for WWII. The most important similarity in their approaches was
  • both relied primarily on voluntary military service without conscription
  • both relied on colonial subjects for the majority of their ground combat forces
  • both avoided strategic bombing of civilian populations in order to maintain moral superiority over Axis powers
  • both used propaganda, industrial mobilization, and constitutional emergency powers to sustain total war while maintaining democratic governance
Both Britain and the U.S. combined total war methods (propaganda, conscription, industrial mobilization, rationing) with constitutional governance that maintained elections, free press (with wartime restrictions), and parliamentary/congressional oversight. This is the defining characteristic of democratic total war: mobilizing all of society's resources while preserving the political framework of consent-based governance.
The Soviet Union's Operation Barbarossa defense is significant in AP World History primarily because
  • it demonstrated that communist ideology was superior to fascist ideology in motivating soldiers
  • the Eastern Front consumed Germany's military capacity and the Soviet counteroffensive was the primary military factor in Germany's defeat
  • it proved that a totalitarian command economy could produce more military equipment than a capitalist democracy
  • Soviet resistance inspired anti-colonial movements across Asia by demonstrating that European powers could be defeated
The Eastern Front was the largest and most costly theater of WWII. Germany deployed approximately 75% of its military forces against the Soviet Union at various points; the fighting consumed German military capacity at a rate no other theater matched. From 1943 onward, Soviet counteroffensives drove German forces westward; the Battle of Berlin (April–May 1945) was fought by Soviet forces. The Eastern Front, not D-Day, was the primary military cause of Germany's defeat — D-Day was important but opened a second front when Germany was already losing.
The Nazi Holocaust is classified as a "mass atrocity" in Topic 7.8, not Topic 7.7 on conducting the war. Why might it be analytically useful to consider the Holocaust as both separate from and connected to Germany's military conduct of WWII?
  • The Holocaust was conducted entirely before WWII began, making it historically separate from the war's military operations
  • The Holocaust used different government agencies than military operations, making them operationally separate even if ideologically connected
  • It is useful because the Holocaust was both an expression of Nazi war ideology and competed with military priorities for resources, revealing how total war and genocide became intertwined in Nazi Germany
  • The Holocaust was conducted in territories not included in the European theater of WWII, making its military connection indirect
The Holocaust was ideologically connected to Nazi military conduct (the war was framed as racial struggle; SS units followed the Wehrmacht into the Soviet Union specifically to murder Jews and commissars) while also consuming resources (railway capacity, personnel, administrative effort) that could have been used for military purposes. Historians have noted that Nazi Germany accelerated the Holocaust in 1942–43 even as the military situation worsened — ideological genocide took priority over military rationality. This reveals how in Nazi Germany, total war and racial genocide were not separate programs but aspects of the same ideological project.
Explain ONE similarity and ONE difference in how democratic states (Britain or the United States) and totalitarian states (Germany or the Soviet Union) mobilized their populations during World War II.
Similarity: Both democratic and totalitarian states used large-scale propaganda campaigns to build popular support for the war. Britain's posters and Churchill's speeches, the U.S.'s "Why We Fight" film series and "Rosie the Riveter" imagery, Nazi Germany's Goebbels-controlled media, and the Soviet Union's "Great Patriotic War" framing all used media to portray their cause as righteous, the enemy as barbaric, and sacrifice as necessary. This was a fundamental feature of total war regardless of political system. Difference: Totalitarian states used ideology backed by terror to enforce participation, while democratic states relied primarily on persuasion supplemented by conscription. Stalin's "Order 227" stationed NKVD units behind Soviet front lines to shoot soldiers who retreated without orders. Nazi Germany executed civilians and soldiers for "defeatism." In contrast, democratic governments operated within constitutional constraints: the U.S. held elections in 1942 and 1944; British citizens could openly criticize Churchill's military decisions in Parliament. The degree of coercion — not just the methods of persuasion — was the fundamental difference.
Evaluate the extent to which World War II was similar to World War I in its methods of mobilization and conduct, and what was fundamentally new about WWII.
While World War II continued and intensified WWI's total war framework — conscripting millions, mobilizing colonial populations, using propaganda to sustain popular support — it represented a qualitative escalation in three ways: the use of ideological systems (fascism and communism) that extended state control into all aspects of daily life; strategic bombing that systematically targeted entire civilian populations; and the atomic bomb, which introduced the capacity to destroy entire cities with one weapon and made future total war potentially civilization-ending.
Model answer: The most important difference is the role of coercion versus consent. Democratic states — Britain and the U.S. — relied primarily on propaganda, voluntary enlistment supplemented by conscription, and genuine popular support to sustain the war effort. Citizens could criticize the war, oppose policies, and organize politically (within limits set by emergency powers). The government needed to actually persuade its population because it lacked the instruments to enforce total compliance. Totalitarian states — Germany and the USSR — used ideology plus terror. Fascism and communism were not merely propaganda tools; they were enforced with secret police, purges, and execution. Germans who expressed defeatism risked death; Soviet soldiers who retreated without orders were shot by their own side. This total coercive power enabled extraordinary mobilization — the USSR's 26 million deaths represents a scale of sacrifice that no democratic government could have demanded or received voluntarily. But it also produced moral catastrophes: the Holocaust (ideological genocide pursued alongside military objectives) and Stalin's Order 227 (shooting your own retreating soldiers). The coercion-versus-consent distinction explains both totalitarian states' military effectiveness and their moral horrors.
Model answer: The Japanese American internment is a crucial complicating case that prevents the democratic/totalitarian distinction from being too clean. In a democracy, constitutional rights are supposed to protect individuals from government persecution without due process — yet 120,000 American citizens and resident aliens were imprisoned based solely on their ethnicity, without evidence of individual wrongdoing, in a decision upheld by the Supreme Court (Korematsu v. United States, 1944). This demonstrates that democracies under wartime pressure can violate their own principles, especially when the targets are a racial minority that lacks political power to defend itself. The distinction from totalitarian practice is one of degree rather than kind: the U.S. government imprisoned Japanese Americans; the Nazi government murdered Jews. Both were racially motivated violations of rights. The internment shows that democracy is not an automatic guarantee against state abuse — it requires active protection of minority rights and institutional resistance to wartime emergency power claims. (The U.S. formally apologized and paid reparations to survivors in 1988.)
Model answer: The Soviet case reveals that totalitarian mobilization can produce extreme military effectiveness at extreme human cost — but that the relationship between the two is complex. The USSR's massive casualties were partly the product of Stalin's brutal coercion (Order 227's shoot-on-retreat policy, which caused enormous losses by preventing tactical retreats) and partly the result of the catastrophic military incompetence of the war's opening (Operation Barbarossa killed or captured millions of Soviet soldiers in 1941 partly because Stalin had purged the Red Army's most experienced officers in 1937–38). So the totalitarian system first caused extraordinary casualties (through the purges that degraded military effectiveness and the terror that prevented rational military decisions) and then forced extraordinary sacrifice to recover. The military effectiveness that eventually defeated Germany — the 1943 battles of Kursk and Stalingrad, the 1944–45 advance to Berlin — came partly from the USSR's genuinely massive industrial capacity (built by the Five Year Plans), partly from the genuine patriotic defense of the Russian motherland (independent of communist ideology), and partly from Lend-Lease support. Totalitarian mobilization was both a cause of the catastrophic early losses and a factor in the ultimate recovery.
Model answer: The debate about the atomic bomb reveals the internal tensions in total war logic. Total war, by targeting entire enemy societies (strategic bombing, blockades, economic warfare), has already dissolved the civilian/combatant distinction — Hiroshima and Nagasaki were not qualitatively different from the firebombing of Tokyo (which killed as many or more people) except in the efficiency of destruction. If you accept total war logic — that the enemy's entire economic capacity and population are legitimate military targets — then the atomic bomb is just a more efficient implement of a strategy already in practice. The moral problem is that this logic, carried to its conclusion, justifies targeting any population whose government is at war. The post-WWII international law response — the Geneva Conventions, the Nuremberg Principles, the prohibition on weapons causing indiscriminate civilian casualties — represents an attempt to reconstruct the civilian/combatant distinction that total war had destroyed. The atomic bomb debate also introduces a new dimension: proportionality. Even if targeting civilians is sometimes justified, is the total destruction of a city (and the lingering radiation effects on survivors) proportionate to military necessity? The bomb lives in the contested space where military effectiveness and moral principle conflict most sharply.
Model answer: The Manhattan Project scientists' post-Hiroshima response reveals a pattern that recurs throughout the history of technology: creators cannot fully control the uses of what they create, and the recognition of responsibility often comes too late. Many scientists — including Leo Szilard, who had originally urged the bomb's development — circulated a petition opposing its use against Japan before the bombing; it was suppressed by the military. After Hiroshima, scientists like J. Robert Oppenheimer famously said "the physicists have known sin." They had created a weapon that could not be uninvented, whose use had killed 200,000 people, and whose existence would permanently change human civilization. Their response — advocacy for international nuclear control, the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists (which founded the Doomsday Clock), and arms control lobbying — reflects the recognition that scientific innovation carries political and moral responsibility that scientists cannot delegate entirely to governments. This is now a standard consideration in scientific ethics: the principle that scientists must consider the potential uses and consequences of their research, not merely its technical feasibility. The Manhattan Project is the foundational example of why scientific ethics and political responsibility cannot be separated from technical innovation.
