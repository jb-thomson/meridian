4.7 Changing Social Hierarchies from 1450 to 1750

👥 Social🏛️ Governance💰 Economics🌍 Interactions
  • LO-A: New racial hierarchies in the Americas: Spanish colonialism in the Americas created an explicit racial classification system called the casta system, which ranked people by their mix of European, indigenous, and African ancestry. At the top were peninsulares (born in Spain); then criollos/creoles (Spanish descent, born in Americas); then a complex grid of racial mixtures (mestizo, mulato, etc.) extending down to indigenous people and enslaved Africans. This was the first formal racial hierarchy in world history to classify people by ancestry. Qing Dynasty and Han Chinese: The Qing conquest of China (1644) brought Manchu rulers to power over a Han Chinese majority. The Qing imposed cultural markers to maintain Manchu identity and political dominance: Han men were required to shave the front of their heads and wear a Manchu-style queue (braid) on pain of death. The Qing simultaneously courted Han elite cooperation through the examination system while maintaining ethnic boundaries. This created a new political hierarchy with ethnic dimensions. Ottoman and Mughal accommodation of diversity: Contrasting with Spanish and Qing approaches, the Ottoman and Mughal empires developed policies of relative religious and ethnic inclusion. The Ottoman millet system allowed religious minorities (Jews, Christians) to govern their own communities under their own religious law. The Mughal Emperor Akbar (r. 1556–1605) abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims, promoted Hindu officials to high positions, and attempted to create a syncretic court culture. When Spain expelled Jews and Muslims in 1492–1502, the Ottoman Empire actively welcomed them, the Ottoman Sultan reportedly saying "you call Ferdinand a wise king, but he is impoverishing his country and enriching mine." Existing elites under pressure: It's worth noting that "the power of existing political and economic elites fluctuated" as they confronted "new challenges" from increasingly powerful monarchs. European nobles lost power to centralizing monarchs (Louis XIV removed French noble autonomy; the Fronde failed). Ottoman timars (land grants to warrior-administrators) were eroded by Ottoman sultans centralizing revenue. The growth of merchant wealth in Europe created new economic elites who competed with hereditary nobility.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. The Spanish casta system in colonial Americas was a formal racial classification system. Why would a colonial government create an explicit racial hierarchy rather than relying on the informal social hierarchies that existed in Spain?
Colonial contexts created social situations that didn't exist in Spain. In Spain, social hierarchy was primarily based on religion, noble lineage, and occupation. But in the Americas, the Spanish colonial project created a mixed-ancestry population (through both forced and voluntary unions of Spanish, indigenous, and African people) whose social position wasn't determined by any existing Spanish categories. The casta system was an administrative response: it provided a legal framework for determining who paid which taxes, who could hold which offices, and who had which legal rights in a society where ancestry was ambiguous and contested. Formal racial classification is often a product of colonial contexts specifically because colonial expansion creates mixed-ancestry populations whose status needs administrative regulation in ways that homogeneous home societies didn't require.
2. The Qing Dynasty required Han Chinese men to adopt the Manchu queue (shaved forehead + braid). Why would a conquering dynasty care about a haircut? What was the political function of this requirement?
The queue requirement was political theater that served a specific function: it forced Han Chinese to make a public, visible, daily declaration of submission to Manchu rule. You couldn't hide a queue; you couldn't wear one only when officials were watching. Every time a Han man left his house, he was visibly displaying Manchu-imposed cultural submission. This is why the punishment for refusing was death, the queue wasn't about hair, it was about compliance. When the Qing Dynasty was eventually overthrown in 1912, one of the first revolutionary acts was cutting off queues in public. Wearing a queue had meant submission; cutting it meant liberation. Symbolic cultural requirements are often more politically significant than they appear on the surface, they're mechanisms for producing and displaying political compliance.
3. When Spain expelled Jews and Muslims in 1492 (Jews) and 1502 (Muslims), the Ottoman Empire sent ships to actively bring them to Ottoman territory. The Ottoman Sultan Bayezid II reportedly said: "You call Ferdinand a wise king, but he is impoverishing his country and enriching mine." What does this comparison between Spanish and Ottoman religious policies reveal about different theories of state power?
Spain under Ferdinand and Isabella operated on a theory of religious homogeneity as the basis of political unity. Catholics believed that a kingdom divided by religion was inherently unstable and that a unified Christian kingdom was stronger than a diverse one. The Reconquista had just been completed; religious purity was politically paramount. The Ottoman Empire operated on a very different theory: diversity was an asset, not a liability. The Ottoman millet system treated religious communities as semi-autonomous self-governing units, allowing Jews, Christians, and Muslims to live under their own religious laws. This reduced the costs of governing a diverse empire and captured the economic benefits of skilled, commercially connected populations (Jewish merchants were especially valuable because of their commercial networks across multiple countries). Bayezid understood that Spain was destroying human capital by expelling skilled artisans, merchants, physicians, and scholars, while the Ottomans were gaining that same human capital. Two theories of state power; very different outcomes.
4. Mughal Emperor Akbar (r. 1556–1605) abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims, appointed Hindu officials to high positions, and patronized artists from multiple religious traditions. His successors, especially Aurangzeb (r. 1658–1707), reversed many of these policies, reinstating the jizya and destroying Hindu temples. What does this reversal reveal about the relationship between a ruler's personal choices and the political sustainability of minority accommodation policies?
Akbar's accommodation policies were effective at building a broad coalition of support across India's diverse religious communities. Rajput Hindu warriors became crucial military allies of the Mughal state precisely because Akbar respected their traditions. But these policies depended entirely on the personal commitment of the ruler; they weren't institutionalized in ways that would survive a different emperor's preferences. Aurangzeb was a devout Sunni Muslim who believed Islamic law required the jizya from non-Muslims and was deeply uncomfortable with Akbar's religious experiments. His reversal of accommodation policies immediately destabilized the Mughal coalition, the Marathas (Topic 4.6) were largely motivated by Aurangzeb's temple destruction and jizya reimposition. The lesson: accommodation policies that depend on individual rulers rather than institutional structures are inherently fragile. Sustainable minority accommodation requires legal institutionalization that survives individual rulers' preferences.
5. In colonial Spanish America, a criollo (person of Spanish descent born in the Americas) and a peninsular (person born in Spain) might be identical in race, religion, and family background, but the peninsular had higher legal status. Why would birthplace, independent of ancestry, create a hierarchical distinction?
The peninsular/criollo distinction was a deliberate colonial administrative strategy: by reserving the highest offices for those born in Spain rather than the Americas, the Crown ensured that the most powerful colonial officials were people whose primary loyalty was to Spain rather than to local colonial interests. A criollo might have deep roots in colonial society, landownership, family networks, local connections, that could create conflicting loyalties between Spain and the colony. A peninsular, by contrast, would typically serve for a fixed term and return to Spain, making their interests more reliably aligned with the Crown. The hierarchy thus served a political control function: it was designed to prevent colonial elites from becoming powerful enough to act independently of the Spanish state. The criollo resentment of this discrimination, being treated as inferior in their own homeland, was a primary driver of Latin American independence movements in the early 19th century.

The Reading

🌎 New Racial Hierarchies in Colonial Americas

The Spanish colonial project in the Americas created something new in world history: a formal, legally institutionalized hierarchy based on racial ancestry. The casta system emerged in the 16th–17th centuries as Spanish colonizers, their indigenous subjects, and enslaved Africans interacted in ways that produced a large mixed-ancestry population whose social position was legally ambiguous.

The casta system had practical legal consequences: different castas paid different taxes, could hold different offices, and had different legal rights. In theory it was based on ancestry; in practice, it was also based on wealth, cultural performance (dress, language, occupation), and social recognition. Wealthy mestizos could sometimes "pass" as criollos; poor criollos might be treated as mestizos. The system was both rigid (in its legal categories) and flexible (in its social application).

💡 Fun Fact
Colonial artists painted series of "casta paintings" (pinturas de castas) that illustrated the offspring of every possible combination of racial categories. These paintings typically showed a family, father, mother, child, labeled with their casta designation (e.g., "Español + India = Mestizo; Español + Negra = Mulato; Mestizo + India = Castizo"). Over 100 named casta categories were eventually developed. These paintings weren't just artistic exercises. They were administrative documents, literally showing colonial officials what the products of racial mixture were supposed to look like and what category they belonged to.
📝 AP Tip

When writing about 4.7, be careful to distinguish between: (1) entirely new social hierarchies created by colonialism and conquest (the Americas, Qing China); and (2) changes to existing hierarchies under pressure from new economic and political circumstances (European nobility; Ottoman timar system). These are different processes with different causes.

🐉 The Qing Dynasty: Ethnic Hierarchy in China

When the Manchu Qing Dynasty conquered China in 1644, it faced a fundamental problem: how to rule a Han Chinese population of approximately 100–150 million people with a Manchu population of only a few million. The solution involved a carefully calibrated combination of coercive cultural imposition and deliberate elite inclusion.

The most famous coercive measure was the queue order: all Han Chinese men were required to shave the front of their heads and wear their remaining hair in a Manchu-style braid (queue). The penalty for refusal was execution, "lose your hair or lose your head" as contemporaries put it. The queue requirement had a precise political function: it forced a daily, visible, public display of Manchu political submission. You couldn't be a secret resistor if everyone could see you were complying.

At the same time, the Qing carefully incorporated Han Chinese elites into the governance structure. The traditional Confucian examination system, which had produced the Han elite for centuries, was maintained. Han Chinese officials were appointed to high positions in the bureaucracy, often paired with Manchu co-administrators. The Qing emperors became sophisticated students of Chinese culture, patronizing Han art, literature, and scholarship.

The result was a hierarchy with ethnic dimensions but ruled through inclusion: Manchu political dominance was maintained through military control and the banner system (Manchu military organization), while Han elites were brought into governance through the examination system in ways that required their cooperation without granting them ultimate power.

🕌 Ottoman and Mughal Accommodation of Diversity

The Ottoman and Mughal empires ruled over profoundly diverse populations, the Ottomans over Christians, Jews, and Muslims of dozens of ethnicities; the Mughals over a Hindu majority with Muslim rulers and a diverse array of other groups. Both empires developed policies of relative accommodation rather than forced homogeneity.

The Ottoman millet system was the most institutionalized approach. Under this system, recognized religious communities (millets: Christians, Jews, and Armenian Christians as separate millets) could govern themselves under their own religious law in matters of personal status, marriage, divorce, inheritance, education. Each millet had its own courts, its own leadership structure (the Patriarch for Orthodox Christians, the Chief Rabbi for Jews), and its own communal institutions. Non-Muslims paid the jizya tax but were otherwise largely autonomous within their communities.

When Spain expelled its Jewish population in 1492 and its Muslim population in 1502, the Ottoman Empire actively recruited these refugees. Sultan Bayezid II sent ships to Spain to bring expelled Jews to the Ottoman Empire, reportedly commenting that Ferdinand was "impoverishing his country and enriching mine." The Ottoman cities of Istanbul, Salonica (Thessaloniki), and Izmir became home to large Sephardic Jewish communities that brought commercial networks, craft skills (especially in textile production), and medical knowledge to the Empire.

💡 Fun Fact
When Spain expelled its Jews in 1492, an estimated 100,000–200,000 people left, one of the largest refugee flows in medieval European history. Many went to the Ottoman Empire, where they were welcomed. The city of Salonica (modern Thessaloniki, Greece) became one of the largest Jewish cities in the world by the 16th century, with a Jewish majority. The Ladino language (Judeo-Spanish) that these Sephardic Jews brought is still spoken by small communities today. Some Ottoman Jews continued to speak a form of 15th-century Spanish for hundreds of years, preserved in their communities while Spanish itself evolved in Spain.

The College Board specifically identifies the "varying status of different classes of women within the Ottoman Empire" as an illustrative example of social hierarchy in this period. Ottoman women's lives differed enormously depending on class, religion, and family position:

  • Elite Muslim women in the imperial harem could wield significant political influence, especially during the 16th–17th century "Sultanate of Women", a period when powerful women such as Hürrem Sultan (wife of Suleiman the Magnificent) and Kösem Sultan (mother of two sultans) effectively governed the empire through their influence over their husbands or sons. These women controlled patronage networks, issued orders to governors, and shaped foreign policy.
  • Urban Muslim women of middling and merchant families had more restricted public roles but had legal rights under Islamic law: they could own property in their own names, initiate divorce under certain conditions, and bring legal cases independently. The Ottoman court records show women actively using the legal system to defend their interests.
  • Rural and peasant women, the large majority, worked alongside men in agricultural production, with fewer formal legal protections and little political visibility.
  • Non-Muslim women (Christian and Jewish) operated under their millet's own religious law (which varied by community) rather than Ottoman Islamic law, meaning their status depended on which religious community they belonged to, not on Ottoman imperial policy directly.
📝 AP Tip
The College Board names this as an illustrative example under LO M (social categories maintained or changed). If you see a stimulus about the "Sultanate of Women" or about property-owning Ottoman women, the AP is testing whether you understand that women's status in the Ottoman Empire was not uniform. It varied dramatically by class, religion, and proximity to the imperial center.

The Mughal approach to diversity reached its highest expression under Emperor Akbar (r. 1556–1605). Akbar abolished the jizya tax on non-Muslims (1564), appointed Rajput Hindu nobles to some of the highest positions in his military and civil administration, patronized Hindu and Jain scholars alongside Muslim ones, and personally explored a syncretic philosophy (Din-i-Ilahi) that drew on Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, and other traditions. His court was deliberately multi-confessional as a signal of his empire's inclusive character.

This accommodation was not unlimited altruism. It was also strategic. Mughal control of India depended on cooperation from the Rajput Hindu warrior nobility who controlled large territories in Rajasthan and elsewhere. Without Rajput military cooperation, Mughal rule was precarious. Akbar's accommodation was as much practical power politics as personal religious open-mindedness.

📉 Existing Elites Under Pressure

The College Board notes that "the power of existing political and economic elites fluctuated" in this period. This is true across multiple regions:

  • European nobility: Centralizing monarchs, Louis XIV in France, the Habsburg emperors in Austria, the Romanovs in Russia, systematically reduced noble autonomy. The French nobility lost independent military commands and governing authority; English nobles faced parliamentary constraints on their power. The Fronde's failure (Topic 4.6) was in part a story of noble power being permanently curtailed by the French monarchy.
  • Russian boyars: The boyars, Russia's hereditary landowning nobility, saw their independent power dramatically curtailed under Ivan the Terrible (r. 1547–1584) and then Peter the Great (r. 1682–1725). Ivan created the oprichnina, a state-within-a-state whose agents terrorized and executed boyars he suspected of disloyalty. Peter later required all nobles to serve the state in military or civil roles, and his Table of Ranks (1722) made advancement dependent on merit and service to the tsar rather than hereditary status, undermining the boyars' claim to power based purely on birth.
  • Ottoman timar holders: Ottoman warrior-administrators (sipahis) had traditionally held land grants (timars) that gave them local power and income in exchange for military service. As the Ottomans shifted to mercenary armies and centralized tax farming in the 17th century, the timar system eroded, undermining the traditional military-administrative elite's power base.
  • New merchant elites: The expansion of long-distance commerce, especially in Europe and China, created wealthy merchant families who acquired economic power that competed with hereditary noble prestige. In the Dutch Republic, merchant elites effectively governed; in England, merchant and gentry families increasingly penetrated Parliament. Economic success created new elites alongside (and sometimes above) traditional hereditary ones.
📝 AP Tip

Answer all three parts.

Part A

Explain how the Spanish colonial casta system created new social hierarchies in the Americas in the period 1450–1750.

The Spanish colonial casta system created a formal racial classification hierarchy that ranked the mixed-race population of colonial Spanish America by their proportion of Spanish, indigenous, and African ancestry. At the top, peninsulares (born in Spain) held the highest offices; below them criollos (Spanish ancestry born in Americas) held most large landholdings; below them mestizos (Spanish/indigenous), mulatos (Spanish/African), indigenous peoples, and enslaved Africans occupied progressively lower legal and social positions. This hierarchy assigned different tax obligations, legal rights, and access to offices based on ancestry, creating a new social structure that had no equivalent in pre-Columbian indigenous societies or in Spain itself, and which was designed to regulate the complexity of a colonial society that had produced a large mixed-ancestry population.

Part B

Compare the Ottoman and Spanish policies toward religious minorities in the period 1450–1750, explaining ONE key difference.

The Ottoman Empire accommodated religious minorities through the millet system, which allowed recognized Jewish and Christian communities to govern themselves under their own religious law in matters of personal status, while Spain pursued religious exclusion, expelling Jews in 1492, Muslims in 1502, and using the Inquisition to enforce Catholic conformity. A key difference is that the Ottoman approach treated minority religious communities as assets to be managed and utilized, while Spain treated them as threats to political and religious unity requiring elimination. When Spain expelled its Jewish population, the Ottoman Empire actively welcomed the refugees, gaining skilled merchants, physicians, and craftsmen whose commercial networks enriched Ottoman cities. Spain's loss was literally the Ottoman Empire's gain, demonstrating that accommodation was not just morally different from exclusion but economically more effective.

Part C

Explain ONE way the power of existing elites changed in the period 1450–1750.

In France, the power of the hereditary nobility declined significantly as Louis XIV systematically stripped noble families of independent governing authority through the 17th century. After the failure of the Fronde rebellions (1648–1653), Louis required leading nobles to reside at Versailles where he could monitor them, removed their independent military commands, and channeled their competition into courtly competition for royal favor rather than regional political autonomy. This transformed the French nobility from an independently powerful class capable of challenging royal authority (as they had during the Fronde) into a gilded aristocracy whose status depended on royal patronage rather than independent power, illustrating how existing elite power fluctuated as they confronted increasingly powerful centralizing monarchs.
"Evaluate the extent to which economic and political change transformed social hierarchies in the period 1450–1750."

Consider both the creation of new hierarchies (casta system, Qing ethnic hierarchy) and changes to existing ones (European nobility; Ottoman elites). Consider what continuities persisted as well.

"Economic and political change transformed social hierarchies to a significant extent in the period 1450–1750, creating entirely new racial hierarchies in the colonial Americas and ethnic hierarchies in Qing China while reshaping existing hierarchies in Europe and the Ottoman Empire, though the fundamental principle that social hierarchy was linked to political power and economic resources persisted as a continuity across all regions."

  • Body 1, Creation of new hierarchies: Spanish casta system (new racial classification); Qing ethnic hierarchy over Han (new ethnic political dominance); both products of conquest and colonial expansion creating social complexity that required new administrative frameworks.
  • Body 2, Transformation of existing hierarchies: European nobility losing autonomy to centralizing monarchs (Fronde, Louis XIV); new merchant elites emerging in Dutch Republic and England; Ottoman timar system eroding. Plus continuity: basic principle of hierarchical social organization linked to power and wealth remained consistent across all regions.
Model answer: Colonial contexts create social ambiguities that don't exist in homogeneous home societies. In Spain, social position was determined primarily by religion, noble lineage, and occupation, categories that were clear and legally manageable. But Spanish colonialism created a large mixed-ancestry population (mestizos, mulatos) whose position in society wasn't covered by existing Spanish legal categories. The casta system was an administrative solution: it extended legal clarity into ambiguous territory by formally ranking every combination of European, indigenous, and African ancestry and assigning each category specific rights, obligations, and restrictions. Without this formal system, colonial administrators would face constant disputes about who owed what taxes, who could hold which offices, and who had which legal protections. The casta system was therefore not primarily a product of Spanish racism (though it reflected and reinforced racism) but an administrative response to the legal complexity of colonial society, racial classification as governance technology.
Model answer: Spain pursued religious homogeneity, expelling Jews (1492) and Muslims (1502), establishing the Inquisition to police religious conformity, and requiring religious purity (limpieza de sangre) as a qualification for certain offices. The Ottoman Empire pursued accommodation, the millet system allowing Jewish and Christian communities self-governance, actively recruiting Jewish refugees expelled from Spain, and generally tolerating non-Muslim worship in exchange for the jizya. The difference is explained by context and political calculation. Spain had just completed the Reconquista, 700 years of war against Islamic rule, and framed religious unity as the foundation of political unity in a kingdom newly consolidated from multiple smaller Christian kingdoms. The Ottoman Empire governed a diverse multi-ethnic, multi-religious empire in which the majority population in many territories was Christian or Jewish; forced conversion or expulsion would have meant governing a permanently hostile majority, which was politically impractical. Accommodation served Ottoman state interests; exclusion served Spanish ones (or so Spanish rulers believed, despite the economic costs Sultan Bayezid pointed out).
Model answer: The Qing approach reveals a sophisticated understanding of the problem of minority rule over a majority population. Pure coercion alone, trying to suppress Han culture entirely, would have required constant military force against a vastly larger population and would have made governance of China's massive, complex bureaucracy impossible (the Manchus lacked enough educated administrators to replace the Han examination-educated bureaucracy). Pure inclusion alone, abandoning Manchu cultural distinctiveness, would have risked the Manchu ruling class being absorbed into Han culture and losing its distinct identity and political cohesion. The queue requirement and other cultural markers maintained Manchu identity and visible political dominance while the examination system and appointment of Han officials to high positions co-opted the Han elite whose cooperation was essential for governance. The combination was the key: visible markers of political submission (queue) + meaningful pathways to participation (examination system) = a manageable partnership between conquerors and conquered that lasted over 260 years.
Model answer: The peninsular/criollo distinction reveals that colonial social hierarchies served multiple functions simultaneously: they were not just about race (biology) but also about political loyalty and administrative control. By reserving the highest offices for peninsulares, the Spanish Crown created an administrative mechanism for ensuring that the most powerful people in the colonies were accountable to Spain rather than to local interests. A criollo landowner might be racially identical to a peninsular official but had deep local roots, family connections, landholdings, business relationships, that could create conflicting loyalties. A peninsular served for a fixed term and went home, keeping his loyalties aligned with Madrid. The hierarchy thus had racial, geographic, and political dimensions simultaneously. This multi-dimensionality is why criollo resentment of their subordinate status, despite racial identity with their rulers, was so politically explosive: they were being ranked below their racial equals for purely political reasons, which they experienced as an insult to their status and a denial of their legitimate claims to govern their own communities.
Model answer: The Akbar/Aurangzeb contrast reveals a fundamental challenge in imperial governance: policies that depend on individual rulers' personal commitments are institutionally fragile. Akbar's religious accommodation worked because it was backed by his personal authority and strategic vision. But he didn't institutionalize it in ways that would bind his successors. He couldn't legally require future emperors to maintain the jizya abolition or Hindu appointments. Aurangzeb, who had very different personal religious commitments, was free to reverse these policies immediately upon taking power. The consequences were severe: the Rajput military alliances that Akbar had built through accommodation frayed; the Marathas (motivated by Aurangzeb's temple destruction and jizya reimposition) became an existential military threat; and the Mughal Empire entered a decline it never reversed. The lesson for the AP: sustainable social and political arrangements require institutional foundation, not just individual commitment. When on the AP exam, existing elites' power "fluctuated," it's pointing to exactly this kind of policy instability, what one ruler builds, the next can dismantle.
