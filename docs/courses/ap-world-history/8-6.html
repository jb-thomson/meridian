AP World History · Unit 8: Cold War and Decolonization

8.6 Newly Independent States

🗺️ New Borders🏛️ State-Led Development✈️ Migration💰 Economic Policy
  • LO-A: New borders = new conflicts and population movements: The redrawing of political boundaries at decolonization created new states (Israel, Pakistan, Cambodia) but also triggered conflicts and massive population displacement, including the Partition of India (12–20 million displaced) and the Arab-Israeli conflict. Newly independent governments took strong economic roles: Without functioning private sectors and facing enormous development needs, governments in newly independent states typically took a strong role in guiding economic development, from Nasser's nationalization in Egypt to Nyerere's ujamaa socialism in Tanzania. Know the four CED examples: Nasser (Egypt), Indira Gandhi (India), Julius Nyerere (Tanzania), Sirimavo Bandaranaike (Sri Lanka). Migration maintained ties between colony and metropole: Former colonial subjects migrated to the imperial metropoles (Britain, France, US) for economic opportunity. South Asians to Britain, Algerians to France, Filipinos to the US, all CED examples. These migrations maintained cultural and economic ties even after political independence. Two LOs, three themes: LO G = territorial/demographic consequences of boundary redrawing. LO H = economic policies + migration. Know all CED illustrative examples for both.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Why did many newly independent governments take a strong role in guiding the economy rather than relying on free markets?
Newly independent states faced a paradox: they needed rapid economic development to justify independence and meet their peoples' expectations, but they had inherited economies that had been structured to serve the colonial power's interests, extracting raw materials for export, not building diversified industrial economies. Private sectors were tiny or dominated by foreign companies. There were few domestic entrepreneurs with the capital or skills to drive investment. And the time pressure was intense, governments had promised independence would bring prosperity. In this context, state-directed development seemed both necessary (only the state had resources) and urgent (it could be faster than waiting for private markets to develop). The model was partly borrowed from the USSR's success in rapid industrialization through state planning, but also arose independently from the practical constraints facing new governments across Asia and Africa.
2. Why is it historically interesting that Sirimavo Bandaranaike (Sri Lanka) was the world's first female prime minister (1960)?
Sirimavo Bandaranaike became the world's first female prime minister in 1960, years before women achieved comparable political power in most Western democracies. Her election challenged assumptions that political leadership was inherently male, and she went on to serve as prime minister three times. Her rise was partly facilitated by her status as the widow of assassinated Prime Minister S.W.R.D. Bandaranaike, "widow politics" where women gained political positions through family connections was common in post-colonial Asia. But she proved to be an effective leader in her own right, implementing significant nationalization and social welfare programs. Her significance extends beyond symbolism: she demonstrates that decolonization created new political opportunities in some ways, even in societies that remained socially conservative in others.
3. How did migration from former colonies to the imperial "metropole" create a new kind of connection between countries after independence?
Migration from former colonies to the colonial power's home country (metropole) created ongoing cultural, economic, and social ties that persisted after political independence. South Asian migrants in Britain sent remittances home, maintained businesses with South Asia, and created diaspora communities that bridged British and South Asian cultures. Algerian migrants in France similarly remitted money, brought French cultural influences back to Algeria, and created Franco-Algerian communities. Filipino migrants in the US created significant financial flows (remittances are a major share of the Philippine GDP) and cultural bridges. These migration patterns showed that decolonization didn't cleanly sever relationships between colonies and metropoles, economic and cultural interdependence continued, though now within a formally equal relationship rather than an imperial one. The legacy of colonial languages, institutions, and connections shaped patterns of migration for decades.

The Reading

🗺️ New States, New Conflicts: Boundary Redrawing, KC-6.2.III.A

Decolonization created a world of new states. But creating those states required redrawing political boundaries, and boundary redrawing had profound and often devastating consequences. The CED identifies three states created by the redrawing of political boundaries: Israel, Cambodia, and Pakistan.

Israel (1948): Britain held a "mandate" over Palestine after WWI, given by the League of Nations to administer the former Ottoman territory. Jewish immigration to Palestine had increased dramatically in the 1920s–40s, particularly as Jews fled Nazi persecution in Europe. After WWII, the UN proposed a partition plan dividing Palestine between Jewish and Arab states. The Zionist leadership accepted it and declared the State of Israel in May 1948. Arab states immediately went to war; Israel survived and expanded its territory. Approximately 700,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled, the Nakba ("catastrophe" in Arabic). The conflict over the territory of the former British Mandate of Palestine has continued to the present day.

Pakistan (1947): British India was partitioned into two new states: Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan. Pakistan was itself geographically divided. East Pakistan (which became Bangladesh in 1971) and West Pakistan separated by 1,000 miles of Indian territory. The partition created one of the largest forced migrations in history: approximately 12–20 million Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs moved across the new borders, accompanied by communal massacres that killed hundreds of thousands to possibly 2 million people.

Cambodia (1953): Cambodia was part of French Indochina; it achieved independence from France in 1953 under King Norodom Sihanouk. The new state's boundaries reflected the colonial-era borders drawn by France, which placed ethnic Khmer populations primarily within Cambodia but left Khmer minorities in Vietnam and Thailand. Cambodia's post-independence history was shaped by the Vietnam War and Cold War competition, leading ultimately to the genocidal Khmer Rouge regime (1975–79).

💡 Fun Fact
The Partition of India (1947) involved one of the most extraordinary feats of logistics in history. British colonial administrator Cyril Radcliffe was given just 5 weeks to draw the borders of India and Pakistan, despite having never visited South Asia before and being given inadequate and outdated maps. He drew the line, separating Punjab and Bengal, while sitting in Delhi, knowing virtually nothing about the communities he was dividing. The resulting "Radcliffe Line" cut through villages, farmlands, canal systems, and railway lines, creating chaos on the ground. Radcliffe reportedly burned his papers and left India immediately after completing the task, never returning.

💼 Governments Guide Economic Life, KC-6.3.I.C

The CED states that "in newly independent states after World War II, governments often took on a strong role in guiding economic life to promote development." This is one of the defining economic patterns of the decolonization era. Know the four required illustrative examples:

Gamal Abdel Nasser's economic development in Egypt: Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal (1956) and used its revenues for development, including the Aswan High Dam, which transformed Egyptian agriculture by controlling the Nile's floods. He also nationalized major industries and implemented land redistribution. Nasser's approach was called "Arab Socialism", state ownership of key industries combined with social programs. The Aswan Dam itself, financed by the USSR after the US withdrew support, became a symbol of state-led development using superpower Cold War competition to Egypt's advantage.

Indira Gandhi's economic policies in India: As India's prime minister from 1966, Gandhi implemented a strongly state-directed economic model: nationalization of 14 major banks (1969), heavy industry investment, import substitution, and poverty reduction programs. She also oversaw the "Green Revolution", introduction of high-yield wheat and rice varieties that transformed Indian agriculture and enabled India to become food self-sufficient. India's non-aligned foreign policy was matched by an economic policy that resisted both US-style free markets and Soviet-style complete planning.

Julius Nyerere's modernization in Tanzania: Nyerere, Tanzania's first president, developed an ideology called ujamaa (Swahili: "familyhood"). African socialism based on collective village farming, state ownership of industry, and self-reliance rather than foreign investment. The Arusha Declaration (1967) committed Tanzania to these principles. The ujamaa village program relocated millions of Tanzanians into collective farming villages. While Nyerere's commitment to education and healthcare was remarkable, Tanzania's literacy rate rose from 15% to over 85% during his tenure, the economic results were mixed, and Tanzania remained among the world's poorest countries.

Sirimavo Bandaranaike's economic policies in Sri Lanka: The world's first female prime minister nationalized foreign-owned businesses, implemented land reform, and expanded social welfare programs. Her governments took a strongly statist approach to development, taking over the Ceylon Transport Board, nationalizing oil companies, and expanding state-owned enterprises. Sri Lanka's path illustrated both the promise and the challenges of state-led development: strong social indicators (high literacy, long life expectancy) but slow economic growth.

✈️ Migration to the Former Metropole, KC-6.2.III.B

The CED identifies a key continuity across the formal end of empire: "The migration of former colonial subjects to imperial metropoles, usually in the major cities, maintained cultural and economic ties between the colony and the metropole even after the dissolution of empires." Three required illustrative examples:

South Asians to Britain: After Indian independence (1947), South Asians, from India, Pakistan, and later Bangladesh, migrated to Britain in significant numbers, especially after the 1948 British Nationality Act confirmed the right of Commonwealth citizens to live and work in Britain. They settled primarily in industrial cities: Birmingham, Bradford, Leicester, London. By the 1960s, South Asian communities were significant in British life. Their presence transformed British culture (curry became Britain's "national dish"), created ongoing economic and cultural ties with South Asia, and also generated significant social tensions that led to immigration restrictions in the 1960s–70s.

Algerians to France: Algeria's proximity to France and the historical connections of French rule meant that Algerian workers had been migrating to France since the early 20th century. After independence (1962), migration continued. France needed labor for its postwar economic boom; Algerians needed economic opportunity. Algerian communities in Paris, Lyon, and Marseille maintained strong cultural connections to Algeria while also becoming permanent residents of France. This Algerian-French community, sometimes called the Beur generation (children of Algerian immigrants born in France), represents a living legacy of colonialism in contemporary France.

Filipinos to the United States: The Philippines had been a US territory (1898–1946), which created deep structural ties: English as an official language, US-style education systems, and already-established migration patterns. After formal independence (1946), Filipinos continued to migrate to the US, initially as agricultural workers in Hawaii and California, later as professionals (especially nurses and healthcare workers). Today, approximately 4 million Filipinos live in the US; remittances from the Filipino diaspora represent one of the most significant sources of income for the Philippine economy.

a) Briefly explain ONE way that the Partition of India illustrates the consequences of boundary redrawing for population and conflict.
Sample Answers: a) The Partition of India (1947) divided British India into two new states, Hindu-majority India and Muslim-majority Pakistan, along religious lines. The new international border cut through the Punjab region, separating communities that had lived together for centuries. An estimated 12–20 million people fled across the border in one of the largest forced migrations in history: Hindus and Sikhs fled westward from what became Pakistan, while Muslims fled eastward from India. The migration was accompanied by massive communal violence, massacres, rape, and arson, that killed hundreds of thousands to possibly 2 million people. This illustrates how boundary redrawing, especially when done rapidly without regard for mixed communities, can trigger catastrophic humanitarian consequences. b) Newly independent states inherited economies that had been structured to serve the colonial power's interests, extracting raw materials for export rather than building diversified domestic industries. Private sectors were typically tiny or dominated by foreign companies with little interest in national development. Only governments had the authority to nationalize foreign-owned resources, the ability to direct investment toward priority sectors, and the political legitimacy (having won independence) to demand economic transformation. Without state direction, development would have been slow and dependent on foreign capital that came with conditions serving foreign rather than national interests. State-led development was therefore both a practical necessity and a nationalist assertion of economic sovereignty. c) South Asian migration to Britain maintained significant economic and cultural ties between the Indian subcontinent and Britain after Indian independence (1947). Economically, South Asian migrants sent remittances to family members in India and Pakistan, creating significant financial flows back to South Asia. Culturally, South Asian communities established restaurants, religious institutions (mosques, temples, gurdwaras), and cultural organizations that made South Asian food, music, and religion permanent features of British urban life. British South Asians also maintained connections through travel, family ties, and business relationships that kept economic and cultural exchange flowing in both directions, demonstrating that political independence didn't sever the structural connections created by decades of colonial rule.
Evaluate the extent to which newly independent states achieved genuine economic independence in the period from 1945 to 1975.
Model Thesis: Although newly independent states used state-directed economic policies, nationalization, land reform, and import substitution, to assert economic sovereignty and reduce direct foreign control over key resources, they faced persistent structural constraints including commodity-dependent economies, foreign debt, Cold War conditionality, and post-colonial migration patterns that maintained economic ties to former metropoles, ultimately limiting the genuine economic independence that political independence had promised.
Model answer: Both cases involved the redrawing of former imperial boundaries to create new states, and both triggered massive conflict and population displacement. In both cases, the new boundary split communities that had lived together. Arab and Jewish communities in Palestine, and Hindu, Muslim, and Sikh communities in South Asia. The displacement was similar in scale: the Palestinian Nakba created approximately 700,000 Palestinian refugees; the Partition of India displaced 12–20 million. They differed in several important ways: Israel was a new state created through UN decision and Zionist movement, representing an internationally recognized claim of Jewish statehood; Pakistan was the result of an independence movement demanding a Muslim-majority state within the existing colonial unit of British India. Israel's creation involved ongoing contested sovereignty over land (the Arab-Israeli conflict continues); Pakistan's creation involved displacement and communal violence during partition but the international borders were eventually stabilized. Both remain contested in different ways.
Model answer: Newly independent governments took strong economic roles for compelling reasons: colonial economies had been structured to extract raw materials for the metropole, not to build diversified domestic economies; private sectors were tiny or foreign-controlled; development pressures were intense (independence promised prosperity). State-led development offered a way to rapidly build infrastructure, nationalize foreign-owned resources, and direct investment toward priority sectors. The limitations were significant: state enterprises were often inefficient without market competition, prone to corruption, and dependent on foreign loans when domestic savings were insufficient. Export earnings from raw materials fluctuated with global commodity prices. Many newly independent states accumulated significant debt during the 1960s–70s that contributed to economic crises in the 1980s. Tanzania's ujamaa, for example, improved social outcomes (literacy, healthcare) but left the economy poor and aid-dependent.
Model answer: Post-colonial migration maintained ties in several ways. Economically, remittances sent by migrants to family at home created significant financial flows. Filipino remittances represent billions of dollars annually to the Philippine economy, and Algerian remittances were important to Algeria's economy especially in early post-independence decades. Culturally, migrant communities carried the culture of their home countries into the metropole: South Asian communities in Britain created curry houses, Bollywood cinema, and religious institutions (mosques, temples) that made South Asian culture a permanent part of British life. At the same time, migrants adopted elements of the metropole's culture, including language (most South Asian migrants in Britain spoke English), education systems, and consumer culture, which they brought back when visiting or returning home. These bidirectional cultural flows created hybrid identities that transcended the formal end of empire.
Model answer: Nyerere and Gandhi illustrate that "state-directed development" was not a single policy but a spectrum. Nyerere's ujamaa was ideologically radical, a comprehensive attempt to restructure Tanzania's economy along African socialist lines, collectivizing farming and rejecting Western capitalist investment. It prioritized equality and self-reliance over growth, producing good social outcomes (education, healthcare) but slow economic growth. Gandhi's approach was more pragmatic: nationalizing key sectors (banks, industries) while maintaining a mixed economy with significant private sector activity, using state power to address inequality and promote food security through the Green Revolution without abolishing private property. India's approach, sometimes called the "Nehruvian consensus", produced faster economic growth than Tanzania's but also significant inefficiency and corruption in state enterprises. The range from Tanzania to India shows that newly independent governments made very different choices within the broad category of state-directed development.
