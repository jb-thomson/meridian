4.6 Internal and External Challenges to State Power from 1450 to 1750

🏛️ Governance👥 Social⚔️ Conflict🌍 Interactions
  • LO-A: State expansion creates resistance — The core argument for this topic: as states expanded and centralized power in the period 1450–1750, they encountered resistance from "an array of social, political, and economic groups on a local level." This resistance took many forms — indigenous uprisings, elite rebellions, peasant revolts, and enslaved resistance — but all shared a common cause: expansion of state power that disrupted existing social arrangements. Local resistance examples (AP exam-named) — Pueblo Revolt (1680): indigenous peoples of New Mexico drove Spanish colonizers out of the region for 12 years. Fronde (1648–1653): French nobles and Paris parlements rebelled against Cardinal Mazarin's expansion of royal power during Louis XIV's minority. Cossack revolts (Russia): free military communities on the Russian frontier resisted incorporation into the Russian state. Maratha conflict with Mughals: Hindu Maratha Confederacy challenged Mughal expansion in the Deccan plateau. Ana Nzinga (Ndongo and Matamba): West African queen who resisted Portuguese encroachment for decades. Metacom's War (King Philip's War, 1675–1676): New England indigenous coalition resisted English colonial expansion. Enslaved resistance — Enslaved Africans in the Americas did not passively accept their condition. Two major forms of organized resistance: Maroon societies — communities of escaped enslaved people who established autonomous settlements in remote areas of the Caribbean, Brazil, and South America, sometimes maintaining independence for generations. Enslaved resistance in North America — slower to organize than Caribbean/Brazilian Maroon communities but included work slowdowns, sabotage, flight, and occasional armed uprisings.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 drove the Spanish out of New Mexico for 12 years. What factors allowed this relatively small indigenous population to succeed against a Spanish colonial power that had previously been militarily superior?
Several factors contributed. First, surprise and coordination: the Pueblo leader Popé organized a coordinated uprising across multiple Pueblo communities simultaneously — something the Spanish had never anticipated and couldn't respond to fast enough. Second, legitimate grievances had accumulated over generations: Spanish demands for labor, tribute, and religious conversion had created deep resentment that made broad coalition-building possible. Third, Spanish colonial infrastructure in New Mexico was thin — isolated from Mexico City, dependent on a small military force, without the rapid reinforcement capacity of more densely settled colonies. The revolt's success also depended on indigenous knowledge of the terrain and on the colonizers' overconfidence — they had assumed decades of colonial domination had broken indigenous capacity for organized resistance.
2. The Fronde (1648–1653) was a series of rebellions in France involving both noble elites and urban commoners against royal centralization. Why would these two very different groups — nobles and commoners — both resist the same royal policies?
Nobles and commoners were resisting the same royal centralization for very different reasons. Nobles (especially the parlementaires — hereditary judicial officers) resisted because royal centralization threatened their traditional privileges, their independent power bases, and their ability to obstruct royal policy through legal mechanisms. They were defending elite autonomy against a more powerful central state. Urban commoners (especially in Paris) resisted because centralization usually meant new or higher taxes, often imposed to fund wars, and economic disruption that fell disproportionately on ordinary people. Both groups shared the experience of having their existing social positions threatened by an expanding state, even though what they were defending was very different — nobles defended privilege; commoners defended subsistence. This coalition of differently motivated resisters is a pattern common to many state-building conflicts.
3. Maroon societies — communities of escaped enslaved people — managed to sustain themselves for decades and sometimes centuries in remote areas of the Caribbean, Brazil, and Suriname. What does their persistence reveal about the limits of European colonial power?
Maroon persistence reveals that European colonial power, while formidable in coastal and accessible areas, had significant geographic limits. Dense jungle, mountain terrain, and remote areas provided natural defenses that European military technology struggled to overcome. European soldiers accustomed to open-field warfare were at a severe disadvantage in jungle environments. Furthermore, Maroon communities often had military knowledge from African backgrounds (some Maroon leaders came from warrior traditions in West Africa) and could draw on indigenous environmental knowledge to navigate terrain Europeans couldn't follow. The most successful Maroon communities (the Quilombo dos Palmares in Brazil, the Windward and Leeward Maroons in Jamaica) forced colonial authorities into negotiated treaties recognizing their autonomy — demonstrating that even the most exploitative colonial systems could be successfully resisted when the terrain, organization, and motivation aligned.
4. Ana Nzinga of Ndongo and Matamba (present-day Angola) resisted Portuguese colonization for decades through a combination of military force, diplomacy, and strategic alliance-building. What does her resistance strategy reveal about effective anti-colonial tactics?
Ana Nzinga's effectiveness came from deploying multiple strategies simultaneously and adapting tactically. Militarily, she organized and led military forces that harassed Portuguese outposts and slave-raiding operations. Diplomatically, she negotiated with the Portuguese when advantageous (converting to Christianity in 1622 to gain diplomatic standing, later abandoning the conversion when circumstances changed), sought Dutch alliances (the Dutch were competing with Portugal in West Africa and had their own reasons to support her), and built alliances with other regional states. She understood that resistance to a technologically superior adversary requires asymmetric tactics — you can't win by fighting on the enemy's terms, so you shift to the terms where you have advantages (knowledge of terrain, flexibility, alliances with the enemy's enemies). Her decades of effective resistance established Matamba as an independent state that maintained sovereignty well into the 18th century.
5. Metacom's War (King Philip's War, 1675–1676) was the most destructive war per capita in American history. Why did indigenous New England peoples, who had largely maintained peaceful relations with English settlers for over fifty years, launch such a widespread and coordinated uprising in 1675?
Half a century of English colonial expansion had progressively eroded the conditions that made peace sustainable. English population growth meant continuous land acquisition — purchases that indigenous communities often understood differently than the English did (as use-rights rather than permanent alienation), and which were increasingly obtained under duress. The expansion of English settlements disrupted indigenous subsistence economies, as English livestock destroyed indigenous crops. The court system increasingly subjected indigenous peoples to English law rather than their own. By 1675, Metacom (Wampanoag leader, called "King Philip" by the English) had concluded that the trajectory of English expansion, if unchecked, would result in the complete displacement and elimination of indigenous New England societies. The uprising was not an impulsive reaction to a single incident — it was a considered strategic decision by indigenous leaders who believed they had to act while they still had sufficient military capacity to do so.

⚔️ Why State Expansion Creates Resistance

The period 1450–1750 was an era of enormous state expansion: Spanish and Portuguese empires stretched across oceans; the Mughal Empire unified most of South Asia; the Russian Empire pushed east across Siberia; the Qing Dynasty conquered China and Central Asia. All of this expansion required something from the people it incorporated: tribute, labor, land, religious conversion, or political submission.

When states demanded these things, people resisted. The AP exam's key insight for Topic 4.6 is that "state expansion and centralization led to resistance from an array of social, political, and economic groups on a local level." This resistance was not random — it arose when state demands threatened the existing social arrangements that communities depended on for survival and identity.

The AP exam's provides a rich set of illustrative examples spanning four continents. Let's examine each one.

🏜️ Local Resistance Across the World

📝 AP Tip
All six AP exam examples involve groups whose existing arrangements were disrupted by state expansion or centralization. The Pueblo Revolt = indigenous response to colonial religious suppression and labor demands. The Fronde = elite response to loss of noble privileges. Cossack revolts = frontier community response to incorporation into serfdom. Maratha conflict = Hindu response to Mughal religious intolerance + territorial expansion. Ana Nzinga = response to Portuguese slave-raiding. Metacom's War = indigenous response to English land encroachment. When asked to analyze any of these, identify: (1) what state expansion demanded, (2) what the resisting group was defending, (3) why resistance took the specific form it did.

🌿 Enslaved Resistance: Maroon Societies

Among the most significant forms of resistance in the period 1450–1750 was the organized resistance of enslaved Africans in the Americas. The College Board specifically identifies two forms: Maroon societies in the Caribbean and Brazil, and resistance in North America.

Maroon societies were communities formed by escaped enslaved people who fled into remote mountains, forests, and swamps beyond the reach of colonial authority. The word "Maroon" derives from the Spanish cimarrón (wild, runaway). These communities were not random collections of fugitives — they were organized societies with governance structures, agricultural systems, military forces, and, crucially, a fierce determination to defend their freedom.

A Maroon village in the mountains of Jamaica — remote, defensible, self-sufficient. Maroon communities used terrain knowledge and guerrilla tactics to resist colonial military forces for generations. AI-generated illustration (Google Gemini, 2025), commercially licensed.

The most famous Maroon communities:

  • Quilombo dos Palmares (Brazil, c. 1605–1694) — The largest Maroon community in history. Located in the mountains of Alagoas, at its peak Palmares housed tens of thousands of people. It survived repeated Portuguese and Dutch military expeditions for nearly ninety years before being finally destroyed in 1694. Its last great leader, Zumbi dos Palmares, became a Brazilian national hero and symbol of Black resistance; November 20 is Zumbi Day in Brazil.
  • Windward and Leeward Maroons of Jamaica — Two distinct communities in the mountains of Jamaica who resisted British authority so effectively that in 1739–1740 the British signed formal treaties with them, recognizing their autonomy in exchange for a promise not to receive new escapees. The British signed a treaty with people they had been trying to exterminate — a remarkable acknowledgment of the limits of colonial military power.
  • Suriname Maroons — The Dutch also signed treaties with Maroon communities in Suriname; the descendants of these communities continue to maintain distinct cultural identities today, with their cultural traditions recognized as UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage.
💡 Fun Fact
In 1739, British Governor Edward Trelawny signed the Maroon Treaty with the leader Cudjoe of the Leeward Maroons of Jamaica — formally recognizing their freedom and autonomy in 150,000 acres of mountain territory. This was, in effect, the British colonial government formally acknowledging that it had failed to defeat a community of formerly enslaved people in military combat and was willing to grant them legal independence rather than continue fighting. The Maroon communities of Jamaica retain treaty rights today — 285 years later.

⛓️ Enslaved Resistance in North America

Enslaved resistance in North America was more difficult to organize into sustained Maroon communities than in the Caribbean and Brazil, largely because the North American geography offered fewer natural defenses (no accessible mountain ranges in the Southeast, where most enslaved people lived), and because the enslaved population was more dispersed across individual farms rather than concentrated on large plantations.

Nevertheless, North American enslaved resistance was constant, though it took different forms:

  • Work slowdowns and sabotage — The most pervasive and difficult-to-suppress form of resistance; enslaved workers could work slowly, break tools, feign illness, and damage crops without the risk of organized armed revolt
  • Flight (petit marronage) — Individual or small-group escapes, temporary or permanent; the Underground Railroad of the 19th century had earlier precedents
  • Cultural resistance — Maintaining African languages, religious practices (see Vodou, Topic 4.5), music, and family connections despite systematic efforts to destroy them
  • Armed uprisings — Less common and more dangerous, but occurred: Stono Rebellion (South Carolina, 1739) was the largest armed enslaved rebellion in North American history before the Revolution
📝 AP Tip
The AP exam may ask about forms of enslaved resistance beyond just armed rebellion. Be prepared to discuss the full spectrum: work slowdowns, sabotage, flight, cultural preservation, and community formation as forms of resistance that didn't require weapons. Maroon societies represent the most organized and sustained form; everyday resistance was more widespread but less visible in historical records (enslaved people didn't document their own resistance for enslavers to read).

Answer all three parts.

Part A

Explain ONE cause of local resistance to state expansion in the period 1450–1750, using a specific example.

The Pueblo Revolt of 1680 was caused by Spanish colonial demands for indigenous labor and tribute alongside the suppression of traditional Pueblo religious practices. Spanish colonizers required Pueblo communities to provide labor for missions and the colonial economy under the encomienda system while Franciscan missionaries actively destroyed kivas (ceremonial spaces) and suppressed Pueblo religion. The combination of economic exploitation and cultural/religious suppression drove Pueblo communities under the leadership of Popé to coordinate a simultaneous uprising across multiple communities, successfully driving Spanish colonizers out of New Mexico for twelve years.

Part B

Explain how enslaved Africans in the Americas challenged colonial authority through organized resistance in the period 1450–1750.

Maroon societies — communities of escaped enslaved Africans established in remote areas beyond colonial control — represent the most sustained form of organized enslaved resistance in the Americas. The Quilombo dos Palmares in northeastern Brazil, established in the early 17th century, housed tens of thousands of people at its peak and survived nearly ninety years of Portuguese military campaigns through geographic inaccessibility, agricultural self-sufficiency, and organized military defense. The Maroon communities of Jamaica similarly resisted British military forces so effectively that in 1739 the British colonial government signed formal treaties recognizing their autonomy — a remarkable acknowledgment that enslaved resistance had defeated the military power of one of the world's dominant colonial empires.

Part C

Explain ONE similarity between two different examples of resistance to state expansion in the period 1450–1750.

Both the Maratha conflict with the Mughals and the Pueblo Revolt share the characteristic of using terrain and asymmetric military tactics to resist a much more powerful state. Shivaji Bhosale used mountain fortresses and guerrilla cavalry raids that made Mughal conventional military operations in the Deccan largely ineffective; the Pueblo peoples used their intimate knowledge of New Mexican terrain and coordinated surprise to overwhelm a thin Spanish colonial presence that depended on supply lines extending hundreds of miles to Mexico City. In both cases, the resisting group could not match the expanding state in conventional military strength but could render the cost of continued expansion prohibitive by exploiting terrain advantages and avoiding pitched battles on the adversary's terms.

Model answer: Both the Pueblo Revolt and Metacom's War were coordinated, large-scale indigenous rebellions against European colonial expansion — both driven by accumulated grievances over land, labor, and cultural autonomy. Both achieved significant initial military success. But their outcomes differed substantially. The Pueblo Revolt succeeded in driving the Spanish out for 12 years because New Mexico was geographically isolated (far from Spanish reinforcement centers in Mexico City), the Spanish colonial presence was thin and dependent on a small garrison, and the Pueblo peoples had geographic advantages in territory they knew intimately. Metacom's War ultimately failed because English colonial New England had far greater population density, faster access to reinforcements, and the ability to sustain military pressure over time that the Pueblo Spanish could not. The Narragansett, Wampanoag, and allied peoples were eventually worn down by English demographic superiority. The lesson: the success of resistance movements depends not just on the strength of the resisters but on the logistics and sustainability of the opposing state's military capacity.
Model answer: The Fronde's failure reveals that elite resistance to state centralization requires sustained unity among the resisting elites, which the French nobility could not maintain. The Fronde was actually two distinct rebellions (the parlementaire Fronde and the noble Fronde) that sometimes worked together and sometimes undermined each other. Different noble factions had different grievances and different goals, and the most powerful princes were as motivated by personal ambition as by constitutional principle. When it became clear that the alternative to Mazarin's policies might be chaos and civil war (the parlementaires eventually settled), the appeal of royal order overcame resistance to royal power. Louis XIV drew the lesson: bring the nobility to Versailles, give them ceremony and privilege in exchange for political submission, and never let them coordinate enough to threaten royal authority again. Elite resistance only succeeds when elites can maintain united opposition against a state that hasn't yet fully consolidated its power — exactly the conditions that were running out in 17th-century France.
Model answer: Ana Nzinga's effectiveness came from her recognition that no single strategy was sufficient against a powerful adversary — effective resistance requires deploying multiple tools simultaneously and adapting tactically as circumstances changed. Military force alone was insufficient: Portugal had superior weaponry and could sustain losses that would devastate her smaller forces. Diplomacy alone was insufficient: Portuguese slave-trading interests consistently overrode diplomatic agreements. Her genius was in combining strategies: she used military force to make Portuguese encroachment costly while simultaneously negotiating to buy time, converted to Christianity in 1622 to gain diplomatic standing as a "Christian queen" who deserved different treatment, sought Dutch alliances (the Dutch had their own reasons to oppose Portuguese dominance in West Central Africa), and moved her base of operations to Matamba when Ndongo was lost, creating a new power base. This adaptive, multi-tool strategy is characteristic of successful asymmetric resistance: you cannot beat a stronger adversary on their terms, so you constantly shift to terms where you have advantages.
Model answer: Geography and demography were the primary factors. The Caribbean islands have dramatic mountain ranges (Jamaica's Blue Mountains, Haiti's Massif du Nord) that are difficult to penetrate militarily and easy to defend with knowledge of the terrain. Brazil's interior contained vast rainforests and highlands — the Quilombo dos Palmares was located in the rugged mountains of Alagoas, inaccessible enough to survive nearly ninety years of military campaigns. North American geography in the colonial Southeast (where most enslaved people were concentrated) had no comparable natural fortifications — the Piedmont and coastal plain terrain was too accessible for sustained fugitive communities to establish themselves. Demographic factors also mattered: large plantations in the Caribbean and Brazil concentrated enslaved people in numbers that made organized resistance and flight more achievable than the dispersed smaller farms of colonial North America, where enslaved workers were more isolated from potential co-conspirators.
Model answer: Taken together, these examples reveal that resistance to state expansion was universal — it occurred on every inhabited continent, in response to every major expanding state, across every social position (from enslaved people at the absolute bottom of social hierarchies to nobles at the top). This universality suggests that resistance was not a product of any particular culture's values but a predictable structural consequence of state expansion itself: whenever states demanded more from people — more labor, more tribute, more land, more religious conformity — those people organized to defend what they had. The examples also reveal a spectrum of outcomes: some resistance succeeded temporarily (Pueblo Revolt, Maroon treaties), some was definitively crushed (Metacom's War), some shaped the ultimate form of the state against which it was directed (Maratha challenge leading to Mughal decline, Cossack revolts shaping Russian serfdom policy). No state in this period expanded without cost; resistance was the price of empire.
