6.7 Effects of Migration in an Interconnected World from 1750 to 1900

🌍 Cultural Interactions👥 Social🏛️ Political
  • LO-A: Ethnic enclaves and diaspora communities: Migrants clustered together in urban neighborhoods for practical reasons: shared language, food, religion, and community support. These ethnic enclaves became culturally vibrant and economically self-sustaining. Chinese communities in San Francisco and Southeast Asia, Irish communities in Boston and New York, Italian communities in Buenos Aires and New York, Indian communities in Natal and Mauritius, all preserved cultural identities while adapting to new environments. KC-5.4.III.A. Gender and migration, women left behind: Most 19th-century labor migration was predominantly male: men left to find work, leaving women to manage households, farms, and children in sending communities. The CED (KC-5.4.III.B) notes that in many sending societies women had to take on new economic and social roles when men migrated. Remittances, money sent home by migrants, could transform family economic situations, but their irregular flow created financial uncertainty for families left behind. Nativism and immigration restriction: Large-scale immigration generated backlash in receiving societies, particularly when migrants were racially or culturally different from the majority. The most explicit examples are the Chinese Exclusion Act (U.S. 1882), the first U.S. law to restrict immigration based on race and nationality, and the White Australia Policy (1901), which explicitly restricted non-European immigration. KC-5.4.III.C. Anti-immigrant sentiment combined racial prejudice with economic anxiety about job competition. Cultural and demographic transformation: Migration transformed both sending and receiving societies. Receiving societies became more diverse in language, religion, food, and cultural practice. Sending societies were transformed by remittances (which funded economic development), returnees with new skills and ideas, and the demographic impact of losing working-age adults. These changes were uneven and contested, not all communities welcomed transformation.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Why might immigrants cluster together in ethnic enclaves rather than dispersing throughout a new city or country?
Ethnic enclaves formed for practical and social reasons. Language: newly arrived immigrants often didn’t speak the local language, making it essential to live near people who did. Economic networks: established community members could provide jobs, credit, housing, and business connections to newcomers. Social support: migration is psychologically difficult; familiar cultural practices, food, and religious institutions provided emotional comfort. Protection: in cities where immigrants faced discrimination and sometimes violence, clustering with members of the same community provided safety in numbers. Legal status: some immigrants lived in legal uncertainty, and community members could provide information and protection. The Chinese community in San Francisco, for example, developed a self-contained economic and social system partly because discrimination made integration into mainstream American society dangerous and unwelcoming.
2. When men migrated for work, women in sending communities often had to take on new roles. Give two examples of specific new roles women might assume.
First: economic management. When men left for wage labor abroad, women often became the de facto head of household, making economic decisions, managing agricultural land, paying bills, and negotiating with landlords and merchants, all functions previously handled by men in patriarchal societies. This gave women new decision-making authority they hadn’t previously held. Second: agricultural labor. In peasant communities that depended on family farming, women had to take over agricultural work previously done by men, plowing, planting, harvesting, in addition to their existing domestic responsibilities. Both examples show that male migration had profound effects on gender roles in sending societies, often expanding women’s economic responsibilities while leaving them with less financial security (if remittances were irregular) and more physical labor.
3. The United States passed the Chinese Exclusion Act in 1882, restricting Chinese immigration. What combination of factors likely drove this policy?
Several factors combined: (1) Economic anxiety: Chinese workers had helped build the transcontinental railroad (1863–69) and moved into California’s mining and agricultural sectors. White workers, especially in the West, feared competition for jobs and blamed Chinese workers for depressing wages. (2) Racial prejudice: 19th-century American society was deeply racialized, and Chinese immigrants were viewed as racially inferior and culturally incompatible with American society. Politicians used racist language freely in debates about the Act. (3) Social anxiety: Chinese communities in San Francisco (Chinatown) and elsewhere were culturally distinct, different language, religion, food, and social practices, which generated social anxiety in white communities. (4) Political calculation: Chinese immigrants could not vote (they were not eligible for naturalization under existing law), while the white workers who feared Chinese competition could and did. The Act was politically easy to pass.
4. How might remittances, money sent home by migrants to their families, both help and harm sending communities?
Remittances help by providing cash income to families that otherwise have only subsistence agricultural earnings. This money could fund children’s education, pay off debts, build better housing, or provide capital for small businesses. For poor rural communities, remittances from family members abroad could be the most significant source of cash income. Remittances also helped: when they funded productive investments (education, tools, small businesses), they could contribute to local economic development. Remittances harm: (1) Dependence, communities that become dependent on migrant remittances may not develop their own economic activities, creating permanent reliance on continued emigration. (2) Irregular flow, remittances were unreliable: if a migrant worker lost his job or died, the family was left without income or support. (3) Social disruption, money from abroad could create inequalities within communities, with remittance-receiving families better off than those without migrants. (4) Brain drain, if the most educated and motivated community members emigrate, local development suffers.
5. Indian indentured workers who went to Trinidad, Fiji, and Natal created distinct “Indo-Caribbean,” “Indo-Fijian,” and “Indian South African” communities that still exist today. What does this tell us about how migrants maintained and adapted their cultural identities?
These communities demonstrate both cultural preservation and cultural adaptation. Migrants maintained elements of their origin culture: Hindu and Muslim religious practices, Indian food (curries, roti), languages (Hindi, Tamil), music, and family structure. But they also adapted: the specific mix of Indian linguistic, religious, and cultural practices in Trinidad is different from what existed in any particular Indian origin community because migrants came from different parts of India and their cultures merged in the new context. New forms emerged: Indo-Caribbean music (like chutney soca), Indo-Fijian Hinduism with Fijian influences, and Indo-South African legal and political traditions like those of young Gandhi in Natal. This dual process, preservation and adaptation, is characteristic of diaspora communities generally, and it explains why diaspora cultures are never simple transplants of origin cultures but distinct new formations.

The Reading

🏘️ Ethnic Enclaves: Building Community in New Places

When migrants arrived in new societies, they rarely dispersed evenly. They clustered in neighborhoods near people from the same region, language, and cultural background, forming ethnic enclaves that became the foundation of immigrant communities. The CED lists KC-5.4.III.A as one of the key effects of migration: sending and receiving societies were transformed, and migrants often created ethnic enclaves that preserved their cultural identities in new locations.

Chinese migrants created vibrant communities in San Francisco (Chinatown, established during the Gold Rush), in cities across Southeast Asia (Singapore, Penang, Manila, Batavia), and in the Caribbean and South America. These communities maintained Chinese-language newspapers, cultural associations, temples, and commercial networks that connected them to each other and to China. They were often formally organized through huiguan (hometown associations) that provided mutual aid, mediated disputes, and represented the community to external authorities.

Indian migrants to Trinidad, Mauritius, Natal (South Africa), and Fiji created communities that maintained Hindu and Muslim religious practices, South Asian food traditions, and family structures. These communities were created primarily by indentured workers, but evolved over generations into substantial permanent communities that have shaped the national cultures of their host countries. Today, approximately 40% of Trinidad’s population is of Indian descent; Mauritius is about 70% Indo-Mauritian.

Irish communities in Boston, New York, and Chicago; Italian communities in Buenos Aires and New York; Lebanese merchants across Latin America: all followed similar patterns, creating cultural institutions, churches, newspapers, mutual aid societies, schools, that maintained ethnic identity while facilitating adaptation to new environments.

💡 Fun Fact
Young Mohandas Gandhi arrived in Natal, South Africa in 1893 to work as a lawyer for an Indian merchant. The experience of encountering the Indian community in South Africa, and experiencing racial discrimination firsthand (he was famously thrown off a first-class railway car despite having a valid ticket), transformed him from an ordinary lawyer into a political activist. The Indian diaspora in South Africa was the laboratory in which Gandhi developed the methods of nonviolent resistance he later used in India. Diaspora communities don’t just preserve culture. They create new ones.

♀️ Gender and Migration: Women Left Behind

19th-century labor migration was predominantly male. Most indentured workers, railroad laborers, and factory workers who migrated were men. Women in sending communities experienced migration’s effects in specific ways that the CED identifies as KC-5.4.III.B: in many sending societies, women took on new economic and social roles when men emigrated.

In southern Italy, Mexican village communities, and South Asian sending regions, the departure of adult men for wage labor meant women had to manage households, farms, and family finances largely alone. In agricultural communities, women took over plowing, planting, and harvesting, labor previously done by men. In mercantile communities, women ran family businesses. Women became the primary point of contact for local authorities, handled financial transactions, and made decisions that patriarchal culture had previously reserved for men.

This shift had complex effects. For some women, male emigration created genuine new autonomy: managing a household budget, negotiating with landlords, and making economic decisions were real expansions of agency. For others, it meant overwhelming burdens: all the previous male labor responsibilities added to existing domestic ones, without additional income until remittances arrived, if they arrived regularly at all.

Remittances, money sent home by migrant workers, could be transformative. A regular flow of cash from an Argentine meatpacking plant or an American railway could fund children’s education, pay off a family’s debts, build a better house, or provide capital for a small business. The CED notes remittances specifically as one of the effects of migration on sending communities. But remittances were also unreliable: if a worker lost his job, was injured, or simply stopped sending money, families were left without the income they had come to depend on.

🚫 Nativism and Immigration Restriction

The large-scale immigration of the 19th century generated substantial backlash in receiving societies, particularly when immigrants were racially or culturally distinct from the majority population. The CED identifies this as KC-5.4.III.C: the effects of migration in receiving societies were often contested, and states and private entities responded with regulation of immigration and conditions of labor.

In the United States, the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 was the most explicit example. Chinese migrants had been essential to American economic development: they built the western portion of the transcontinental railroad, worked California’s mines and farms, and staffed manufacturing industries. But as white workers in the West competed for the same jobs, racial prejudice and economic anxiety combined into a powerful political coalition. The Chinese Exclusion Act prohibited Chinese laborers from entering the United States and prevented existing Chinese residents from becoming citizens. It was the first U.S. federal law to restrict immigration by race and nationality. It remained in effect (with expansions) until 1943 and stands as one of the most explicit examples of racism in American immigration law.

In Australia, the White Australia Policy (formalized in the Immigration Restriction Act of 1901) explicitly sought to maintain Australia as a white, European nation by restricting immigration from Asia, Africa, and the Pacific. The policy used a “dictation test”, where officials could administer a language test in any European language to exclude unwanted migrants, as a technically race-neutral mechanism for racial exclusion. Like the Chinese Exclusion Act, it reflected settler colonial anxieties about racial purity and demographic control.

Anti-immigrant sentiment in the United States also targeted European groups: Irish immigrants faced “No Irish Need Apply” discrimination in the 1850s-1860s; Italian and Eastern European immigrants faced nativist restriction through the Immigration Act of 1924. The pattern was consistent: as immigration scaled up and the demographic composition of immigrant groups shifted, nativist backlash sought to use political power to restrict cultural and demographic change.

💡 Fun Fact
Australia’s “dictation test” for immigration was administered in any European language the official chose, including Latin and Welsh. Immigration officials used it to exclude non-European migrants who were otherwise fully qualified, and to exclude specific European migrants who were considered politically undesirable. Egon Kisch, a Czech journalist and anti-fascist activist who arrived in Australia in 1934, was given the dictation test in Scottish Gaelic, a language he’d never encountered. When he predictably failed, he was ordered deported.

🌏 Cultural Transformation in Receiving and Sending Societies

Migration transformed both receiving and sending societies in ways that went beyond economics. In receiving societies, immigrant communities brought new languages, religions, foods, musical traditions, and cultural practices. New York in 1900 was a kaleidoscope of languages: Italian, Yiddish, Polish, Chinese, Irish Gaelic, and dozens more were spoken in different neighborhoods. Catholic churches, Jewish synagogues, Chinese Buddhist temples, and Greek Orthodox parishes all served immigrant communities in cities that had been predominantly Protestant English-speaking a generation earlier.

These cultural transformations were contested. Nativists pushed for assimilation, the erasure of immigrant cultural differences in favor of majority culture. Immigrants pushed back: maintaining cultural identity was not just sentimental but practical, as it provided community support networks essential for economic survival. The result was an ongoing negotiation between preservation and adaptation that produced the hybrid cultures characteristic of immigrant societies.

In sending societies, returnees, migrants who came back after years abroad, brought new ideas, skills, and capital. Japanese Americans who returned from California brought new agricultural techniques. Lebanese migrants returned from South America with savings that funded local businesses. Indian workers who returned from Natal or Trinidad sometimes brought legal and political knowledge acquired abroad. Gandhi’s return to India in 1915, shaped by his South African experience, transformed Indian nationalist politics.

Answer parts A, B, and C. Each requires a separate short response. Do not write a full essay.

Part A

Briefly describe ONE way migration transformed a receiving society in the period 1750–1900, using a specific ethnic enclave or diaspora community as your example.

Chinese migration to Singapore in the 19th century transformed the city from a small British colonial outpost into a diverse, predominantly Chinese-speaking commercial center. Chinese migrants, who came as traders, laborers, and merchants from various southern Chinese provinces, created distinct clan associations and dialect organizations that provided mutual aid, education, and commercial networks. By 1900, Singapore’s Chinese majority maintained Chinese-language newspapers, Buddhist and Taoist temples, and a commercial culture oriented toward Chinese trade networks across Southeast Asia. The city’s transformation from a small colonial port to a major commercial center was substantially driven by Chinese migrant labor, capital, and commercial energy. Singapore’s contemporary Chinese-majority identity reflects this 19th-century migration history.

Part B

Briefly explain ONE way migration affected the lives of women in a sending society in the period 1750–1900.

In southern Italian villages, the mass emigration of adult men to factories in northern Italy and construction sites in Argentina left women as the primary managers of family farms and household economies. Women who had previously been responsible only for domestic labor and childcare were now also making planting decisions, negotiating with landlords and merchants, managing debt payments, and handling the irregular flows of remittances from absent husbands. KC-5.4.III.B specifically identifies this pattern: in many sending societies, women took on new economic and social roles when men emigrated. The effect was ambivalent, some women developed real skills and autonomy; others faced crushing burdens without the social support of their partners and community structures that had depended on male presence.

Part C

Briefly explain how the Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) or the White Australia Policy (1901) reflected the effects of immigration on receiving societies (KC-5.4.III.C).

The Chinese Exclusion Act (1882) reflected the combination of racial prejudice and economic anxiety that characterized nativist responses to immigration. Chinese workers had been essential to building the western transcontinental railroad and to California’s agricultural economy, but when economic competition intensified in the 1870s recession, white working-class Californians organized politically to demand Chinese exclusion. The Act was the first U.S. law to restrict immigration by race and nationality, explicitly targeting Chinese people for exclusion while imposing no similar restrictions on European immigrants. It reflected a fundamental tension in receiving societies: immigrant labor was economically valuable, but immigrant cultural presence generated anxieties about racial composition and job competition that nativist politics sought to resolve through exclusion. KC-5.4.III.C identifies this pattern: states and private entities in receiving societies responded to migration with regulation, restriction, and attempts to control the demographic composition of their populations.

Once the railroad was built and Chinese workers moved into other sectors competing with white workers, the same groups that had benefited from Chinese labor, California’s agricultural economy, mining companies, manufacturing industries, supported or tolerated exclusion. Economic utility did not translate into political rights: Chinese immigrants could not vote, could not become citizens under existing naturalization laws, and had no political constituency to advocate for their inclusion. The Exclusion Act demonstrates that without political representation, even economically essential communities are vulnerable to exclusion when conditions change.
Model answer: The persistence of Indian and Chinese diaspora communities directly challenges the “melting pot” model of immigration, which suggests that over generations immigrants assimilate into a single national culture. In Trinidad, Mauritius, Fiji, and South Africa, Indian communities maintained South Asian religious practices (Hinduism, Islam), languages, food traditions, and family structures for 150+ years after their arrival as indentured workers. Chinese communities in Singapore, Malaysia, and San Francisco maintained Chinese-language education, community associations, and cultural practices across many generations. The melting pot model was always more ideological than descriptive. It described what American nativists wanted to happen, not what actually happened. Diaspora communities demonstrate that cultural identity is resilient and that adaptation to a new society is compatible with preserving a distinct cultural heritage.

Women who managed household finances and made economic decisions independently were exercising forms of agency that patriarchal norms had previously denied them. Managing a farm or a household budget develops real skills and, over time, can change how women see themselves and how communities view women’s capacities. On the other hand, taking on male labor responsibilities without any reduction in existing domestic responsibilities, childcare, food preparation, domestic maintenance, often meant crushing workloads. And the financial precariousness of depending on irregular remittances created anxiety rather than freedom. Historical analyses of specific sending communities, southern Italian villages, South Asian rural communities, Mexican peasant towns, find exactly this mixed picture: some women thrived in expanded roles; many others were simply overwhelmed. The same structural change had different impacts depending on economic circumstances, community support, and individual personality.
