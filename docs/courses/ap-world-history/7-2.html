7.2 Causes of World War I

🏛️ Political⚔️ Militarism🌍 Imperialism👥 Nationalism
  • LO-A: MAIN: the four underlying causes: Militarism (arms race, especially Germany vs. Britain in naval buildup), Alliances (Triple Alliance vs. Triple Entente turned a regional conflict into a world war), Imperialism (colonial competition created tensions and rivalries), Nationalism (especially in the Balkans, where Slavic nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary). These were the deep causes that made the powder keg; the assassination was the spark. The spark: Sarajevo, June 28, 1914: Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, was assassinated by Gavrilo Princip, a Bosnian Serb nationalist, in Sarajevo. Austria-Hungary blamed Serbia and issued an ultimatum; Serbia refused key terms; Austria-Hungary declared war. The alliance system then dragged in Germany, Russia, France, and Britain within five weeks. A regional Balkan dispute became WWI. The alliance system was the mechanism of escalation: The alliance system didn't cause the war, but it ensured a regional conflict would become a world war. When Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia, Russia mobilized (to protect Serbia). Germany declared war on Russia, then France (Russia's ally). Germany invaded Belgium; Britain declared war on Germany. The interlocking alliances converted a bilateral dispute into a continental war in about five weeks. Imperialist competition: the deeper root: The CED explicitly links imperialist expansion and competition for resources to WWI's causes. European powers had spent decades competing for colonies, resources, and markets; this competition created tensions, arms races, and strategic rivalries that made diplomacy fragile. The war was partly a product of imperial competition boiling over after decades of managed tension.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Historians distinguish between the "long-term causes" and the "immediate trigger" of WWI. Why is this distinction important for understanding the war?
The distinction matters because the immediate trigger (the assassination of Franz Ferdinand) doesn't explain why a single political assassination produced a four-year global war involving 30+ countries. For that, you need the long-term causes: the alliance system that locked nations into mutual defense commitments, the arms race that had militarized European foreign policy, the imperialist competition that had created decades of tensions, and the nationalist movements that made compromise difficult. The trigger lit the fuse, but the underlying causes built the bomb. Understanding both levels of causation helps you see that WWI was neither inevitable nor purely accidental. It was the product of specific structural conditions that made a small crisis catastrophic.
2. The alliance system that existed in 1914 was supposed to preserve peace through deterrence, the idea that any aggressor would face multiple enemies simultaneously. Why did deterrence fail?
Deterrence failed because it assumed decision-makers would be rational calculators who would back down when facing coalition opposition. Instead, several things went wrong: (1) Austria-Hungary decided the risk of not acting (losing prestige, encouraging further Serbian nationalism) was greater than the risk of war; (2) Germany gave Austria-Hungary a "blank check" of unconditional support, removing the normal restraint of alliance consultation; (3) military planning (especially the German Schlieffen Plan, which required attacking France immediately if war with Russia began) left little room for diplomatic delay once mobilization began; (4) nationalist pressures in Russia made backing down from supporting Serbia politically impossible. The alliances also had a "use it or lose it" quality, once one side began mobilizing, the other felt compelled to mobilize immediately or risk being caught unprepared.
3. The AP CED identifies "imperialist expansion and competition for resources" as a cause of WWI. How does imperial competition connect to a war fought mostly in Europe?
Imperial competition shaped European politics in several ways that contributed to war. First, competition for colonies created direct diplomatic crises, the Morocco Crises (1905, 1911) brought France and Germany to the brink of war over colonial control of North Africa. Second, the arms race (especially the Anglo-German naval rivalry) was itself driven partly by the logic that great powers needed large navies to protect global empires. Third, the Balkans crisis that triggered WWI was itself partly a product of imperial competition: Austria-Hungary and Russia were competing for influence in the Balkans as the Ottoman Empire withdrew; Serbian nationalism threatened Austria-Hungary's own multi-ethnic empire; and Britain's alliance with Russia connected Balkan politics to global imperial strategy. The war was "fought in Europe" but emerged from a global political system shaped by decades of imperial competition.
4. Why was nationalism in the Balkans particularly dangerous for the existing European order in 1914?
The Balkans were ruled by a combination of the declining Ottoman Empire and the Austro-Hungarian Empire, both multi-ethnic, multi-lingual states in an age when nationalism demanded that each ethnic group have its own state. Slavic nationalism (the movement for unified South Slavic, later "Yugoslav", states) was particularly threatening to Austria-Hungary, which contained millions of Slavic subjects (Czechs, Slovaks, Slovenes, Croats, Serbs). If Serbian nationalism succeeded in uniting South Slavic peoples, it would pull apart Austria-Hungary from within. Austria-Hungary therefore saw Serbia's success as an existential threat. Russia, positioning itself as the protector of Slavic peoples, backed Serbia. This combination, Serbian nationalism, Austro-Hungarian existential fear, and Russian patronage, created a tripwire that, once tripped by the Sarajevo assassination, set off the alliance chain reaction.
5. Some historians argue WWI was "inevitable" given the alliance system and imperial tensions; others argue it was a tragic accident produced by miscalculations in summer 1914. Which view is more convincing?
The stronger argument acknowledges both structural conditions AND contingent decisions. WWI was not inevitable in the sense that the specific war beginning in August 1914 required specific choices. Germany's blank check to Austria-Hungary, Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia, Russia's decision to mobilize, Germany's Schlieffen Plan logic. Different decisions could have limited the conflict. But "inevitable" captures the structural reality that the underlying tensions (alliance competition, arms race, imperial rivalry, Balkan nationalism) made some major conflict likely, the powder keg was built; whether it would explode required only a spark. Most historians today take a middle position: the war was not inevitable but was the product of structural conditions that made it very probable, combined with specific decision-making failures under crisis pressure that made it impossible to reverse once begun. For AP purposes, know both levels: the underlying structural causes AND the specific events of the "July Crisis" that escalated the assassination into world war.

The Reading

🌍 A Continent Armed and Angry: Europe in 1914

By 1914, Europe was a powder keg. Decades of industrialization had produced military technology of unprecedented destructive power. Imperial competition had created tensions among the great powers over colonies, markets, and strategic position. The alliance system had divided Europe into two armed camps. And nationalism, the intense belief that ethnic groups deserved their own states and that national honor must be defended, had raised the emotional stakes of every diplomatic dispute.

Historians use the acronym MAIN to organize the four underlying causes of World War I: Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, and Nationalism. The AP CED specifically requires you to know all four. These were the structural conditions that made a small spark catastrophic, the "long fuse" that turned a political assassination into the deadliest war in human history up to that point.

💡 Fun Fact
Britain and Germany were in the middle of a massive naval arms race when WWI began. Between 1906 and 1914, Britain launched 29 large battleships (called "dreadnoughts"); Germany launched 17. The race cost both countries enormous sums and convinced each that the other was preparing for war. When the war actually came, the anticipated giant naval battle never materialized, the two fleets met only once (Battle of Jutland, 1916), and the result was inconclusive. The arms race helped cause the war, then failed to deliver the decisive victory it had promised.

⚔️ Militarism: Arms Races and War Culture

Militarism refers to two related phenomena: the buildup of military forces and the cultural glorification of military power. By 1914, European great powers were spending unprecedented fractions of their national income on armies and navies. Germany's army had doubled in size since 1870. Britain and Germany were locked in a naval arms race. France, after its humiliating defeat in the Franco-Prussian War (1871), had rebuilt its army with revenge against Germany as an explicit goal.

The culture of militarism was equally important. Military officers held high social prestige across Europe. War was widely seen, at least until the slaughter of the trenches proved otherwise, as noble, glorious, and a test of national character. This cultural glorification of military power meant that when crisis came, military solutions were seriously considered rather than dismissed. The arms buildup also created a "use it or lose it" strategic logic: military plans were designed around rapid mobilization, and once mobilization began, it was technically difficult to reverse without leaving the mobilizing state vulnerable.

🤝 Alliances: The Mechanism of Escalation

The alliance system was supposed to preserve peace through deterrence: any power that attacked another would face a coalition response. Instead, the alliances converted a regional dispute into a world war. By 1914, Europe was divided into two armed blocs:

  • Triple Alliance: Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy (Italy stayed neutral in 1914, then switched sides in 1915)
  • Triple Entente: France, Russia, and Britain

When the assassination of Franz Ferdinand triggered an Austro-Serbian dispute, the alliances activated like dominoes. Austria-Hungary declared war on Serbia → Russia mobilized to defend Serbia → Germany declared war on Russia → Germany declared war on France (Russia's ally) → Germany invaded Belgium to attack France → Britain declared war on Germany (Britain had guaranteed Belgian neutrality in 1839). A bilateral dispute became a continental war in five weeks.

📝 AP Tip
The AP exam often uses a stimulus (a cartoon, a quote, or a table) to ask about the causes of WWI. For the alliance-system questions, know that alliances were the mechanism of escalation. They didn't cause the underlying tensions, but they ensured that any conflict between two powers would drag in others. Distinguish between underlying causes (MAIN) and the immediate trigger (assassination) and the mechanism of escalation (alliance system).

🌐 Imperialism: Competition That Created Tension

The AP CED explicitly identifies "imperialist expansion and competition for resources" as a cause of WWI. By 1914, European nations had spent decades competing for colonies across Africa, Asia, and the Pacific. This competition created tensions in two ways.

First, direct colonial disputes: the Moroccan Crises (1905 and 1911) brought France and Germany to the brink of war over who would control Morocco. Germany backed down both times, humiliated, feeding a desire for future confrontation. Second, imperial logic shaped military planning: Britain's global empire required a large navy; Germany wanted a larger navy to compete for global influence; the resulting Anglo-German naval arms race was itself a product of imperial ambition. The Balkans crisis that triggered WWI was also connected to imperialism: Austria-Hungary and Russia were competing for influence in the Balkans as Ottoman power retreated, a classic imperial competition over a weakening power's territory.

🔥 Nationalism: The Fuel That Made Everything Worse

Nationalism, intense pride in one's nation and the belief that national honor must be defended, was the emotional fuel that made the underlying tensions explosive. Nationalism operated on two levels in 1914:

Great-power nationalism made backing down from any confrontation feel like national humiliation. Germany had backed down in the Moroccan Crises; Austria-Hungary had backed down when Russia supported Serbia in earlier Balkan disputes. Nationalist opinion in both countries demanded that the humiliations stop. In 1914, with Germany's "blank check" of unconditional support, Austria-Hungary decided it would not back down again, even at the risk of war.

Slavic nationalism in the Balkans was the most immediate danger. The Balkans had been the site of two wars (1912–13) as Slavic nations broke free from Ottoman rule. Now Pan-Slavism, the movement for South Slavic unity, threatened Austria-Hungary's own multi-ethnic empire, which contained millions of Slavic subjects. Serbia's growing power and nationalist ambition appeared to Austria-Hungary as an existential threat. The assassination of Franz Ferdinand by a Bosnian Serb nationalist confirmed their worst fears.

💥 The Spark: Sarajevo, June 28, 1914

On June 28, 1914, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the Austro-Hungarian throne, was visiting Sarajevo, the capital of Bosnia (a region Austria-Hungary had annexed in 1908, to Serbian outrage). A group of young Bosnian Serb nationalists, members of a secret society called the "Black Hand," had planned to assassinate him. The first attempt failed, a bomb bounced off the archduke's car. But as the motorcade changed routes, the car stalled directly in front of 19-year-old Gavrilo Princip, one of the conspirators. He fired two shots, killing both Franz Ferdinand and his wife Sophie.

Austria-Hungary blamed the Serbian government (incorrectly. Serbia's government hadn't planned the attack, though some Serbian military officers were involved) and issued an ultimatum so harsh it was designed to be rejected. Serbia accepted almost all the terms but balked at one: allowing Austrian officials to conduct investigations on Serbian soil, a direct violation of Serbian sovereignty. Austria-Hungary declared war. The July Crisis had begun.

💡 Fun Fact
The June 28 assassination almost didn't happen. The first attempt, a conspirator threw a bomb at Franz Ferdinand's car, failed when the archduke's driver sped up and the bomb bounced off, exploding under the following car and injuring several people. Franz Ferdinand attended his scheduled events, then changed his route to visit the wounded at the hospital. The new route brought his car directly past the café where Gavrilo Princip was eating a sandwich, dejected that the first attempt had failed. The driver, confused by the route change, stopped the car right in front of Princip to ask for directions. WWI's immediate trigger depended on a wrong turn.

The Schlieffen Plan's routing through Belgium was thus the mechanism by which Germany's war with France brought in the British Empire, adding Britain's global naval and colonial resources to the Allied side.
Explain how TWO of the four underlying causes of World War I (Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism) contributed to the outbreak of the war in 1914.
Militarism: The Anglo-German naval arms race created mutual suspicion and a "use it or lose it" military culture across Europe. Germany's military planning (the Schlieffen Plan) was designed for a two-front war and required immediate attack if mobilization began, leaving little room for diplomacy once a crisis erupted. Military solutions were taken seriously because military power had been built up at enormous cost. Nationalism: Pan-Slavic nationalism in the Balkans directly triggered WWI by making Austria-Hungary view Serbian success as an existential threat to its multi-ethnic empire. The Black Hand conspirators who planned Franz Ferdinand's assassination were motivated by South Slavic nationalism. Simultaneously, great-power nationalism in Germany and Austria-Hungary made backing down from previous Balkan humiliations politically unacceptable, reducing willingness to seek diplomatic compromise.
Evaluate the relative importance of underlying structural causes versus the immediate trigger in explaining the outbreak of World War I in 1914.
Although the assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand provided the immediate trigger, the underlying structural causes of World War I, especially the alliance system that converted regional disputes into continental conflicts and the imperialist competition that had created decades of great-power tension, were more fundamentally responsible, since different structural conditions would have allowed the assassination to remain a minor political event rather than a world war.
Model answer: The claim is partly true: no major power in 1914 wanted a four-year war of attrition that would kill 20 million people and destroy their economies. But "no one wanted a world war" doesn't mean no one wanted war at all. Austria-Hungary wanted a limited war against Serbia to punish Serbian nationalism. Germany was willing to risk a bigger war to support Austria-Hungary and to break out of the encirclement it felt the Triple Entente represented. Russia was not willing to back down from defending Serbia again. Each power wanted the outcome they wanted (Austrian punishment of Serbia; Russian prestige; German security) without triggering a world war, and each miscalculated what the others would do. The alliances, the military mobilization plans, and the compressed diplomatic timetable meant there was no mechanism to stop the escalation once it began. World war was not wanted but was produced by a system that made it extremely difficult to avoid once a certain point of escalation was reached.
Model answer: Imperial competition shaped European politics in ways that produced the tensions behind WWI without the war itself necessarily being "about" colonies. Imperial competition meant that European great powers saw themselves as competitors in a global struggle for resources, markets, and prestige. This competitive framework made diplomatic disputes harder to resolve, because each concession felt like a step backward in the global competition. The arms race (especially the Anglo-German naval buildup) was itself driven by imperial logic. You needed a powerful navy to protect and expand a global empire. Colonial disputes (Morocco) nearly caused war multiple times before 1914. When the Balkan crisis came in 1914, it landed in a political environment shaped by decades of imperial competition, which is why nationalists and military planners were more willing to risk war than to accept humiliation. The war was the product of an imperial political system, even if its immediate cause was a Balkan assassination.
Model answer: The blank check reveals several things about German thinking. First, Germany overestimated its ability to control escalation. German leaders hoped for a "localized" Austro-Serbian war that wouldn't trigger Russian intervention. They miscalculated Russia's willingness to back down again after previous humiliations. Second, it reflects German willingness to risk a bigger war: some German military leaders, especially Chief of Staff Helmuth von Moltke, believed that Germany was in a "now or never" moment. Russia was rapidly industrializing and would be too powerful to defeat in a few years, so if war was coming anyway, 1914 was the best time. Third, it reveals the failure of the alliance system as a peace-keeping mechanism: rather than restraining Austria-Hungary, Germany actively encouraged its most aggressive option. The blank check was arguably Germany's single most consequential decision in the July Crisis, without it, Austria-Hungary might have been more cautious.
Model answer: The MAIN framework is a useful organizing tool but does oversimplify in important ways. It omits: (1) the role of specific leaders and their decisions, the July Crisis involved specific choices by specific people (Kaiser Wilhelm II's blank check, Tsar Nicholas II's mobilization order, Austrian foreign minister Berchtold's ultimatum design) that weren't predetermined by structural causes; (2) the internal political pressures that constrained decision-makers, in Russia, liberal nationalists had been embarrassed by previous Balkan retreats and demanded a firmer response; in Germany, conservatives feared domestic socialist opposition and saw war as a way to unite the country; (3) the role of military planning, the Schlieffen Plan's built-in logic meant that once war with Russia began, attacking France through Belgium was almost automatic, bringing in Britain and transforming the war's character; (4) the role of misperception, each power's estimates of what others would do were wrong. The MAIN framework describes the structural conditions; explaining how those conditions produced the specific war of 1914 requires attention to the specific decisions and contingencies of the July Crisis.
Model answer: The Balkans mattered not for their intrinsic economic value but for what they represented in the imperial competition and for what the stakes meant to each power. For Austria-Hungary, the Balkans were existential: Serbian nationalism threatened to pull apart its own multi-ethnic empire by encouraging its Slavic subjects to demand independence or unification with Serbia. Losing influence in the Balkans meant losing the empire itself. For Russia, the Balkans represented two things: (1) prestige as "protector of Slavic peoples" (Pan-Slavism), and (2) strategic access to the Turkish Straits and the Mediterranean, crucial for Russian trade and naval power. For Germany, supporting Austria-Hungary meant fulfilling its primary alliance obligation and maintaining its only reliable great-power ally. For France and Britain, war with Germany activated their Entente commitments. The Balkans were the trigger point precisely because they intersected with the most sensitive interests of multiple great powers simultaneously. It was the place where the powder keg of European politics was most likely to explode.
