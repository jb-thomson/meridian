9.8 Institutions Developing in a Globalized World

🏛️ Governance🌐 International Organizations🕊️ Peacekeeping🤝 Cooperation
  • LO-A: The United Nations was founded to prevent another world war: After WWII's devastation, world leaders created the United Nations (1945) with the stated goal of maintaining international peace and facilitating cooperation. The catastrophe of two world wars in 30 years convinced leaders that some form of international governance was necessary. The UN's structure reflected Cold War power realities: The five permanent members of the Security Council (US, USSR, UK, France, China) each held veto power, a compromise ensuring great-power participation while limiting the UN's ability to act when those powers disagreed. The veto effectively paralyzed the Security Council during the Cold War. The UN expanded far beyond peacekeeping: The UN system grew to include specialized agencies addressing health (WHO), children (UNICEF), refugees (UNHCR), food (FAO), culture (UNESCO), and labor (ILO). These agencies addressed global problems that no single nation could solve, making the UN an essential infrastructure of the modern world even when its political bodies were paralyzed. Global institutions reflected but also challenged the state system: International organizations constrained national sovereignty, nations that joined agreed to follow international rules. They also reflected the existing distribution of power (wealthy nations had more influence). But they created forums where all nations, however small, had a voice, representing a genuinely new form of global governance.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Why was the United Nations founded in 1945, and how was it different from the League of Nations?
The UN was founded in the immediate aftermath of WWII, which had killed an estimated 70–85 million people, the most destructive war in human history, coming only 25 years after WWI. World leaders were determined to create an international institution that could prevent another such catastrophe. The key differences from the League of Nations (1919): (1) The US joined, the League had been fatally weakened by the US Senate's refusal to ratify it, leaving it without its most powerful potential member. (2) The UN included the USSR and China, the League had excluded the Soviet Union (until 1934) and the Republic of China. (3) The UN had the Security Council with enforcement powers (the ability to authorize military action), not just the League's Assembly-based structure. (4) The UN's permanent Security Council structure explicitly accommodated great-power politics, the P5 veto ensured that major powers wouldn't be bound by decisions they opposed. This was both the UN's great strength (ensuring great-power participation) and its great weakness (ensuring that great-power conflicts couldn't be resolved by the Security Council).
2. How did the Security Council veto affect the UN's ability to maintain peace during the Cold War?
The veto dramatically limited the Security Council's effectiveness during the Cold War. The five permanent members (US, USSR, UK, France, China) each had the right to veto any Security Council resolution. During the Cold War, the US and USSR were on opposite sides of most conflicts, whenever one side wanted the Security Council to act on a crisis, the other would veto. The Korean War (1950–53) was an exception: the USSR was boycotting the Security Council over the Taiwan-mainland China issue when North Korea invaded, allowing UN forces to be authorized. But this was unique. The Suez Crisis (1956), the Vietnam War, the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan (1979), and numerous Cold War conflicts were not addressed by the Security Council because one or both superpowers vetoed action. The UN worked better through the General Assembly (where all nations voted equally but resolutions were non-binding) and its specialized agencies (WHO, UNICEF, etc.) than through the Security Council during the Cold War era.
3. How did UN specialized agencies like WHO and UNICEF address global problems that individual nations couldn't solve alone?
The World Health Organization (WHO), founded 1948, coordinated global responses to infectious disease, most famously organizing the global vaccination campaign that eradicated smallpox by 1980. No single nation could have achieved this; it required coordinating vaccine distribution, surveillance, and containment across every country on Earth. WHO also coordinates responses to epidemic outbreaks (Ebola, COVID-19) by collecting and sharing data globally, setting standards, and mobilizing resources. UNICEF (UN Children's Fund) coordinates humanitarian assistance to children in conflict zones and disasters where no individual nation's government could operate, providing food, vaccines, clean water, and educational support across dozens of countries simultaneously. These agencies work because global problems, disease spread, refugee crises, food insecurity, do not respect national borders. They require the kind of sustained international coordination that only permanent multilateral institutions can provide, even when the political Security Council is paralyzed by great-power rivalry.

The Reading

🌍 A New World Order: The United Nations (KC-6.3.II.A)

After the catastrophe of two world wars in 30 years, which together killed an estimated 100 million people, world leaders were determined to create an international institution that could prevent another such disaster. The AP CED identifies this development precisely: "New international organizations, including the United Nations, formed with the stated goal of maintaining world peace and facilitating international cooperation."

The United Nations was formally established on October 24, 1945, when its charter was ratified by 51 founding member nations. The word "United Nations" had been used by Franklin Roosevelt to describe the Allied coalition fighting the Axis powers; now it described a permanent international organization. The UN's founding represented both a continuation of earlier attempts at international governance, notably the League of Nations (1919), and a significant departure from them. Most crucially, the United States joined. The League had been fatally undermined by the US Senate's refusal to ratify it, leaving the world's most powerful nation outside the organization designed to keep the peace. The UN's founders were determined not to repeat this mistake, designing the Security Council's permanent membership and veto system specifically to ensure great-power buy-in.

🏛️ Structure: How the UN Works

The UN's architecture balanced universality with great-power realism. It had two major deliberative bodies:

The General Assembly includes all member states (193 as of the 21st century), with each nation having one vote. The General Assembly debates and passes resolutions on any matter within the UN's scope, but its resolutions are non-binding. They carry moral authority without legal force. The General Assembly gave small and developing nations a formal global voice; decolonization-era nations used it extensively to press for independence, economic equity, and disarmament. It passed the Universal Declaration of Human Rights in 1948 and created the UN Environment Programme and numerous other bodies.

The Security Council has primary responsibility for international peace and security and can take binding decisions, including authorizing the use of military force. It has 15 members: 10 rotating members elected by the General Assembly for two-year terms, and 5 permanent members (the P5): the United States, the United Kingdom, France, the Soviet Union (now Russia), and China. Each permanent member holds the right to veto any substantive resolution, meaning a single P5 member can block any Security Council action, regardless of how many other members support it. This veto was a deliberate compromise: it ensured that the great powers would join the UN (unlike the League of Nations) but at the cost of making the Security Council ineffective whenever great powers disagreed.

💡 Fun Fact
The UN headquarters in New York City occupies land that was previously a slaughterhouse district. John D. Rockefeller Jr. donated $8.5 million to purchase the East River site after the city of Philadelphia also bid to host the headquarters. The building, completed in 1952, features the distinctive glass-curtain-wall Secretariat skyscraper designed by an international team of architects including Le Corbusier (France), Oscar Niemeyer (Brazil), and Sven Markelius (Sweden). The headquarters sits on international territory, technically not part of the United States, a physical embodiment of the international status the UN was meant to represent.

❄️ The UN During the Cold War: Accomplishments and Limitations

The Cold War immediately tested the UN's capacity. The US-Soviet rivalry meant that the Security Council was frequently paralyzed by vetoes: whenever one superpower wanted action, the other blocked it. The Soviet Union vetoed 100 Security Council resolutions in the UN's first decade alone. Major Cold War conflicts, the Vietnam War, the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, US interventions in Latin America, were not resolved by the Security Council.

There were exceptions. The Korean War (1950–53) was authorized under the UN flag, but only because the USSR was boycotting the Security Council in protest over China's representation at the time. UN peacekeeping operations, "Blue Helmets", were deployed to conflict zones including the Middle East, Cyprus, Congo, and eventually Bosnia and Rwanda, though their effectiveness was limited by the rules under which they operated (they could observe and monitor, but not actively fight unless fired upon).

The UN was more effective through its specialized agencies. The World Health Organization (WHO) coordinated the global vaccination campaign that eradicated smallpox by 1980, perhaps the greatest public health achievement in history, and coordinated responses to polio, malaria, and later HIV/AIDS and COVID-19. UNICEF delivered humanitarian assistance to children in conflict zones worldwide. The UN High Commissioner for Refugees (UNHCR) protected tens of millions of refugees. UNESCO promoted education, science, and cultural preservation. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) addressed food security globally. These agencies operated relatively independently of great-power politics, making them more consistently effective than the Security Council.

🌐 The Post-Cold War UN: Expanded Role, New Challenges

The end of the Cold War in 1991 briefly appeared to unlock the Security Council's potential. With US-Russian cooperation suddenly possible, the Security Council authorized the Gulf War (1990–91) against Iraq, UN peacekeeping expanded dramatically in the early 1990s, and Secretary-General Boutros-Ghali announced an ambitious "Agenda for Peace." But new challenges quickly revealed continuing limitations.

The Rwandan genocide of 1994, in which 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu were killed in 100 days while UN peacekeepers stood by, instructed not to intervene, revealed the devastating gap between the UN's stated goals and its actual capacity and political will. The Security Council refused to authorize effective intervention despite clear evidence of genocide. The Srebrenica massacre (1995), in which UN-protected Bosnian Muslim men and boys were slaughtered by Serbian forces, further damaged UN credibility. These failures produced the doctrine of the "Responsibility to Protect" (R2P), the principle that the international community had a responsibility to intervene when a state committed atrocities against its own people, but implementation remained contested.

The 21st century brought new challenges: terrorism (September 11, 2001), the US invasion of Iraq (2003) without Security Council authorization, climate change (requiring international coordination the Security Council couldn't provide), and great-power rivalry between the US, China, and Russia that once again paralyzed the Security Council on major conflicts. The UN remained an essential forum and its specialized agencies continued vital work, but its founding promise of maintained world peace proved aspirational rather than achieved.

💡 Fun Fact
The UN has 6 official languages: Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian, and Spanish. All official documents are translated into all six languages. The UN employs thousands of professional interpreters and translators. During Security Council sessions, simultaneous interpretation is provided in all six languages, delegates speak in their own language and hear through headsets in their language of choice. This multilingual infrastructure is a physical embodiment of the UN's aspiration to represent all of humanity, and a constant reminder of the enormous logistical challenge of global governance.

🔗 The UN in Context: Part of a Broader Institutional Order

The United Nations was the central institution of a broader post-WWII international order that included multiple interconnected organizations. The Bretton Woods institutions, the IMF and World Bank (both discussed in 9.7), were created at the same 1944 conference that designed the postwar economic order. The General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), eventually replaced by the WTO (1995), governed global trade. The NATO alliance (1949) provided collective security for Western nations. The European integration project, from the European Coal and Steel Community (1951) to the European Union (1993), created the most deeply integrated regional institutional framework in history. Together, these institutions represented an unprecedented attempt to govern key aspects of international life through rules-based multilateral frameworks rather than purely through great-power competition.

These institutions had critics from left and right: from those who thought they served wealthy-nation interests (the anti-IMF activists of 9.7), those who thought they infringed on national sovereignty, and those who thought they were too weak to address genuine global challenges. But their existence and persistence, despite these critiques, represented a genuine transformation in how international relations operated compared to the pre-WWII world. The question of whether international institutions could become strong enough to address the major challenges of the 21st century, climate change, nuclear proliferation, pandemics, inequality, remained one of the defining questions of the era.

No previous international institution had ever achieved anything comparable: the complete elimination of a disease that had killed hundreds of millions throughout human history required coordinating vaccination programs in every country on Earth, including across Cold War battle lines. This was only possible through a permanent international organization with the technical capacity, legitimacy, and sustained funding to operate globally over decades. The League of Nations, in comparison, had no specialized health agency with comparable capacity or authority. B. The Rwandan genocide of 1994, in which approximately 800,000 Tutsi and moderate Hutu were killed in 100 days while UN peacekeepers stood by, demonstrates the gap between the UN's founding goals and its actual performance. Despite clear evidence of a genocide in progress and UN peacekeepers on the ground, the Security Council refused to authorize effective intervention, partly due to political calculations by major powers, partly due to the precedent-setting risk of humanitarian intervention. The UN's own secretary-general later acknowledged this as a failure. The founding goal of "maintaining world peace" was not achieved in one of the most complete failures of international governance in the late 20th century. C. Evaluate the extent to which the United Nations and other international organizations changed international interactions among states in the period 1900 to the present.
International organizations, most notably the United Nations, significantly changed international interactions after 1945 by creating permanent multilateral forums for diplomacy, developing international law and human rights frameworks, and addressing global problems through specialized agencies; however, these organizations consistently fell short of their founding goals of maintaining peace, as national sovereignty, great-power vetoes, and inadequate political will limited their ability to prevent or stop major conflicts and atrocities.
Model answer: The founders included the veto because they had learned from the League of Nations' failure: the US Senate refused to ratify the League, leaving the world's most powerful nation outside the organization. The founders reasoned that great powers would only join and stay in an international organization if they retained the ability to block actions that threatened their vital interests. The veto was the price of great-power participation. The consequences were significant and double-edged. Positive: all major powers joined and remained in the UN throughout the Cold War, maintaining at least a forum for dialogue even during extreme tension. The UN continued to function as a diplomatic meeting place and coordination mechanism even when the Security Council was paralyzed. Negative: the veto made the Security Council nearly useless for resolving Cold War conflicts where superpowers disagreed. Any US-Soviet or US-Chinese or Russia-Western conflict could not be resolved by the Security Council because one side would veto. Major atrocities (Rwanda 1994, Srebrenica 1995) went unaddressed because action was blocked. The veto prioritized great-power participation over effective collective security, a reasonable trade-off in 1945, but with devastating consequences in specific crises.
Model answer: The UN specialized agencies, WHO, UNICEF, UNHCR, UNESCO, FAO, were consistently more effective than the Security Council for several reasons. First, their missions were technical rather than political, coordinating vaccination campaigns, delivering humanitarian aid, setting food safety standards. They didn't threaten great-power interests in the way that Security Council peacekeeping or sanctions decisions might. A US-Soviet veto dynamic that paralyzed Security Council action on conflicts didn't apply to WHO coordinating a global smallpox eradication campaign that both superpowers supported. Second, specialized agencies operated on consensus and technical expertise rather than geopolitical bargaining, which made their work less subject to great-power manipulation. Third, their work was cumulative, each successful vaccination campaign built on previous ones; each refugee crisis response built institutional capacity. The eradication of smallpox (1980) stands as perhaps the greatest success in UN history precisely because it was a technical achievement requiring genuine global cooperation, not a geopolitical dispute requiring great-power consensus.
Model answer: Change: The UN represented a qualitatively new form of international governance. Before WWII, international relations operated primarily through bilateral diplomacy and great-power competition, with occasional multilateral conferences (Congress of Vienna 1815, Hague Conferences 1899 and 1907) but no permanent international institutions with broad membership and ongoing governance functions. The UN created permanent multilateral forums where all nations, great powers and small, could participate in global governance. Its Security Council could authorize binding international military action; its General Assembly gave every nation a formal voice; its specialized agencies addressed genuinely global problems. This was unprecedented. Continuity: The UN did not eliminate great-power competition or national sovereignty. It worked around them. The P5 veto embedded great-power privilege into the UN's structure. Nations continued to pursue national interests and use the UN as an instrument of great-power competition (Cold War vote-gathering in the General Assembly; veto-trading in the Security Council). The fundamental anarchic character of international relations, no supreme authority over sovereign states, persisted within the UN framework. The organization moderated and institutionalized great-power competition without eliminating it.
Model answer: The UN's actual peacekeeping record is mixed, significant in some contexts, catastrophic in others. No WWIII occurred between the Cold War superpowers, whether because of the UN, nuclear deterrence, or other factors is debated. The UN successfully eradicated smallpox, delivered humanitarian aid globally, and protected millions of refugees. But the Korean War, Vietnam War, numerous Cold War proxy conflicts, and major post-Cold War atrocities (Rwanda, Srebrenica, Syria) demonstrated severe limitations. Several factors limited effectiveness: (1) The P5 veto prevented Security Council action whenever great powers disagreed. (2) The UN had no independent military force. It depended on member states to contribute peacekeepers, who had limited mandates and often insufficient training, equipment, and will to act decisively. (3) Sovereignty norms prevented intervention in internal conflicts until the late 20th century. (4) The UN reflected the world's power distribution, rich nations dominated its funding and leadership, shaping its priorities. The gap between the UN's stated goals and actual achievements reflects the difficulty of building effective international governance in a world of sovereign states with competing interests, a challenge that remained as relevant in the 21st century as in 1945.
