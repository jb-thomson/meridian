AP World History · Unit 8: Cold War and Decolonization

8.9 Causation in the Age of the Cold War and Decolonization

🧠 Argumentation🌎 Hemispheric Comparison⚖️ Causation🔄 Synthesis
  • LO-A: Topic 8.9 is a synthesis and argumentation topic — Like all final unit topics (4.8, 5.10, 6.8, 7.9), this is designed to develop complex argumentation using evidence from the whole unit. The LO asks you to assess the EXTENT to which Cold War effects were similar in both hemispheres. The LO asks for comparison across hemispheres — LO K: "Explain the extent to which the effects of the Cold War were similar in the Eastern and Western Hemispheres." Think: What was common worldwide? What was different between, say, Eastern Europe vs. Latin America vs. Southeast Asia? Key global similarity: proxy wars everywhere — Eastern hemisphere (Korea, Vietnam, Angola, Afghanistan) and Western hemisphere (Nicaragua, Cuba, Chile) all experienced Cold War proxy conflicts. The pattern of superpower intervention in local conflicts was hemispheric-crossing. Key difference: decolonization primarily in Eastern hemisphere — The breakdown of European empires was mainly an African/Asian phenomenon (Eastern hemisphere). The Western hemisphere had mostly already decolonized in the 19th century, so the Cold War played out against different historical backgrounds on each side.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. In what ways were the effects of the Cold War similar in both the Eastern and Western Hemispheres?
Key similarities: (1) Proxy wars — both hemispheres experienced Cold War-funded and -armed proxy conflicts: Korea and Vietnam in the Eastern hemisphere; Cuba, Nicaragua, and various Latin American conflicts in the Western hemisphere. (2) Superpower economic pressure — both hemispheres experienced US and Soviet economic instruments designed to win allegiance or punish disloyalty: US aid programs and Soviet COMECON worked on both sides of the globe. (3) State-led development — newly independent or developing nations in both hemispheres pursued state-directed economic development, often pitting nationalist economic policies against superpower preferences. (4) Civil society impacts — in both hemispheres, Cold War competition shaped domestic politics, strengthening anti-communist repression in the Western hemisphere (Pinochet, Batista) and constraining reform in communist Eastern hemisphere countries. (5) Nuclear anxiety — populations in both hemispheres lived under the threat of nuclear war.
2. In what ways were the effects of the Cold War DIFFERENT between the Eastern and Western Hemispheres?
Key differences: (1) Decolonization — the Eastern hemisphere (Africa, Asia, Middle East) experienced decolonization simultaneously with the Cold War; these two processes interacted. The Western hemisphere had largely decolonized in the 19th century, so the Cold War there played out through already-independent states, often involving support for or opposition to existing governments rather than independence movements. (2) Direct superpower presence — the USSR directly controlled Eastern Europe through the Warsaw Pact and stationed troops there; the US had military bases across the Eastern hemisphere (Japan, South Korea, Philippines, Germany) and in the Western hemisphere (Guantánamo), but direct Soviet presence in the Western hemisphere was limited primarily to Cuba. (3) Communist revolution — communist takeovers occurred primarily in the Eastern hemisphere (China, Vietnam, Korea, Cuba being the Western hemisphere exception); armed communist revolution succeeded in some Eastern hemisphere countries but largely failed in the Western hemisphere. (4) The Non-Aligned Movement drew predominantly from the Eastern hemisphere's newly independent nations.
3. The CED says the skill for Topic 8.9 is "argumentation" — specifically "corroborating, qualifying, or modifying an argument using diverse and alternative evidence." What does this look like on the AP exam?
On the AP exam, developing a "complex argument" means going beyond a simple claim to add nuance. For Topic 8.9, this means: (1) Make a clear claim about the extent of similarity. (2) Corroborate it with specific evidence from multiple regions. (3) Qualify or modify it by acknowledging where the evidence doesn't fit — where Eastern and Western hemisphere experiences diverged. For example: "Cold War proxy wars affected both hemispheres similarly [evidence: Korea, Vietnam, Nicaragua, Angola]. However, the interaction of Cold War competition with decolonization was distinctly an Eastern hemisphere phenomenon, making the Cold War's effects more complex in Asia and Africa than in the already-independent Western hemisphere nations where the Cold War primarily reinforced existing state structures rather than creating new ones [evidence: contrast between India/Indonesia's independence movements vs. Chile/Argentina where Cold War shaped already-independent governments]." The qualification deepens the argument and earns the "complexity" point on the AP rubric.

Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: A conflict in which two major powers (US and USSR) support opposing sides in a regional war rather than fighting each other directly. Significance: Proxy wars appeared in both hemispheres: Korea, Vietnam, Angola, and Afghanistan (Eastern); Cuba, Nicaragua, Chile, and various Latin American conflicts (Western). The proxy war pattern was the most consistent Cold War similarity across hemispheres.
  • Definition: The process by which Asian and African colonial territories gained independence from European empires, primarily between 1945 and 1975. Significance: Decolonization was primarily an Eastern hemisphere phenomenon — it occurred simultaneously with the Cold War and the two processes interacted: the US and USSR both sought to win newly independent nations to their side, while anti-colonial movements used Cold War rhetoric to gain superpower support.
  • Definition: A grouping of nations — primarily newly independent Asian and African states — that sought to remain neutral in the Cold War rather than aligning with either the US or USSR. Founded at the Bandung Conference (1955). Significance: The Non-Aligned Movement drew predominantly from Eastern hemisphere nations; this is one reason Cold War dynamics were more complex in the Eastern hemisphere, where new nations had ideological leverage that already-independent Western hemisphere nations lacked.
  • Definition: A region where a major power exercises dominant political, economic, or military control, even if it does not formally govern the territory. Significance: The US exercised a sphere of influence in Latin America (Western hemisphere) and the USSR controlled Eastern Europe directly through the Warsaw Pact (Eastern hemisphere). Both superpowers intervened in their respective spheres to prevent governments from shifting ideological alignment.
  • Definition: The contest between two opposing systems — US-backed capitalism and democracy vs. Soviet-backed communism — for global influence and the allegiance of newly independent nations during the Cold War. Significance: Ideological competition was the one truly global similarity between hemispheres: both the US and USSR used economic aid, military alliances, propaganda, and covert action in both hemispheres to expand their respective ideological systems.
The Reading

🌍🌎 The Central Question: Same Cold War, Different Experiences?

The Cold War was a global phenomenon — its ideological conflict, proxy wars, nuclear shadow, and economic competition touched virtually every corner of the world. But the LO for Topic 8.9 asks a specific and sophisticated question: to what extent were the Cold War's effects similar in the Eastern and Western Hemispheres? This question invites you to use the skill of argumentation — making a claim, supporting it with evidence, and qualifying it where the evidence reveals limits or complexity.

The honest answer is: partly similar, partly different. The similarities are real and significant. So are the differences. A strong AP response will articulate both, using specific evidence from multiple regions, and will make a defensible claim about the overall extent of similarity.

🔗 What Was Similar Across Both Hemispheres?

1. Proxy Wars: The most concrete similarity is the pattern of proxy warfare — local conflicts shaped by US and Soviet intervention on opposing sides. In the Eastern hemisphere: Korean War (1950–53), Angolan Civil War (1975–2002), Soviet-Afghan War (1979–89). In the Western hemisphere: Cuban Revolution and US Bay of Pigs invasion (1961), Sandinista-Contras conflict in Nicaragua (1980s), US support for anti-communist regimes across Latin America. In both hemispheres, superpower competition weaponized local conflicts, prolonged them, and raised their human cost dramatically.

2. Superpower Interference in Internal Affairs: In both hemispheres, neither the US nor the USSR respected the sovereignty of smaller nations when strategic interests were at stake. The US-backed coup in Chile (1973) and CIA operations against leftist governments in Guatemala (1954) and Iran (1953) in the Eastern hemisphere paralleled Soviet military interventions in Hungary (1956), Czechoslovakia (1968), and Afghanistan (1979) in the Eastern hemisphere. Superpower willingness to override local sovereignty was a universal Cold War pattern.

3. State-Led Economic Development: In both hemispheres, governments took strong roles in guiding economic development — often as a direct response to Cold War pressures. Nasser's nationalization in Egypt, Nyerere's ujamaa in Tanzania, and India's mixed economy model (Eastern hemisphere) paralleled nationalist economic policies in Latin America — import substitution industrialization in Mexico, Brazil, and Argentina (Western hemisphere). The idea that independent states should control their own economic destiny, rather than leaving it to foreign investment, was a response to colonial and neocolonial experience across both hemispheres.

4. Nuclear Threat: The nuclear shadow fell over the entire globe. The Cuban Missile Crisis (1962) brought the Western hemisphere closest to nuclear war, but populations across the Eastern hemisphere — in Europe, Asia, and eventually across the developing world — also lived with the knowledge that superpower conflict could destroy civilization. Anti-nuclear movements (Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament in Britain, Japanese Gensuikyō) emerged on multiple continents.

💡 Fun Fact
The Cuban Missile Crisis was the closest the world came to nuclear war — but it almost started in the Eastern hemisphere too. During the Crisis, Soviet nuclear-armed submarines operating near Cuba lost contact with Moscow and believed war had already begun. One submarine, the B-59, prepared to launch a nuclear torpedo. As noted in Topic 8.3, only Soviet officer Vasili Arkhipov's refusal to authorize the launch prevented it. The point: nuclear danger during the Crisis involved submarines that had been deployed from the Soviet Union (Eastern hemisphere) to operate in Western hemisphere waters near Cuba. Nuclear war doesn't respect hemispheric boundaries.

↔️ Where Did Eastern and Western Hemisphere Experiences Diverge?

1. Decolonization: The single most significant difference between the hemispheres was that decolonization — the dissolution of European empires and the creation of dozens of new states — occurred primarily in the Eastern hemisphere. Africa and Asia experienced decolonization simultaneously with Cold War competition, creating a complex interaction: independence movements had to navigate Cold War pressures; newly independent states became arenas for superpower competition; and the Non-Aligned Movement emerged specifically from Eastern hemisphere newly independent nations trying to resist Cold War alignment.

The Western hemisphere had already largely decolonized in the 19th century (most Latin American nations achieved independence from Spain and Portugal between 1810 and 1825). So in the Western hemisphere, the Cold War played out through already-independent states — often reinforcing existing authoritarian governments or destabilizing elected ones — rather than shaping the creation of new states. This difference is fundamental: the Cold War was woven into the very birth of states in Africa and Asia in ways it simply wasn't in Latin America.

2. Communist Revolution: Successful communist revolutions — where communist parties actually seized state power — occurred primarily in the Eastern hemisphere (China, 1949; Vietnam, 1975; Korea's division). In the Western hemisphere, Cuba (1959) is the major exception, and even there, Fidel Castro did not publicly identify as communist until after taking power. US proximity and intervention consistently prevented successful communist takeovers across Latin America (though leftist governments won elections in Chile, Guatemala, and elsewhere before being overthrown). The geography of communist success was asymmetric.

3. Direct Soviet Military Presence: The Soviet Union directly controlled Eastern Europe through the Warsaw Pact, stationing troops in Poland, East Germany, Hungary, and other satellite states. Soviet military power was the visible foundation of communist governments in Eastern Europe. In the Western hemisphere, Soviet military presence was almost entirely absent — Cuba had Soviet missiles for 13 days in 1962, and Soviet advisors were present in Cuba, but the USSR never stationed significant military forces in the Western hemisphere. American military dominance in the Western hemisphere was matched by Soviet military dominance in Eastern Europe — but the Soviet sphere of influence in the Western hemisphere was far more limited.

📝 AP Tip
Topic 8.9's LO asks you to "explain the extent to which" the effects were similar — that "extent to which" phrasing is your signal to make a qualified argument, not an absolute claim. The best AP responses: (1) Acknowledge both the similarities and differences. (2) Make a clear claim about whether they were MORE similar or more different overall. (3) Use specific evidence from BOTH hemispheres. (4) Qualify your argument by acknowledging the strongest counterevidence. The HAPP complexity point goes to responses that explicitly qualify or modify their argument — "while X was similar, Y differed significantly because..."

Decolonization's simultaneous occurrence with the Cold War in the Eastern hemisphere is the most significant difference; Western hemisphere nations had been independent since the 19th century and faced Cold War dynamics differently.
Which of the following provides the strongest evidence that Cold War effects were SIMILAR across both hemispheres?
  • The Non-Aligned Movement, which drew members from both Eastern and Western hemisphere nations
  • The simultaneous timing of decolonization movements in Asia, Africa, and Latin America
  • The pattern of superpower-backed proxy wars occurring in both hemispheres: the Korean War and Soviet-Afghan War in the east, and the Sandinista-Contras conflict and Cuban interventions in the west
  • The adoption of communist economic policies by governments in both hemispheres
Proxy wars are the clearest evidence of similar Cold War effects across hemispheres, as both experienced the pattern of US and Soviet backing of opposing forces in third-country conflicts.
The AP argumentation skill for Topic 8.9 emphasizes "corroborating, qualifying, or modifying an argument using diverse and alternative evidence." A student arguing that "Cold War effects were broadly similar across hemispheres" would best qualify this argument by:
  • Providing more examples of proxy wars in both hemispheres to strengthen the similarity claim
  • Noting that the Cold War ended earlier in the Western hemisphere than in the Eastern hemisphere
  • Arguing that the US was more aggressive in the Western hemisphere than the USSR was in the Eastern hemisphere
  • Acknowledging that while superpower intervention patterns were similar, the Eastern hemisphere's simultaneous decolonization created qualitatively different political contexts that shaped how Cold War competition unfolded
A strong qualification acknowledges the decolonization difference as a genuine limitation on the similarity argument, showing sophisticated engagement with the evidence rather than simply reinforcing the initial claim.

A historian argues that "the Cold War and decolonization were not two separate historical phenomena but one interconnected transformation of the global order." Which of the following Unit 8 developments best supports this argument?
  • The Soviet invasion of Afghanistan, which occurred after most decolonization was complete
  • The Korean War, which divided a single country between communist north and capitalist south
  • Ho Chi Minh's Vietnamese independence movement, which was simultaneously anti-colonial and communist — making it impossible to separate Cold War competition from decolonization in this case
  • The fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989, which occurred as the last wave of African decolonization was completing
Vietnam's independence movement under Ho Chi Minh fused anti-colonial nationalism and communist ideology in a single movement, demonstrating the interconnection of the Cold War and decolonization: it was impossible to understand one without the other in this case.
Use the following argument to answer parts a–c: "The effects of the Cold War were broadly similar in both the Eastern and Western Hemispheres, as both experienced superpower intervention in local conflicts, ideological competition between capitalism and communism, and pressure on governments to align with one bloc or the other."
Sample Answers: a) The Sandinista-Contras conflict in Nicaragua (Western hemisphere) and the Angolan Civil War (Eastern hemisphere) both exemplify the same Cold War dynamic: the US backed anti-communist forces (Contras in Nicaragua, UNITA in Angola) while the USSR and Cuba supported Marxist governments (Sandinistas, MPLA). In both cases, local conflicts were shaped and prolonged by superpower intervention, and local populations bore the human cost of superpower competition. This parallel structure in both hemispheres supports the argument that Cold War effects followed similar patterns globally. b) The argument needs qualification because decolonization — the simultaneous creation of dozens of new states — occurred primarily in the Eastern hemisphere, creating a fundamentally different political context for the Cold War's effects there. In Asia and Africa, the Cold War was intertwined with the birth of new nations: independence movements had to navigate superpower pressure; newly independent states used Cold War competition to extract aid from both sides; and the Non-Aligned Movement emerged specifically from these newly independent Eastern hemisphere nations. This dynamic was absent in Latin America, where nations had been independent since the early 19th century and faced Cold War pressures as established (if sometimes fragile) states rather than newly created ones. The Eastern hemisphere's Cold War was therefore qualitatively more complex than the Western hemisphere's. c) The Suez Crisis (1956) illustrates a direct causal connection between the Cold War and decolonization. When Egypt's Nasser nationalized the Suez Canal — an act of economic decolonization — Britain and France invaded to reassert imperial control. Both the US and USSR, for Cold War strategic reasons (the US to maintain Arab world goodwill, the USSR to support an anti-Western nationalist), pressured Britain and France to withdraw. The outcome — European imperial powers retreating under superpower pressure — directly accelerated decolonization by demonstrating that Cold War constraints prevented European powers from maintaining empire by force, showing how Cold War dynamics causally shaped decolonization's pace and form.
Evaluate the extent to which the effects of the Cold War were similar in both the Eastern and Western Hemispheres in the period from 1945 to 1991.
Model Thesis: Although the Cold War produced broadly similar patterns of superpower intervention, proxy conflict, and ideological competition across both hemispheres, the effects were ultimately more complex in the Eastern hemisphere, where the Cold War interacted with the simultaneous dissolution of European empires — creating dozens of new states that had to navigate decolonization and Cold War alignment simultaneously — while the Western hemisphere, composed of nations independent since the 19th century, experienced the Cold War primarily through superpower-backed coups and proxy forces that reinforced or destabilized existing governments rather than shaping the birth of new nations, making the hemispheres' Cold War experiences similar in pattern but different in historical depth and political consequence.
Model answer: Defensible argument (similarity emphasis): The Cold War's effects were more similar than different across hemispheres, as both experienced the defining patterns of superpower intervention, proxy conflict, economic distortion, and ideological pressure. Evidence: (1) Proxy wars occurred in both hemispheres — Korea and Vietnam in the east, Nicaragua and Cuba in the west; (2) Superpower-backed coups occurred in both — Soviet tanks crushed the Prague Spring (1968) in the east; CIA-backed coups in Iran (1953) and Chile (1973) in east and west respectively; (3) State-led economic development was a response to Cold War-era pressures across both hemispheres — India's mixed economy and Tanzania's ujamaa paralleled import substitution industrialization across Latin America. Qualification: However, the eastern hemisphere's simultaneous decolonization created a distinct layer of complexity — newly independent nations had to navigate Cold War pressures while simultaneously building state institutions, a challenge most Western hemisphere nations had resolved a century earlier. This qualification is important but doesn't outweigh the broader similarities in superpower behavior patterns.
Model answer: The Cold War and decolonization are best understood as one interconnected transformation rather than two separate phenomena. Both were caused by the same root event — WWII's exhaustion of European imperial powers and the rise of two non-European superpowers with anti-colonial rhetoric. Both were shaped by each other: decolonization created new Cold War battlegrounds, while Cold War competition accelerated decolonization by giving independence movements superpower support and by exposing colonial powers' inability to maintain empire without superpower approval (Suez, 1956). Both produced the same outcome: the dismantling of the European imperial world order. Treating them as separate phenomena misses how intertwined they were — Ho Chi Minh's struggle was simultaneously anti-colonial and communist; Nkrumah's non-alignment was a response to both European imperialism and Cold War pressure; the Cuban Missile Crisis was simultaneously a Cold War confrontation and a legacy of US imperialism in the Caribbean. The two phenomena were so intertwined that the CED names the entire unit "Cold War AND Decolonization" — they're a single era, not two parallel developments.
Model answer: The two observations capture the dual nature of Unit 8's era. "Unprecedented worldwide conflicts" explains the Cold War's global reach — proxy wars on every continent, nuclear standoff between superpowers, an arms race that consumed enormous global resources. These conflicts were "unprecedented" in their global scope, ideological intensity, and nuclear danger. The term also encompasses the devastating proxy wars in Korea, Vietnam, Angola, and Nicaragua. "States challenging existing political and social orders" explains both decolonization (newly independent states challenging the European imperial order) and the internal challenges within states — communist revolutions, land reform movements, nonviolent resistance movements, and the political transformations of the 1980s-90s. Both observations together capture the period's essential dynamic: the existing world order — based on European empires and 19th-century power balances — was under challenge from multiple directions simultaneously, and the resolution of that challenge produced the contemporary world of nation-states, US-led international institutions, and post-imperial global economics.
Model answer: Supporting evidence: Both the US and USSR exerted significant pressure against colonialism — the US pushed Britain and France to accept decolonization; the USSR supported independence movements rhetorically and materially. The Suez Crisis (1956) is the clearest example where US and Soviet joint pressure forced Britain and France to accept the end of their independent imperial capacity. Without this superpower constraint, European powers might have been able to maintain empires longer. Challenging evidence: European empires faced internal pressures that would have forced decolonization regardless — nationalist movements had been building for decades before the Cold War (Indian National Congress founded 1885, Ho Chi Minh's communist movement 1920s, Nkrumah's political organizing before independence). The internal economic cost of maintaining empire against organized popular resistance was enormous — Britain would likely have left India eventually even without US pressure, as the cost-benefit calculation had shifted. Algeria shows that France refused to decolonize despite superpower pressure for eight years, only accepting independence after sustained armed struggle. The argument overestimates superpower causation and underestimates the agency of independence movements and the internal logic of colonial decline. A more accurate claim: the Cold War accelerated decolonization and shaped its timing and form, but was not its primary cause.
