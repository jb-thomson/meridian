7.8 Mass Atrocities After 1900

💀 Genocide🏛️ State Power👥 Human Rights⚖️ International Law
  • LO-A: Four required AP examples of mass atrocity (CED list): (1) Armenian Genocide in the Ottoman Empire during and after WWI; (2) Holodomor (Ukraine) in the Soviet Union 1920s–30s; (3) Holocaust (Jews in Nazi Germany) during WWII; (4) Cambodia under the Khmer Rouge in the late 1970s; (5) Tutsi genocide in Rwanda in the 1990s. Know ALL five, AP questions may ask you to compare or provide examples. Common factors: extremist ideology + state power + dehumanization: All 20th-century genocides share a pattern: an extremist group gains state power, constructs an ideology that dehumanizes a target group (Jews, Armenians, kulaks, intellectuals, Tutsi), uses state infrastructure (police, military, propaganda) to organize systematic killing, and eliminates the ability to resist or flee. Genocide requires state capacity; it is a crime of governments, not mobs. The Holocaust: the defining case: The Nazi Holocaust killed approximately 6 million Jews (two-thirds of European Jewry) and 5–6 million others (Roma, disabled people, Soviet POWs, political opponents, homosexuals). It was industrialized genocide: railways, bureaucracies, and extermination camps organized killing at industrial scale. The Holocaust produced the 1948 Genocide Convention, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and the principle of universal jurisdiction for crimes against humanity. Consequences: international law and the "Never Again" promise: Mass atrocities of the 20th century produced the Nuremberg Trials (1945–46), which established that individuals can be held criminally responsible for crimes against humanity regardless of state authority. The Genocide Convention (1948) defined genocide as a crime under international law. The ICC (International Criminal Court, 2002) provides a permanent court for such crimes. The promise "Never Again" has been repeatedly broken, Rwanda (1994) and Srebrenica (1995) occurred despite it, raising questions about international intervention obligations.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. The word "genocide" was invented in 1944 by Polish-Jewish lawyer Raphael Lemkin specifically to describe what was happening to European Jews under Nazi rule. Why did the world need a new word for this phenomenon?
Lemkin coined "genocide" (from Greek "genos" = race/tribe + Latin "cide" = killing) because the existing legal vocabulary was inadequate. "Murder" described individual crimes; "war crimes" described illegal acts against combatants or occupied civilian populations in the context of war. But the Holocaust involved something different: the deliberate attempt to eliminate an entire people as a category of human being, not as a military measure, not as punishment for individual acts, but simply for existing. There was no existing legal concept that captured the targeting of an entire group for collective elimination. Lemkin's innovation was to recognize that this was a distinct category of crime requiring a distinct legal framework. The 1948 Genocide Convention, which Lemkin lobbied tirelessly to achieve, defined genocide as "acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group." The concept matters practically: genocide requires different legal responses and obligations than war crimes or crimes against humanity, and without the concept, states had no legal framework for intervention or prosecution.
2. The Armenian Genocide (1915–1923) is sometimes disputed by the Turkish government, which calls it "war-era deaths" rather than genocide. What historical criteria determine whether a mass killing constitutes genocide?
The legal definition from the Genocide Convention (1948) requires "intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group." The key element is intent, not merely causing deaths but specifically targeting a group for destruction because of its group membership. The Armenian case has been recognized as genocide by historians and most international bodies based on: (1) Documentary evidence of Ottoman official orders to deport and kill Armenians, not merely to relocate them; (2) The organized, systematic nature of the killings, not wartime chaos but planned massacres and death marches conducted by military and paramilitary units; (3) The targeting of Armenian populations even in areas with no military threat (women, children, priests, the elderly were killed, not just military-age men); (4) Approximately 600,000–1.5 million Armenians killed (estimates vary) out of a pre-war population of approximately 2 million. The Turkish government's argument that these were wartime measures against a disloyal population, while Armenians had legitimate security concerns, does not meet the legal or historical standard for distinguishing mass killings from genocide, because the targeting was based on ethnic identity, not individual conduct.
3. The Holocaust killed two-thirds of European Jewry. What made the Holocaust uniquely industrialized compared to previous genocides?
The Holocaust was uniquely industrialized in its organization and efficiency. Previous mass killings (including the Armenian Genocide) used traditional methods: soldiers shooting people, forcing death marches, allowing starvation. The Nazi genocide systematically applied industrial methods to killing: railways transported victims from across Europe to extermination camps; the camps used industrial-scale gas chambers (disguised as showers) that could kill hundreds or thousands at a time; bodies were processed (hair shaved for industrial use, gold teeth extracted, ashes used as fertilizer) using industrial techniques; bureaucratic systems recorded and tracked victims with machine efficiency; Zyklon B (a commercial pesticide) was repurposed as a killing agent. The industrial scale allowed the murder of 6 million people over approximately 4 years, an average killing rate of 1.5 million per year. Previous mass atrocities had been destructive; the Holocaust achieved destruction at an industrial pace that had not been possible before. This industrial character was only possible because Nazi Germany was a modern industrial state that redirected its bureaucratic and logistical capacity toward genocide.
4. The Rwandan genocide (1994) killed approximately 500,000–800,000 Tutsi in roughly 100 days, while UN peacekeeping forces were present in the country but ordered not to intervene. What does this reveal about the international community's "Never Again" commitment?
Rwanda reveals that the "Never Again" commitment, while genuine as a moral principle, has consistently failed as a practical policy when genocide occurs. UN peacekeepers in Rwanda were under orders not to use force to stop the killings; the U.S. and other governments refused to use the word "genocide" officially because the Genocide Convention arguably obligated intervention. UN Commander Roméo Dallaire, who warned of the impending genocide and begged for authority to act, was denied the resources and authorization to stop it. The Rwandan genocide occurred in 100 days, an extraordinarily rapid killing pace (approximately 5,000–8,000 per day), during which the international community held meetings and issued statements. The lesson: "Never Again" functions as an aspirational norm that shapes international institutions (ICC, Responsibility to Protect doctrine) but is repeatedly overridden by geopolitical calculations, sovereignty concerns, and the domestic political costs of intervention. The gap between the moral commitment and the practical response is one of the most troubling patterns in 20th-21st century international relations.
5. The Nuremberg Trials (1945–46) established that individuals can be held criminally responsible for "crimes against humanity" even if they were following orders from legitimate government authority. Why is this principle both important and controversial?
The Nuremberg principle is important because it closes a key legal loophole: prior to Nuremberg, state officials could claim immunity from prosecution by arguing they were acting under state authority. The principle that "following orders" doesn't excuse crimes against humanity established that individuals retain moral and legal responsibility for their actions regardless of official authority. This enabled the prosecution of Nazi war criminals who had committed atrocities under state direction and set precedents for the 1948 Genocide Convention, the International Criminal Tribunal for Yugoslavia, the Rwanda Tribunal, and the ICC. But it is also controversial for several reasons: (1) Retroactivity, the Nuremberg defendants were tried under laws that didn't exist when they committed their acts (the legal principle "nullum crimen sine lege", no crime without prior law, was arguably violated). (2) Victor's justice, the Nuremberg tribunal was run by the victorious Allied powers, who were not themselves tried for comparable acts (strategic bombing of civilian cities, the atomic bomb). (3) Selective application, the Nuremberg principles have been applied inconsistently, primarily to the defeated and the powerless. These concerns don't invalidate the principles, but they do require acknowledgment of the gap between the principle's justice and its imperfect application.

The Reading

⚠️ What Is a Mass Atrocity? Why the 20th Century Was Uniquely Terrible

The 20th century produced more mass killings, genocides, ethnic cleansings, and politicides, than any previous century. Historians estimate that government-directed mass violence killed between 100 and 200 million people between 1900 and 2000. This is not because human beings became more evil in the 20th century. It is because the 20th century combined two things that previous centuries lacked in combination: extremist ideologies capable of dehumanizing entire categories of people, and modern state power (bureaucracies, railways, weapons, propaganda) capable of organizing systematic mass killing at industrial scale.

All of the AP CED's required examples share a common structure: an extremist group gains control of a state, constructs an ideology identifying a target group as an enemy to be eliminated, uses state infrastructure to organize systematic killing, and eliminates the victims' ability to resist or flee. The specific ideology differs, racial antisemitism (Holocaust), ethnic/nationalist hatred (Armenians, Rwanda), class-based ideology (Cambodia, Holodomor), but the structural pattern is consistent. Understanding mass atrocities requires understanding both the specific history and the common pattern.

🕌 Armenian Genocide (1915–1923): The First 20th-Century Genocide

The Armenian Genocide occurred within the collapsing Ottoman Empire during WWI. The Young Turk government, which had launched a program of modernization and Turkish nationalism, viewed the Armenian Christian minority as a potential threat: Armenians lived in eastern Anatolia, close to the Russian border, and some Armenian leaders had expressed sympathy for Russia (the Ottoman enemy). The Young Turk leadership used this security pretext to justify what became systematic extermination.

Beginning in April 1915, Ottoman authorities arrested and executed Armenian intellectuals and leaders. Armenian men in the army were disarmed and worked to death or shot. Armenian women, children, and the elderly were forced on death marches into the Syrian desert without food or water. Massacres occurred throughout Anatolia. The exact death toll is disputed, estimates range from 600,000 to 1.5 million killed, out of a pre-war Armenian population of approximately 2 million. The genocide effectively ended the centuries-old Armenian presence in Anatolia. The surviving Armenian diaspora settled in Lebanon, Syria, France, the United States, and Russia, communities that persist today.

💡 Fun Fact
Adolf Hitler, when planning the invasion of Poland in 1939, reportedly said: "Who today remembers the annihilation of the Armenians?", meaning that without international accountability, a genocide could be committed without permanent consequence. Whether or not Hitler actually said these exact words (the source is disputed), the sentiment reflects how the lack of accountability for the Armenian Genocide may have emboldened later perpetrators. This is precisely why the international human rights framework built after WWII, the Genocide Convention, Nuremberg Principles, and eventually the ICC, was designed to ensure accountability and deter future atrocities.

☭ Holodomor (1932–33): Famine as a Weapon

The Holodomor ("death by starvation") refers to the famine that killed an estimated 3–7 million Ukrainians between 1932 and 1933, caused by Soviet policies under Stalin. As discussed in Topic 7.4, Stalin's forced collectivization of Ukrainian agriculture, combined with unrealistic grain quotas, a blacklisting system that prevented designated villages from receiving food, and a passport system that prevented peasants from leaving famine-stricken areas, created the conditions for mass starvation.

Whether the Holodomor constitutes genocide in the legal sense, requiring specific intent to destroy Ukrainians as a group, is debated among historians. Many scholars and governments (including Ukraine, Canada, and others) recognize it as genocide; others classify it as a crime against humanity or politicide (killing based on political class, in this case "kulaks" and Ukrainian nationalist communities). What is not disputed: the Soviet state's policies caused millions of Ukrainians to die, and evidence suggests that Soviet officials knew the policies were causing mass death and continued them anyway, in some cases accelerating grain extraction.

✡️ The Holocaust: Industrialized Genocide

The Holocaust was the systematic, state-sponsored murder of approximately six million Jews, roughly two-thirds of European Jewry, by Nazi Germany and its collaborators between 1941 and 1945. It also killed approximately five to six million others: Roma (Sinti), disabled people, Soviet prisoners of war, political opponents, Jehovah's Witnesses, and homosexuals.

The Holocaust was the product of Nazi racial ideology combined with modern industrial state capacity. It proceeded in stages:

  • 1933–1939: Legal persecution. The Nuremberg Laws (1935) stripped Jews of German citizenship and prohibited marriage between Jews and non-Jews. Jewish-owned businesses were boycotted, then seized. Jews were progressively excluded from public life, professions, and education.
  • 1938–1941: Violence and ghettoization. Kristallnacht (November 1938), a nationwide pogrom destroying Jewish businesses, synagogues, and homes, killed hundreds and sent 30,000 to concentration camps. As Germany conquered Poland and the Soviet Union, Jews were concentrated in overcrowded urban ghettos.
  • 1941–1945: "Final Solution", systematic extermination. The Wannsee Conference (January 1942) coordinated the systematic murder of all European Jews. Mobile killing units (Einsatzgruppen) had already killed over 1.5 million Jews in the Soviet Union by shooting. The extermination camps, Auschwitz-Birkenau, Treblinka, Sobibor, Belzec, Majdanek, Chelmno, used industrial-scale gas chambers, processing thousands per day. In total, approximately six million Jews were murdered.

🇰🇭 Cambodia (1975–1979): Genocide by Ideology

The Khmer Rouge, a radical communist movement led by Pol Pot, seized power in Cambodia in April 1975 and immediately began implementing an extreme agrarian utopian program. They evacuated all cities (Phnom Penh, a city of 2 million, was emptied in days), abolished money, schools, and religion, and forced the entire population into agricultural collectives. Anyone associated with the old government, the educated class ("intellectuals" were defined as anyone wearing glasses), or foreign influence was targeted for execution.

The results were catastrophic: approximately 1.5–2 million people died, roughly 20–25% of Cambodia's pre-1975 population, through execution, starvation, forced labor, and disease. The Khmer Rouge additionally targeted ethnic Vietnamese, Chinese, and Muslim Cham minorities specifically for ethnic destruction, which meets the Genocide Convention's definition. The regime fell when Vietnam invaded in December 1978 (January 1979), driven in part by border attacks. Pol Pot fled and was never extradited for trial; he died in 1998 under house arrest by his own former comrades. The Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia (established 2006) has convicted several Khmer Rouge leaders, but accountability came decades late.

🇷🇼 Rwanda (1994): "Never Again" Failed

The Rwandan genocide took place in approximately 100 days between April and July 1994, killing an estimated 500,000–800,000 Tutsi (and moderate Hutu). It was organized by Hutu extremists within the Rwandan government using radio propaganda, political networks, and local militia (interahamwe). RTLM radio, "Radio Mille Collines" ("Radio of a Thousand Hills"), broadcast the names of Tutsi targets and called on Hutu listeners to "cut down the tall trees" (a euphemism for killing Tutsi). The killings were largely carried out with machetes by ordinary civilians, organized through local administrative networks.

The international response was a catastrophic failure. The UN Mission to Rwanda (UNAMIR) had approximately 2,500 peacekeepers on the ground but was under orders not to use force except in self-defense. General Roméo Dallaire, the UNAMIR commander, sent detailed warnings to UN headquarters about the impending genocide and requested authority to act. He was denied. The U.S. and other governments refused to officially use the word "genocide" because the Genocide Convention arguably obligated intervention they were unwilling to undertake. The genocide ended when the Rwandan Patriotic Front (a Tutsi rebel army based in Uganda) defeated the Hutu extremist government and took control of Rwanda. The international community's failure in Rwanda directly contributed to the development of the "Responsibility to Protect" (R2P) doctrine (2005), which asserts that states have a responsibility to protect their populations from genocide and that the international community has a responsibility to intervene when states fail to do so.

💡 Fun Fact
During the Rwandan genocide, the Hotel des Mille Collines in Kigali sheltered approximately 1,268 Tutsi refugees from the killing, an episode made famous by the film "Hotel Rwanda" (2004). The hotel's manager, Paul Rusesabagina, is often portrayed as a hero, though his story has been complicated by subsequent controversy. What is less well-known: neighboring countries (Tanzania, Zaire/Congo) accepted hundreds of thousands of Hutu refugees as the RPF advanced. Those refugee camps then became bases for former Interahamwe militia who continued attacks on Tutsi in the Democratic Republic of Congo for decades. The genocide created cascading regional consequences that destabilized Central Africa for the next 30 years.

⚖️ Consequences: International Law, Accountability, and the Limits of "Never Again"

The mass atrocities of the 20th century produced a significant international legal response:

  • Nuremberg Trials (1945–46): Established individual criminal responsibility for crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity; "following orders" is not a defense.
  • Genocide Convention (1948): The first international treaty specifically prohibiting genocide and obligating signatories to prevent and punish it.
  • Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948): Established the framework of universal human rights owed to every person regardless of nationality or government.
  • International Criminal Tribunal for Yugoslavia (1993) and Rwanda (1994): Ad hoc tribunals established by the UN Security Council to prosecute genocide and crimes against humanity in those specific conflicts.
  • International Criminal Court (ICC, 2002): Permanent international court with jurisdiction over genocide, crimes against humanity, and war crimes.
  • Responsibility to Protect (R2P, 2005): International norm asserting that states have a responsibility to protect their populations from genocide, and the international community has a responsibility to intervene when they fail.

Despite this legal architecture, mass atrocities have continued: Srebrenica (1995), Darfur (2003–ongoing), Myanmar (2016–ongoing), and others have occurred in the post-ICC world. The gap between the legal commitment to "Never Again" and the political will to act remains one of the defining failures of the contemporary international order.

According to the AP CED, the rise of extremist groups in power led to attempted destruction of specific populations. Which of the following best explains why modern states are particularly capable of carrying out genocide compared to pre-industrial societies?
  • Modern populations are more susceptible to ideological extremism than pre-industrial populations
  • International law in the modern period has failed to establish clear prohibitions on mass killing
  • Modern states possess bureaucratic organization, transportation infrastructure, and weapons technology that enable systematic killing at industrial scale
  • Modern media allows extremist groups to spread hatred more effectively than pre-modern oral traditions
Modern states' bureaucratic capacity (detailed census records identifying target populations, administrative networks reaching every village), transportation infrastructure (railways that transported Holocaust victims across Europe), and weapons technology (gas chambers, automatic weapons) are what enabled 20th-century genocides to kill at the scale they did. Pre-industrial societies had hatred and ethnic violence, but lacked the organizational capacity for industrial-scale systematic killing.
The Holodomor is classified in the AP CED under "Ukraine in the Soviet Union in the 1920s and 1930s" as a mass atrocity. Its inclusion in this category is most directly supported by
  • the fact that it killed more people than any of the other listed atrocities
  • the scale of agricultural failure, which was entirely the result of drought and climate rather than policy decisions
  • the Soviet Union's official acknowledgment that the famine constituted genocide
  • evidence that Soviet policies deliberately targeted Ukrainians and continued grain extraction during famine, causing mass starvation
The Holodomor's classification as a mass atrocity rests on the evidence that Soviet policies, unrealistic grain quotas, confiscation of all food, blacklisting of non-compliant villages, prohibition on peasants leaving, continued even as mass starvation was occurring and officials were fully aware of deaths. Whether it was explicitly designed to destroy Ukrainians as a people (genocide) or was primarily class-based (killing "kulaks") is debated, but the deliberate policy continuation during documented mass death places it in the category of state-directed mass atrocity.
The Holocaust's industrialized character, using railways, bureaucracies, and gas chambers, distinguished it from the Armenian Genocide primarily in
  • the degree of ideological hatred motivating the perpetrators
  • the systematic application of modern industrial methods and state organization to mass killing, enabling a scale and efficiency of murder not previously possible
  • the fact that the Holocaust targeted a religious rather than ethnic minority
  • international recognition, the Holocaust was recognized as genocide while the Armenian Genocide was not
Both the Armenian Genocide and the Holocaust were driven by intense ideological hatred. The difference is the industrial infrastructure applied: the Holocaust used railway networks across occupied Europe, bureaucratic registration of Jewish populations, purpose-built extermination camps with industrial-capacity gas chambers, and a coordinating administrative structure (the SS), enabling the systematic murder of 6 million people. The Armenian Genocide used shooting, death marches, and starvation, lethal but not industrialized in the Holocaust's sense.
The Rwandan genocide occurred in approximately 100 days despite the presence of UN peacekeeping forces. This is primarily significant as a demonstration that
  • genocide prevention requires not only international legal frameworks but political will by major powers to authorize intervention
  • UN peacekeeping operations are structurally incapable of preventing any form of mass violence
  • the Rwandan government's military superiority made international intervention logistically impossible
  • the Genocide Convention (1948) was poorly designed and ineffective in all subsequent applications
The Rwanda case reveals the gap between international legal frameworks (the Genocide Convention obligated prevention and punishment) and political will (the U.S. and other powers were unwilling to authorize or undertake intervention). UN peacekeepers were present but had a mandate that prevented them from using force. The failure led to institutional reforms (ICC, R2P doctrine) designed to close the gap, though those reforms have also proven insufficient in subsequent atrocities.
The Nuremberg Trials established the principle that individuals can be held criminally responsible for "crimes against humanity" regardless of state authority. The most historically significant consequence of this principle was
  • the immediate end of all state-directed mass violence following WWII
  • the requirement that all future military conflicts be subject to UN Security Council approval
  • the creation of the International Criminal Court and a framework of universal jurisdiction for the most serious human rights crimes
  • the dissolution of the German state and permanent Allied military occupation
The Nuremberg Principles provided the legal foundation for the Genocide Convention (1948), the subsequent UN human rights framework, and eventually the permanent International Criminal Court (2002). The principle of individual criminal responsibility regardless of state authority, and the rejection of "following orders" as a defense, is the foundational concept of international criminal law. It created the framework within which subsequent atrocities (Yugoslavia, Rwanda) were prosecuted and the ICC was established.
Using ONE specific example from the AP CED list, explain the causes of a mass atrocity in the period 1900–present and ONE consequence of that atrocity for international law or human rights.
Causes: The Holocaust resulted from the combination of Nazi racial ideology (which defined Jews as a biological threat to the German "racial body"), the political power of an extremist state (which could direct all state resources toward implementing genocide), and the specific conditions of total war (which provided both the opportunity, occupied territories contained large Jewish populations, and the cover of wartime emergency for mass killing). The Wannsee Conference (1942) coordinated the "Final Solution" using railway infrastructure and purpose-built extermination camps to kill approximately six million Jews. Consequence: The Holocaust produced the Nuremberg Trials (1945–46), which established the principle that individuals can be held criminally responsible for crimes against humanity regardless of state authority, "following orders" is not a defense. This principle became the foundation of the Genocide Convention (1948) and eventually the International Criminal Court (2002), creating a permanent legal framework for prosecuting perpetrators of genocide and crimes against humanity.
Evaluate the extent to which mass atrocities in the period 1900–present shared common causes, despite occurring in very different political and cultural contexts.
Despite their different ideological frameworks, racial antisemitism (Holocaust), ethnic nationalism (Rwanda, Armenia), class-based utopianism (Cambodia, Holodomor), 20th-century mass atrocities shared essential common causes: an extremist group gaining control of state power, the construction of a dehumanizing ideology that defined a target group as an existential threat, and the use of modern state infrastructure (bureaucracies, transportation, weapons, propaganda) to organize systematic mass killing at a scale impossible without state capacity, demonstrating that genocide is primarily a crime of states rather than of cultures or civilizations.
Model answer: Dehumanization is psychologically necessary for genocide because it overcomes a fundamental human inhibition: the recognition that victims are people like ourselves, whose suffering matters and whose deaths are wrong. Most human beings have strong inhibitions against killing other people; those inhibitions have to be systematically dismantled to produce mass killers. Dehumanizing propaganda, portraying Jews as rats and vermin, Armenians as traitors, Tutsi as cockroaches, accomplishes this by placing the target group outside the moral community in which ordinary ethical constraints apply. If Jews are literally rats spreading disease, killing them is pest control, not murder. If Tutsi are literally inyenzi (cockroaches), exterminating them is civic hygiene. The propaganda thus performs a crucial psychological function: it allows ordinary people (not just ideological fanatics) to participate in killing by reframing the act. The pattern is consistent enough across genocides that "dehumanizing language" is now recognized as a warning sign in genocide prevention frameworks (Genocide Watch's "Ten Stages of Genocide").
Model answer: The uniqueness claim has genuine historical basis: the Holocaust combined three features in an unprecedented way. It targeted an entire group for total elimination across all of Europe; it used modern industrial methods (railways, gas chambers, bureaucratic record-keeping) to achieve killing at scale; and it was conducted as state policy at the level of a major European power. No previous genocide had this combination of ideological totalism, industrial efficiency, and geographic reach. But emphasizing uniqueness carries risks. It can imply that other genocides are less serious or less worthy of attention, which is incorrect both morally and historically. The Armenian Genocide, Holodomor, Cambodian genocide, and Rwandan genocide were all catastrophic mass killings that killed hundreds of thousands to millions of people through systematic state action. Emphasizing Holocaust uniqueness can also suggest that genocide is always a singular aberration rather than a recurring pattern with identifiable causes, warning signs, and prevention opportunities. The most useful historical approach acknowledges the Holocaust's specific character while treating it as one, the most industrialized and extensively documented, instance of a recurrent phenomenon that demands ongoing vigilance.
Model answer: The different target categories reveal that genocide can emerge from different ideological frameworks but follows a common structural logic: identify a group, construct an ideology that defines them as an enemy of the nation/class/race, use state power to organize their destruction. The Khmer Rouge's targets (educated people, ethnic minorities, religious practitioners) were defined by class ideology, anyone associated with the old order or foreign influence threatened the agrarian utopia. Rwanda's genocide was driven by ethnic ideology, colonial-era Belgian racial classification of Hutu and Tutsi as distinct "races" created an ethnic identity that Hutu extremists politicized into a survival conflict. The Holocaust was driven by racial antisemitism, the pseudo-scientific claim that Jews were a biological threat to the German racial body. What unites these different target categories is the function: each ideology required a group to be cast as an existential threat that had to be eliminated for the community to survive and flourish. The specific category of victim (race, class, ethnicity, education) is determined by the ideology; the genocidal logic is consistent across cases.
Model answer: Rwanda's failure illustrates that international law is only as effective as the political will to enforce it. The Genocide Convention obligated signatories to "prevent and punish" genocide, but it provided no automatic enforcement mechanism. Acting required political decisions by governments with their own domestic interests and constraints. In Rwanda's case: (1) The U.S. had just experienced a disastrous intervention in Somalia (Black Hawk Down, 1993) and was unwilling to risk another African intervention. (2) Officially classifying the killings as "genocide" would have obligated action, so the Clinton administration instructed officials to avoid the word ("acts of genocide may be occurring"). (3) France had historically supported the Hutu government and was diplomatically compromised. (4) The UN's mandate and command structures prevented peacekeepers from acting. The result: 500,000–800,000 people were killed in 100 days while the international community issued statements. The lesson: international law creates norms and establishes accountability after the fact, but preventing genocide in real time requires political decisions that states are reluctant to make when intervention has costs and non-intervention has few immediate consequences for the decision-makers.
Model answer: The Nuremberg principle is essential because it closes the loophole that allows state authority to shield individual perpetrators: if "I was following orders" is always a complete defense, then state-organized atrocities become legally impossible to prosecute. The principle is most clearly justified in extreme cases, participating in operating a gas chamber, shooting prisoners, organizing deportations, where the action is so obviously criminal that no reasonable person could believe it legitimate. But the principle is also complicated in practice. Soldiers and civil servants regularly follow orders they find questionable without being able to verify their legality or morality; full individual judgment of every order would make any military or bureaucratic organization impossible. Legal systems generally distinguish between acts that are manifestly and obviously illegal (torture, murder of prisoners, genocide) versus acts that may be wrong but are within legal authority. The practical standard that emerged from Nuremberg is that individuals are responsible when they participate in acts that are manifestly illegal, that a "reasonable person" would recognize as crimes, and when they had a genuine choice not to participate (even if participation was safer). Cases involving soldiers in combat zones with unclear rules of engagement are more complicated; cases involving operating extermination camps are not.
