5.9 Society and the Industrial Age

👥 Social👩 Gender🏙️ Environment
  • LO-A: Women's roles diverged by class — Working-class women typically held wage-earning jobs in factories, textile mills, or domestic service to supplement family income — children often worked too. Middle-class women increasingly did not work outside the home (the "cult of domesticity") — they were expected to manage the household, raise children, and maintain the family's social respectability. This class division in women's experience is a required AP distinction: the CED specifically contrasts "women and often children in working class families" who worked for wages with "middle-class women who did not have the same economic demands to satisfy" and were "increasingly limited to roles in the household." Industrialization created two new major classes: (1) the middle class (or bourgeoisie) — factory owners, managers, merchants, lawyers, doctors, engineers — people who did not do manual labor but were not aristocrats; they valued education, "respectability," and professional achievement; (2) the industrial working class (or proletariat) — factory workers, miners, railroad workers, domestic servants — people who sold their labor for wages and worked with their hands. These two classes replaced the older three-tier structure (aristocracy/church / peasants) that had defined pre-industrial Europe. Urbanization's challenges — Cities grew far faster than infrastructure could accommodate. Challenges the CED lists: pollution (coal smoke, industrial waste), poverty, crime, public health crises (cholera epidemics), housing shortages, and insufficient infrastructure. London's 1854 cholera epidemic was traced to contaminated water by John Snow — leading eventually to modern sewage systems and germ theory's application to public health. By 1900, industrial cities were beginning to address these problems through municipal reform, but the early industrial city was often genuinely dangerous and miserable for the poor.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Before industrialization, European society was divided mainly into aristocracy, clergy, and peasants/craftspeople. How did industrialization change this class structure?
Industrialization created two new major classes that didn't fit the old structure: (1) a middle class — factory owners, managers, merchants, lawyers, doctors, engineers — people with professional or business income who weren't manual laborers but weren't aristocrats either; they valued education, professional achievement, and "respectability." (2) an industrial working class — factory workers, miners, railroad workers — who sold their labor for wages. These new classes were defined by economic relationship to production (owning businesses vs. selling labor) rather than birth or religious status. They gradually became the dominant social groups in industrialized societies, overshadowing the old aristocracy and making the peasant class smaller as people moved to cities.
2. Working-class women typically worked for wages; middle-class women typically did not. Why did the same industrialization process produce such different outcomes for women of different classes?
Working-class family budgets required every able-bodied person to contribute income — one factory worker's wages were often not enough for a family to survive, so wives and children also worked. Working-class women went to factories, textile mills, or domestic service out of economic necessity. Middle-class families had enough income that the wife didn't need to work for wages. Middle-class culture (influenced by Enlightenment ideas about gender and Victorian social norms) held that a respectable middle-class woman managed the home and raised children — working outside the home was seen as lowering the family's social status, suggesting the husband couldn't support them. So industrialization created wealth for some that allowed women to not work outside the home while simultaneously forcing working-class women to work more than before.
3. London grew from about 1 million people in 1800 to about 6.5 million by 1900 — all before modern urban infrastructure existed. What kinds of problems would this rapid growth create?
Growing a city from 1 million to 6.5 million in 100 years means building housing, roads, water supply, waste disposal, schools, hospitals, and police forces for 5.5 million additional people. Without planning and investment, the result is: (1) housing shortage — overcrowded tenements, multiple families per room; (2) no sewage systems — human waste goes into rivers that people drink from; (3) no clean water supply — disease spreads easily; (4) coal smoke from thousands of factories and home fires creates severe air pollution; (5) poverty concentration — poor people unable to afford better housing cluster in slums; (6) crime — poverty and desperation increase; (7) public health crises — cholera and other diseases spread in dense, poorly sanitated conditions. All of these are exactly what happened in early industrial cities.
4. John Snow traced London's 1854 cholera epidemic to a single contaminated water pump. Why was this discovery historically significant beyond just stopping one epidemic?
Before Snow, most people (including doctors) believed disease spread through "miasma" — bad air from rotting matter. Snow's careful mapping of cholera cases around a single water pump, and his removal of the pump handle to stop the epidemic, demonstrated empirically that cholera spread through contaminated water, not air. This was one of the first applications of epidemiology (the science of disease patterns) to public health. It changed what governments needed to do: if disease spread through air, not much could be done; if through water, governments could build sewage systems and clean water supplies to prevent epidemics. London's modern sewer system (built 1858–1875) followed directly from this insight. The discovery was foundational to modern public health.
5. The middle class in industrial societies valued education and "respectability." How does this value system reflect the way middle-class people made their living, compared to both aristocrats and working-class people?
Aristocrats inherited their wealth and status — they didn't need to work or achieve anything to maintain their position. Working-class people sold their physical labor — their economic value was their bodies' capacity to work. Middle-class people made their living through professional expertise or business management — their economic value was their education, technical knowledge, and professional judgment. This economic reality made education and professional achievement their natural values: education produced the skills that produced their income. "Respectability" was important because middle-class status, unlike aristocratic status, was not guaranteed by birth — it had to be demonstrated through behavior, education, and social standing. The middle class's values directly reflected how they earned their living.

The Reading

🏗️ New Social Classes: The Middle and Working Class

Before industrialization, European social structure had a fairly simple shape: at the top, a small aristocracy whose wealth came from inherited land; in the middle, a small clergy; at the bottom, a large mass of peasants and craftspeople. Birth determined class. A peasant's child was a peasant; a lord's child was a lord.

Industrialization broke this structure. Two entirely new social classes emerged, defined not by birth but by their economic relationship to production:

The middle class — factory owners, managers, merchants, lawyers, doctors, engineers, accountants, shopkeepers — these were people who earned their living through professional expertise or business management rather than manual labor, but who were not aristocrats. They were the great new creation of industrial capitalism. In 1750, this class was small. By 1900 in Britain, it had grown to perhaps 25–30% of the population. They valued education, professional achievement, and "respectability" — the behaviors and appearances that marked them as different from both the aristocracy (too refined and leisured) and the working class (too rough and manual).

The industrial working class — factory workers, miners, railroad laborers, domestic servants — these were people who sold their physical labor for wages. They were concentrated in industrial cities, worked in factories or on railroads, and had no property except their labor to sell. They replaced the peasantry as the largest social class in industrialized countries, though peasants remained numerically dominant in less-industrialized regions like Russia and southern Europe into the 20th century.

💡 Fun Fact
The Victorian middle class's obsession with "respectability" produced some genuinely strange customs. It was considered respectable to have a piano in your parlor — whether or not anyone could play it. It was considered unrespectable to show emotion in public, discuss money at dinner, or let anyone see your ankles (for women). Entire industries grew up around producing the props of middle-class respectability: china dishes, lace curtains, calling cards, and specific styles of clothing that signaled "I am not a laborer." Respectability was the middle class's way of marking its identity when birth alone wasn't enough.

👩 Women's Roles: A Class Divide

Industrialization affected women's lives profoundly — but very differently depending on class. The AP CED draws a direct contrast that the exam regularly tests.

Working-class women typically worked for wages. Factory work, textile mills, domestic service (as maids and cooks in wealthier households), and other low-wage employment were the options. Working-class family budgets required every able-bodied person to contribute income — a factory worker's wages were often not enough to support a family alone. Women and children worked not because they wanted to but because survival required it. Working-class children also commonly worked until child labor laws gradually restricted this (Factory Acts 1833, 1844, 1847).

Middle-class women increasingly did not work outside the home. Middle-class culture — shaped by Victorian social norms and Enlightenment-era gender ideology — developed the "cult of domesticity": the ideal that a respectable middle-class woman's proper sphere was the home. She managed the household, raised children, organized social life, and embodied the family's moral and cultural values. Working outside the home for wages was seen as socially degrading — it implied the husband couldn't support the family and reduced the wife to the same level as working-class women. Paradoxically, as middle-class women were excluded from paid work, they were idealized as morally superior to men — the "angel in the house."

📝 AP Tip
The CED specifically states: "While women and often children in working class families typically held wage-earning jobs to supplement their families' income, middle-class women who did not have the same economic demands to satisfy were increasingly limited to roles in the household or roles focused on child development." This contrast — working-class women work, middle-class women stay home — is a required distinction. Know both parts.

This class divide in women's experience also shaped the women's rights movement. Middle-class women, with education and leisure time, were the ones who organized the suffrage and feminist movements of the 19th century. They had the time and literacy to write, organize, and argue for rights — and the contradiction between their social respectability and their political exclusion was particularly sharp.

💡 Fun Fact
Florence Nightingale — who became famous for reforming nursing during the Crimean War (1854–1856) — came from a wealthy upper-middle-class family. Her family was appalled when she announced she wanted to become a nurse, which they considered beneath her class and morally questionable (nurses had a reputation for being drunken and disreputable). Nightingale's success in professionalizing nursing — making it a respectable, skilled career for educated middle-class women — was itself a challenge to the cult of domesticity. She argued that organized, educated women could do valuable public work, not just domestic work.

🏙️ The Industrial City: Growth and Its Problems

Cities grew faster during industrialization than at any previous point in human history. London grew from roughly 1 million people in 1800 to 6.5 million by 1900. Manchester went from a small market town to a city of 300,000 in a century. Chicago barely existed in 1830; by 1900 it had 1.7 million people.

This growth was faster than infrastructure could handle. The result was a long list of serious social problems that the CED specifically requires you to know:

  • Pollution: Thousands of coal-burning factories and home fires produced dense smog over industrial cities. Rivers received industrial waste and untreated sewage. London's Thames was described in 1858 as producing a "Great Stink" — the smell of raw sewage so bad that Parliament had to shut down. Air pollution caused chronic respiratory illness; water pollution caused epidemic disease.
  • Poverty: Industrial wages, especially in the early period, barely covered subsistence. Working-class families spent most of their income on food and rent, with nothing left for emergencies or savings. Unemployment meant immediate destitution — no social insurance existed until the late 19th century. Poverty in industrial slums was concentrated and visible in ways rural poverty was not.
  • Increased crime: Dense, poor, and transient urban populations produced higher crime rates than rural communities. Pickpocketing, theft, and violence increased in industrial cities. Police forces barely existed in the early 19th century — the London Metropolitan Police was established only in 1829.
  • Public health crises: The worst were the cholera epidemics. Cholera spread through water contaminated with human feces — and in a city without sewage systems, where human waste ran into the same rivers people drank from, epidemics were inevitable. The 1831 epidemic killed 52,000 in Britain; the 1848 epidemic killed 62,000. The 1854 London outbreak killed over 600 people in a single week.
  • Housing shortages: Cities grew faster than housing supply. Working-class families crowded into tenements — large, poorly maintained apartment buildings — with multiple families per room, no running water, and shared outdoor toilets. Jacob Riis's photographs of New York tenements in 1890 shocked American middle-class readers who had no idea how the urban poor actually lived.
  • Insufficient infrastructure: No sewers, no clean water supply, no regular garbage collection, inadequate roads, overcrowded schools. Cities were building infrastructure as fast as they could, but growth outpaced construction.

🔬 Public Health Reform: John Snow and the Germ Theory

The cholera epidemics forced governments to think scientifically about public health. The key breakthrough came in 1854, when Dr. John Snow — a London physician — mapped the cases in a cholera outbreak in the Soho neighborhood. He found that nearly all cases clustered around a single water pump on Broad Street. He persuaded local officials to remove the pump handle, and the outbreak rapidly subsided.

Snow's work demonstrated empirically that cholera spread through contaminated water — not through "miasma" (bad air) as most doctors believed. This insight required a governmental response: build sewage systems to keep human waste out of drinking water. London's modern sewer system, designed by Joseph Bazalgette and built from 1858–1875, was the direct result. It was one of the great public health engineering achievements of the 19th century and essentially ended cholera as a major threat in London.

Gradually, through the late 19th century, industrial cities got better. Sewage systems, clean water supplies, police forces, building codes, parks, and public transportation all improved urban life. By 1900, a London factory worker lived in conditions far better than those of 1840 — still modest, but not the desperate squalor of the early industrial period.

💡 Fun Fact
The Great Stink of 1858 — when the Thames smelled so appalling in summer that Parliament had to shut down and MPs fled the building — directly caused the construction of London's sewer system. Politicians who had debated the problem for years found their debates impossible when the windows had to be shut against the smell. Sometimes it takes a personal crisis to turn a political problem into political action. The modern London sewer system that Bazalgette built handled sewage from 4 million people; today it serves 8.5 million. It's still working 160 years later.

Part A

Identify ONE way the social hierarchy of industrialized countries in the period 1750–1900 differed from the social hierarchy of pre-industrial European societies.

Sample answer: Pre-industrial European social hierarchy was defined primarily by birth — aristocratic status was inherited, peasants were born into their class, and movement between classes was rare. Industrial society created a middle class whose status was defined not by birth but by professional achievement, education, and income from business or employment. A factory owner, merchant, lawyer, or doctor could achieve middle-class status through economic success regardless of birth, giving industrial society somewhat more social mobility than the feudal system. This shift from birth-based to achievement-based class identity was a major social change produced by industrialization, even though inequality remained substantial and working-class people rarely achieved middle-class status.

Part B

Explain ONE way women's roles in the period 1750–1900 differed between the working class and the middle class in industrialized societies.

Sample answer: Working-class women typically worked for wages in factories, textile mills, or as domestic servants because their family's economic survival required their income — a factory worker's wages were often insufficient to support a family without supplementary earnings from wives and children. Middle-class women, by contrast, were increasingly expected to remain in the household rather than seek paid employment. Middle-class culture developed the "cult of domesticity," holding that a respectable middle-class woman's proper role was managing the household, raising children, and embodying moral virtue. Middle-class families had sufficient income that the wife didn't need to work for wages, and the middle-class cultural ideal made domestic life the marker of female respectability. The same industrial economy that forced working-class women into paid labor enabled and idealized the domestic role for middle-class women.

Part C

Explain ONE specific challenge created by rapid urbanization in industrial cities in the period 1750–1900 and ONE way this challenge was addressed.

Sample answer: Rapid urbanization created severe public health crises, most dramatically the cholera epidemics that struck British industrial cities in 1831, 1848, 1854, and 1866. Cholera spread through water contaminated by human waste — and industrial cities without sewage systems had human waste running into the same rivers people drank from. Dense urban populations meant one infected person's waste could contaminate a water supply used by thousands. The 1848 epidemic killed 62,000 in Britain. This challenge was addressed through the application of epidemiological investigation and infrastructure investment: John Snow's 1854 investigation traced the Soho cholera outbreak to a contaminated water pump, demonstrating that cholera spread through water rather than air. This finding led London to build a comprehensive sewage system (1858–1875) designed by Joseph Bazalgette that separated waste disposal from drinking water. The 1866 epidemic killed only 4,000 — a dramatic reduction attributable to the new infrastructure. The cholera crisis shows how urbanization forced governments to invest in public health infrastructure they had previously ignored.

Middle-class families had enough income that the husband's earnings could support the family without the wife working for wages. So the same industrialization that forced working-class women into paid labor created the material conditions that allowed middle-class women to stay home, while the cultural ideology made the choice of staying home appear natural and virtuous rather than economically determined.
Model answer: Cholera spreads through water contaminated with human feces — specifically the bacterium Vibrio cholerae. Industrial cities lacked sewage systems, so human waste went into cesspits or directly into rivers and streams. People also drew drinking water from those same rivers and streams. The dense population of industrial cities meant that one infected person's waste could contaminate a water supply used by thousands. John Snow's 1854 investigation showed this definitively: cases clustered around a single pump drawing from a contaminated well. The implication was clear: build sewage systems to separate waste from drinking water. Snow's work showed that public health was fundamentally a question of infrastructure — governments needed to invest in sewage and water systems, not just treat individual sick people. This insight drove modern public health policy.
Model answer: The working class's new social power came from concentration: hundreds or thousands of workers in a single factory, concentrated in industrial cities, working under identical conditions. This made collective organization possible in ways it hadn't been for dispersed rural peasants. Workers formed labor unions that could credibly threaten to stop production through strikes — costing owners far more than the cost of modest wage increases. Workers in industrial cities could also organize politically: they could vote (once extended the franchise), support candidates who backed labor legislation, and build working-class communities and institutions. By 1900, working-class political parties were major forces in Germany, France, and Britain. The working class's collective power — based on their indispensability to industrial production — was the mechanism through which they improved their conditions.
Model answer: Municipal reform drove most of the improvement: (1) sewage systems — London's (1858–1875) ended cholera epidemics there; (2) clean water supply systems — separate from sewage; (3) police forces — London's Metropolitan Police (1829) established the model; (4) building codes — setting minimum standards for housing; (5) public health legislation (Public Health Act 1875 in Britain required local governments to provide clean water and sanitation); (6) urban planning — parks, wider streets, regulated housing density; (7) compulsory education (1870 in Britain); (8) public transportation — horse-drawn buses, then electric trams and subways — allowed workers to live further from factories. These improvements were forced by public pressure, working-class political demands, middle-class outrage at urban conditions (documented by journalists and novelists like Charles Dickens), and the economic self-interest of cities in having healthy, productive workers.
