AP World History · Unit 3: Land-Based Empires

3.4 Comparison in Land-Based Empires

🏛️ Political💰 Economics🎨 Culture⚙️ Technology🌍 Interactions
  • LO-A: The big comparison question: How did land-based empires increase their influence 1450–1750? The AP's answer involves four categories: gunpowder military expansion, bureaucratic/military administration, religious/artistic legitimation, and revenue collection. Compare and contrast the empires on each dimension. What all empires shared: All four major empires (Ottoman, Mughal, Safavid, Qing) used gunpowder weapons, developed centralized administrative systems, used religion and art to legitimize power, and extracted revenue through tax systems. These are the common patterns across diverse contexts.
  • LO-B: Apply the argumentation historical thinking skill using specific,…: What made each empire distinct: The Ottoman devshirme; Mughal policy of religious tolerance under Akbar; Safavid Shi'a state identity; Qing dual Manchu-Chinese administration. These distinctions reflect different populations, geographic situations, and historical contexts.
  • LO-C: Synthesize Unit 3 content into comparative claims: The AP comparison framework: A strong comparative argument requires: (1) a clear claim about similarity or difference, (2) specific evidence from at least two empires, and (3) an explanation of why the similarity or difference matters historically (what does it reveal about the period?).

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. What is the difference between description and comparison in historical writing? Why does the AP exam specifically test comparison as a skill?
Description tells you what happened in one place or time. Comparison tells you something about how two or more things relate, how they are similar, different, or why those similarities/differences matter. Comparison is a higher-order thinking skill because it requires synthesis: you must understand multiple cases well enough to identify meaningful points of contact between them. The AP values comparison because it tests whether students can move beyond memorized facts to draw conclusions about historical patterns. A student who can say "both the Ottoman and Mughal empires used religion to legitimize power, but through different methods, the Ottomans claimed the Sunni caliphate while Akbar practiced inclusive tolerance, reflecting their different subject populations" is demonstrating genuine historical understanding, not just recall.
2. What is the difference between a similarity that is merely interesting and a similarity that is historically significant? Give an example using the land-based empires.
A similarity is interesting if both things happen to share a feature. It is historically significant if the similarity reveals a pattern, has a common cause, or allows us to draw a broader conclusion. For example: both the Ottoman and Qing empires had elaborate court ceremonies. That's interesting. But the historically significant comparison is: both empires used monumental architecture and court ritual to project power because rulers universally recognized that legitimacy required visible demonstrations of greatness, not just force. That second version uses the specific similarity to make a claim about how imperial power worked generally in this period. Good AP comparison always asks: "So what? What does this similarity or difference reveal about the broader historical pattern?"
3. The AP exam says Topic 3.4 focuses on "argumentation", the skill of supporting a claim with specific evidence. What makes historical evidence "specific" rather than vague?
Specific evidence names particular people, events, places, dates, or institutional details that the reader can verify and that directly support the claim. Vague evidence uses general statements without details. Compare: vague, "The Ottoman Empire used religion to legitimize its rule." Specific, "The Ottoman sultan Selim I claimed the title of caliph after conquering Egypt in 1517, using this position as protector of Sunni Islam to justify his authority over the Islamic world and provide religious legitimacy beyond his military power." The specific version names the person (Selim I), the event (conquest of Egypt), the date (1517), the title claimed (caliph), and explains the political function (religious legitimacy beyond military force). Every claim in an AP essay should be supported by specific evidence of this quality.
4. The Ottoman, Mughal, and Safavid empires were all Muslim-ruled empires. Does their shared religion make them more similar to each other than to the non-Muslim Qing Empire, or are their differences more significant than this shared characteristic?
This is a genuine historical judgment question. The Ottoman-Mughal-Safavid shared Islam gives them some commonalities: Arabic-influenced administrative cultures, Islamic legal frameworks (sharia courts, qadis), Persian as a literary and administrative language for all three, similar legitimation strategies through Islamic identity. But their differences are also profound: the Ottomans and Safavids fought each other in devastating wars over the Sunni-Shi'a divide, shared religion didn't prevent bitter conflict. The Mughal approach to Islam was radically different from both (Akbar's tolerance vs. Ottoman caliphal orthodoxy vs. Safavid Shi'a coercion). All three used religion differently for legitimation. Meanwhile, the Qing used Confucian and Buddhist frameworks that, functionally, served many of the same political purposes as Islamic legitimation in the other three empires. In some ways, comparing administrative methods reveals more important similarities (all four used trained officials, all four built monumental architecture, all four extracted agricultural taxation) than comparing religion reveals differences. Good historical comparison always asks: similar or different in what respect, for what purpose?
5. Why is it more useful to compare the methods empires used rather than simply ranking empires from most to least powerful?
Ranking empires by power is a misleading exercise because "power" is multidimensional and context-dependent. The Ottoman Empire was militarily dominant in the eastern Mediterranean but had no presence in the Indian Ocean; the Mughal Empire controlled the world's most commercially productive territory but struggled with internal religious tensions; the Qing expanded to the greatest Chinese territorial extent but faced European military challenges by the 18th century. Comparing methods is more useful because it reveals patterns that transcend any individual empire: all successful land-based empires combined gunpowder military power with administrative sophistication and legitimation strategies. Understanding these patterns allows you to explain why empires succeeded or declined (those that couldn't extract sufficient revenue, or whose legitimation strategies failed, or whose military technology fell behind rivals, struggled regardless of their size). Comparative method is the tool for identifying these explanatory patterns.

Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: A term historians use for the Ottoman, Safavid, Mughal, and Ming/Qing empires, which all used gunpowder weapons (cannons, muskets) to conquer territory and consolidate power in the 15th–17th centuries. Significance: The CED requires comparison of how these empires expanded; gunpowder military technology is what they share, but they used different administrative and religious systems to maintain power.
  • Definition: The use of religion to justify political authority: Ottoman sultans claimed the Sunni caliphate, Safavid shahs enforced Twelver Shi'a Islam as state religion, Mughal emperors promoted a syncretic "Divine Faith," and European kings claimed divine right under Christianity. Significance: Key comparison point, all land-based empires used religion to legitimate power, but in very different ways that shaped their administrative choices.
  • Definition: A revenue system in which governments sell the right to collect taxes in a region to private individuals (tax farmers), who pay an upfront lump sum and then collect taxes themselves for profit. Significance: Used by both the Ottoman and Mughal Empires; explicitly named in the CED as a method empires used to administer diverse populations. Often led to corruption and local resentment.
  • Definition: The Ottoman system of recruiting (and converting) Christian boys from conquered Balkan populations to serve as soldiers (Janissaries) and administrators; these men owed total loyalty to the sultan, not to noble families. Significance: Named in the CED as an administrative innovation; contrasts with Mughal zamindar system and European hereditary aristocracy, illustrates different approaches to solving the "loyal official" problem.
  • Definition: An arrangement in which subordinate states or peoples pay regular tribute to a more powerful empire in exchange for protection or political autonomy; used by the Qing Dynasty with neighboring states and by the Mughal Empire with regional rulers. Significance: Cross-empire comparison point, most land-based empires in this period extracted tribute from peripheral territories rather than directly administering them.
The Reading

A Framework for Comparing Land-Based Empires

Before comparing the land-based empires of 1450–1750, it helps to establish what you're comparing them for. The AP Learning Objective asks: how did these empires "increase their influence"? That question points to four dimensions of comparison across all the empires in this unit:

💡 Fun Fact
The Mughal emperor Akbar was illiterate, yet he built one of the most sophisticated bureaucratic empires in the world! His court was a center of art, philosophy, and debate, and he established a Din-i-Ilahi (Divine Faith) blending elements of Islam, Hinduism, Zoroastrianism, and Christianity. Akbar's tolerant religious policies were so unusual that his own son rebelled against them, but the administrative reforms Akbar created outlasted the controversy and shaped Mughal governance for a century. 📚
  • Military expansion: How did they conquer territory? What technology, strategy, and organization enabled expansion?
  • Administrative consolidation: How did they govern conquered populations? What institutions and officials maintained control?
  • Legitimation: How did they make their rule seem rightful rather than merely forced? What religious, artistic, and cultural tools did they use?
  • Revenue extraction: How did they fund their armies, bureaucracies, and building projects? What tax systems did they develop?

The key insight for AP comparison: all four major land-based empires faced fundamentally similar challenges (governing diverse populations, funding large armies, maintaining central control against centrifugal regional forces), and they developed solutions in each of these categories. The similarities reveal the structural requirements of imperial power; the differences reveal how specific historical contexts shaped the specific solutions.

Comparing Methods of Expansion

What the comparison reveals: All four empires used gunpowder weapons in their expansion, but the degree and method varied significantly. The Ottoman and Mughal cases show the most direct military use of artillery in field battles and sieges. The Safavid case shows the cost of resisting technological innovation. The Qing case shows that military technology alone was insufficient, diplomatic legitimation (Buddhist ties to Mongolian leaders) was as important as firearms for securing their Inner Asian empire.

📝 AP Tip
The AP exam says imperial expansion "relied on the increased use of gunpowder, cannons, and armed trade." The key word is "relied". It doesn't say "was solely caused by." Strong AP arguments acknowledge the necessity of gunpowder while also explaining why other factors (administration, diplomacy, legitimation) were required for durable empire. The Safavid example is particularly useful for showing that rejecting gunpowder (Chaldiran, 1514) had devastating consequences, which demonstrates the technology's importance by negative example.

Comparing Methods of Administration

All four empires faced the same core administrative challenge: how to govern large, diverse populations without the information technology or transportation infrastructure we associate with modern states. Their solutions varied in the specific mechanisms used but shared the same underlying logic, create loyal agents, reduce the power of potential rivals, and extract sufficient revenue to fund the system.

The most dramatic parallel is between the Ottoman devshirme system and the Chinese civil service examination (continued by the Qing). Both produced trained officials whose position derived from merit and imperial appointment rather than hereditary privilege. Both reduced the power of traditional aristocracies by creating alternative paths to power through the imperial state. The difference: devshirme involved forced recruitment of non-Muslims who were converted; the Chinese examination was open to any literate male subject and was based on Confucian classical knowledge.

The Mughal mansabdari system assigned numerical ranks (mansabs) to all imperial officials and military commanders, with salary and military obligation tied to rank. Positions were not hereditary, at death, a mansabdar's rank died with him and property reverted to the emperor. This created officials entirely dependent on imperial service for their income and status.

The Qing maintained a dual administration: Manchu and Han Chinese officials occupied parallel positions at every level of government, ensuring no single ethnic group had autonomous control of any part of the bureaucracy. This was both a control mechanism (preventing any single Chinese official from building an independent regional power base) and a legitimation strategy (showing Confucian scholar-officials that their traditions would continue).

The Ottoman Janissaries (devshirme recruits) and the Qing Eight Banners (Manchu military units) represent different solutions to the same problem: maintaining a military force loyal to the central state rather than to regional lords or personal commanders. Janissaries were recruited from outside the Ottoman Muslim elite specifically to prevent their integration with potentially rival noble families. The Eight Banners maintained strict ethnic separation (Manchu, Mongol, and Han Chinese battalions were kept separate) to preserve the Manchu military identity that was the dynasty's power base.

Comparing Legitimation Strategies

What the comparison reveals: All empires used religion and monumental architecture for legitimation. The specific religions differed, but the function was identical: connecting the ruler's authority to a sacred order that transcended human law, and demonstrating material power through projects no private individual could match.

An important distinction worth noting in AP essays: the Mughal case shows that legitimation strategies could change within a single empire. Akbar's policy of religious tolerance (eliminating the jizya, appointing Hindus, creating the Din-i-Ilahi) and the later emperor Aurangzeb's orthodox Islamic policy (restoring the jizya, destroying Hindu temples) represent opposite approaches to legitimation within the same dynasty, and Aurangzeb's more coercive approach contributed to the rebellions that weakened the empire in the late 17th and early 18th centuries. This suggests that Akbar's tolerance was not merely idealism. It was strategically superior for governing a Hindu-majority empire.

Comparing Revenue Systems

All empires needed revenue; all developed tax systems adapted to their population and resources. The comparison reveals a spectrum from highly centralized direct taxation to decentralized intermediary-based collection:

  • Ottoman tax farming (iltizam): Decentralized, rights auctioned to private collectors; guaranteed revenue upfront but incentivized over-extraction by intermediaries.
  • Mughal zamindar system: Intermediary-based, hereditary local lords collected taxes and forwarded a share; Akbar's zabt assessment tried to standardize and regulate it.
  • Mexica tribute: Goods-based, subject city-states delivered specific items (cacao, textiles, feathers, gold) rather than currency; tracked in pictographic codices; combined economic extraction with symbolic submission.
  • Ming/Qing silver taxation: Currency-based, most consequential globally because silver demand connected China to American mines via the Manila Galleon trade; inadvertently created the first global economic system.

The key comparison for AP purposes: all these systems served the same function (generating revenue for the imperial center) but with different mechanisms and different consequences. The Ming silver taxation is unique in its global consequences, a domestic Chinese tax policy became a driver of global economic integration that no other tax system in this period equaled in scope.

Writing Comparative AP Arguments: A Step-by-Step Guide

Topic 3.4 focuses on the skill of argumentation, specifically, using specific evidence to support comparative claims. Here is a framework for constructing strong comparative arguments in LEQ and DBQ responses:

A strong comparative claim goes beyond "X and Y are both similar/different." It should: (a) name the specific dimension of comparison, (b) make a clear claim about similarity or difference, and (c) suggest the historical significance. Example: "Both the Ottoman and Mughal empires used religion as a tool of legitimation, but through fundamentally different methods, Ottoman caliphal authority versus Mughal religious tolerance, reflecting the distinct demographic challenges each faced in governing their subject populations."

Don't say "the Ottomans used religion." Say: "Ottoman Sultan Selim I claimed the title of caliph after conquering Egypt in 1517, using this position as defender of all Sunni Muslims to extend his legitimacy beyond purely political and military authority." Specific names, dates, events, and mechanisms are what differentiate scoring essays from mediocre ones.

Why were they similar? (Both faced the challenge of legitimizing rule over diverse populations, a structural requirement of empire.) Why were they different? (The Ottomans governed a majority-Muslim population for whom caliphal authority was meaningful; the Mughals governed a Hindu majority for whom Islamic legitimation was insufficient without tolerance.)

What does this comparison reveal about imperial power generally? Example: "The variation between Ottoman and Mughal legitimation strategies suggests that there was no single 'correct' approach to religious legitimation, rulers had to adapt their strategies to the specific religious composition of their subject populations, making flexibility and contextual sensitivity essential to successful imperial administration."

📝 AP Tip
The 2025 AP World History rubric awards the "complexity" point for essays that: (1) explain both similarity AND difference, (2) explain both cause AND effect, (3) explain both continuity AND change, (4) make connections across time periods, geographic areas, or themes, or (5) qualify or modify the main argument by considering diverse perspectives or evidence. For Unit 3 essays, the easiest complexity move is usually to connect to another unit: the Mongol Empire (Unit 2) provided the political vacuum that enabled the gunpowder empires; the silver trade driven by Ming taxation (Unit 3) connects directly to the Columbian Exchange and Atlantic economy (Unit 4).

"Historians who study land-based empires of 1450–1750 often note both striking similarities and important differences in their methods of expansion and governance. All four major empires (Ottoman, Mughal, Safavid, Qing) used gunpowder weapons and developed centralized administrative systems; yet each adapted these tools to its specific geographic, demographic, and cultural context in ways that produced distinct imperial formations."

Answer parts A, B, and C. Each part requires a separate, direct response. Do not write an essay.

Part A

Briefly describe ONE similarity in the methods two different land-based empires used to legitimize and consolidate political power in the period 1450–1750.

Both the Ottoman Empire and the Mughal Empire used monumental architectural projects to legitimize and display imperial power. The Ottomans commissioned magnificent mosques, most famously the Süleymaniye Mosque in Istanbul (1550s) and the Blue Mosque (1609), that demonstrated the empire's wealth, the sultan's piety, and Istanbul's status as the center of the Islamic world. Similarly, the Mughals built architectural masterpieces like the Taj Mahal (completed 1653) and Fatehpur Sikri that combined Islamic aesthetic traditions with Indian craftsmanship. In both cases, the scale of construction communicated that no rival ruler commanded comparable resources, and the religious character of the buildings associated imperial authority with divine favor. Both empires understood that visible, permanent architectural achievement was a form of political communication that outlasted any military campaign.

Part B

Briefly explain ONE significant difference in the methods two different land-based empires used to administer diverse subject populations in the period 1450–1750.

A significant difference in administrative method between the Ottoman and Mughal empires was their approach to governing non-Muslim subjects. The Ottomans administered religious diversity through the millet system, which granted recognized religious communities (Orthodox Christians, Armenian Christians, Jews) autonomy to govern their own internal affairs, marriage, inheritance, religious practice, under their own religious leaders, in exchange for loyalty and tax payment (including the jizya). This system maintained clear hierarchical distinction between Muslim and non-Muslim subjects. By contrast, Mughal emperor Akbar pursued active integration: he abolished the jizya entirely, married Hindu Rajput princesses and incorporated their families into the imperial nobility, and appointed Hindus to the highest administrative positions based on merit. Where the Ottomans tolerated difference through separation, Akbar sought to dissolve it through inclusion, a difference reflecting both personal philosophy and strategic necessity in an empire where the ruled (Hindu majority) vastly outnumbered the rulers (Muslim minority).

Part C

Briefly explain how comparing the methods of two land-based empires reveals a broader pattern about imperial governance in the period 1450–1750.

Comparing the Ottoman devshirme system and the Qing dual Manchu-Han administration reveals a broader pattern: successful land-based empires consistently sought to create administrative classes dependent on central imperial authority rather than on independent power bases. The Ottoman devshirme produced officials (Janissaries and palace administrators) who owed their entire status to the sultan's favor, having no family lands, dynastic claims, or regional armies. The Qing positioned Manchu officials alongside Han Chinese officials at every administrative level, ensuring neither group controlled any part of the bureaucracy autonomously. Despite operating in very different cultural and religious contexts, one using forced conversion of Christian boys, the other maintaining ethnic separation, both empires were solving the identical problem: preventing regional administrators from accumulating enough independent power to challenge central authority. This parallel suggests that the structural challenge of preventing administrative fragmentation was a universal feature of pre-modern empire that transcended specific cultural or religious contexts.
Compare the methods by which TWO land-based empires consolidated and maintained their power in the period 1450 to 1750.

Write a full essay with thesis, contextualization, evidence, and historical reasoning. This prompt requires comparison, identify at least one similarity AND one difference, and explain the significance of each. Historical reasoning skill: Comparison / Argumentation.

Strong thesis (Ottoman vs. Mughal): "Although the Ottoman and Mughal empires both used gunpowder weapons and monumental architecture to expand and legitimize their power, they differed significantly in their approach to governing religiously diverse populations, the Ottomans through the millet system's formal separation of communities and the Mughals under Akbar through active inclusion of Hindu elites, differences that reflected their distinct demographic challenges and had divergent long-term consequences for imperial stability."

  • Contextualization: Describe the post-Mongol political landscape of 1400–1450: the collapse of the Mongol Empire had created power vacuums across Eurasia; gunpowder technology had matured; trade routes had recovered. This context explains why multiple new imperial powers emerged simultaneously using similar tools.
  • Similarity evidence: Both used gunpowder weapons (Ottoman cannons at Constantinople; Mughal artillery at Panipat); both built monumental Islamic architecture as legitimation (Ottoman mosques in Istanbul; Mughal Taj Mahal and Fatehpur Sikri); both developed tax collection intermediary systems (Ottoman tax farming; Mughal zamindars); both created centralized administrative classes (Ottoman devshirme; Mughal mansabdari).
  • Difference evidence: Ottoman millet system maintained clear Muslim/non-Muslim hierarchy with taxation of non-Muslims (jizya) vs. Akbar's elimination of jizya, Hindu appointments, and policy of religious tolerance; Ottoman expansion through Mediterranean naval power vs. Mughal primarily land-based conquest of the subcontinent; Ottoman claim to caliphate as universal Sunni authority vs. Mughal religious synthesis across Hindu-Muslim divide.
  • Significance of differences: The Mughal tolerance strategy was more sustainable for governing a Hindu majority. Aurangzeb's reversal to Orthodox Islamic policy triggered the rebellions that ultimately fragmented the empire, suggesting Akbar's approach was strategically superior. The Ottoman millet system was more stable for a diverse Mediterranean empire where clear religious boundaries were already established through centuries of coexistence.
  • Complexity/synthesis: Connect to Unit 2 (both empires built on Mongol and Timurid cultural legacies) or Unit 4 (both empires' silver demands connected to the same global silver trade network).
Model answer: The most important shared features are structural, arising from the shared challenge of governing large, diverse populations in a pre-modern world. All four: used gunpowder weapons (most prominently cannons and firearms) as key tools of military expansion; developed centralized administrative systems that attempted to reduce the independent power of regional elites; used religious authority and monumental architecture to legitimize the ruler's power as sacred rather than merely political; developed tax collection systems to extract revenue from agricultural populations; and faced the challenge of state rivalries that required constant military readiness. These shared features reflect the structural requirements of empire rather than cultural diffusion from one empire to another, the Qing didn't learn their administrative methods from the Ottomans, yet they solved similar problems with similar tools because the problems themselves were similar. This is a key point for the AP: parallel solutions to parallel challenges don't require cultural contact to explain. They arise from structural necessities.
Model answer: The most historically significant difference is arguably the Mughal Empire's experiment with religious tolerance under Akbar versus the other empires' generally more exclusive approaches to religious identity. While the Ottomans used the millet system (tolerating but hierarchically separating non-Muslims), the Safavids forced mass conversion to Shi'a Islam, and the Qing maintained ethnic separation. Akbar genuinely attempted to build an empire that transcended religious identity, creating a court culture where Hindu and Muslim traditions contributed equally. This matters historically for several reasons: it produced the most culturally creative and economically prosperous period in Mughal history; it provided a model of multicultural imperial governance that was not replicated by contemporaries; and the decision to reverse it under Aurangzeb directly contributed to the rebellions that fragmented the empire. The Mughal case thus offers a historical experiment in the consequences of tolerance versus enforcement, with measurable outcomes: tolerance (Akbar's reign, 1556–1605) → stability and expansion; enforcement (Aurangzeb's reign, 1658–1707) → territorial maximum followed by fragmentation. This makes it one of the clearest historical tests of the relationship between religious policy and imperial stability.
Model answer: Comparing 1450–1750 land-based empires with earlier examples reveals both striking continuities and important changes. Continuities: all empires used religious legitimation (the Song Dynasty in Unit 1 used Confucianism; the Abbasid Caliphate used Islamic authority; Unit 3 empires used Islam, Confucianism, and divine right); all extracted taxes from agricultural populations; all built monumental architecture to display power; all faced the challenge of governing cultural diversity. Changes: the specific role of gunpowder weapons was genuinely new in the 1450–1750 period, the Mongol Empire of Unit 2 relied primarily on cavalry, not cannons. The scale and commercial integration of Unit 3 empires was also new: the Mughal Empire's connection to global silver networks and the Ottoman Empire's connection to Mediterranean commerce represented a degree of economic integration that earlier empires had not achieved. The Protestant and Catholic Reformations represented genuinely new religious dynamics, the Roman Church had not faced this kind of institutional split since the East-West schism of 1054. And the Safavid establishment of a Shi'a state was a novelty within Islamic history. So: the tools and challenges of empire showed remarkable continuity, but the specific religious landscape, technological arsenal, and global commercial connections were significantly new.
Model answer: This argument is a starting point, not a finished comparison. To make it historically sophisticated, you would need to: (1) Specify exactly how the gunpowder use differed. Ottoman artillery was decisive at Constantinople in 1453; Mughal artillery was field-based at Panipat 1526; the Safavids initially rejected firearms and suffered catastrophic defeat at Chaldiran 1514; the Qing combined Manchu cavalry with diplomacy as much as cannons. The fact that all four used gunpowder doesn't mean they used it the same way or depended on it equally. (2) Differentiate the religious legitimation strategies, the Ottoman claim to the Sunni caliphate (Selim I, 1517) is fundamentally different in function from Akbar's religious tolerance policy, which is fundamentally different from the Safavid forced conversion to Shi'a Islam, which is different from Qing Buddhist diplomacy with Mongolian leaders. All used religion, but each used it differently in response to a different religious demographic situation. (3) Add a "so what", why does the similarity matter? Because it reveals that military technology and religious legitimation are structural requirements of any large pre-modern empire, not choices unique to these four states. And why do the differences matter? Because they reveal how specific historical contexts (demographic, geographic, religious) shaped the specific implementations of these universal imperial tools.
