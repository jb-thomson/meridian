7.1 Shifting Power After 1900

🏛️ Political🌍 State Building⚙️ Technology & Change👥 Nationalism
  • LO-A: Three great empires collapsed, 1911–1923: The Ottoman, Russian, and Qing empires, all land-based, multi-ethnic, and struggling with modernization, collapsed within roughly a decade. Their collapse was not coincidental: industrialization had widened the military and economic gap between them and Western powers, while internal reformers and nationalist movements pushed from within. Know the specific internal AND external factors for each. Russia's path: war → revolution → communism: The Russian Empire was severely weakened by WWI defeats (1914–1917), which discredited Tsar Nicholas II. The February Revolution (1917) ended the Romanov dynasty; the October Revolution (1917) brought the Bolsheviks to power. The result was the world's first communist state, a development that shaped all of 20th-century world history. Qing China: internal crisis + foreign pressure: The Qing Dynasty was weakened by the Opium Wars, Taiping Rebellion (20–30 million dead), Boxer Rebellion, and unequal treaties that gave foreign powers extraterritorial rights. In 1911, the Republican Revolution ended 2,000 years of imperial rule in China, though the republic was weak and soon consumed by warlordism. Mexico: revolution from political crisis: The Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) began as a political crisis when dictator Porfirio Díaz refused to allow free elections, then expanded into a social revolution over land, labor rights, and indigenous rights. It produced a new constitution (1917) guaranteeing land reform, workers' rights, and restrictions on foreign ownership, a model for later 20th-century revolutions.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. The Ottoman, Russian, and Qing empires all collapsed between 1911 and 1923. What common structural weakness did all three share that made them vulnerable to collapse in the industrial age?
All three were large, multi-ethnic land-based empires struggling to modernize fast enough to compete with industrialized Western powers. They faced the same core dilemma: industrialization required centralization, educated bureaucracies, and capital investment, but their traditional power structures (sultans, tsars, emperors) and entrenched elites resisted the changes needed to modernize. They were also stretched thin: defending vast borders against industrialized military powers required resources they couldn't generate. External military defeats exposed internal weakness, and internal nationalist/reformist movements exploited that weakness. The timing was not coincidental, the same forces that built Western imperial power were undermining these traditional empires simultaneously.
2. Why did military defeat in WWI specifically trigger revolution in Russia, rather than in other countries that also suffered wartime losses?
Russia's military losses were catastrophic, by 1917 Russia had suffered approximately 5 million casualties, the army was collapsing from desertion and mutiny, and food supply to cities had broken down. But the deeper reason is that Russia's political system offered no legitimate avenue for change. Autocratic tsarism had refused to share power with the Duma (parliament), so frustration had no outlet except revolution. When the tsar lost both military legitimacy and the support of the army, there was nothing left to sustain his rule. In Germany, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire, the war also triggered political collapse, so Russia's experience was actually one instance of a broader pattern where WWI destroyed regimes that couldn't survive military catastrophe.
3. The Mexican Revolution is described as arising from a "political crisis." Why might political crises produce revolutions rather than merely political reforms?
Revolutions typically happen when political crisis combines with: (1) deep social grievances that reform alone can't address, (2) a breakdown of the state's ability to enforce order, and (3) organized opposition with an alternative vision. In Mexico's case, Díaz's refusal to allow elections blocked the political avenue for change, so opponents had no legitimate path to power. Once violence began and the army's loyalty became uncertain, the state could no longer suppress opposition. The political crisis exposed underlying social tensions, land concentration in a few hands, indigenous dispossession, abusive labor systems, that couldn't be resolved by merely replacing one president with another. The combination of political illegitimacy + social grievance + state weakness produced revolution rather than reform.
4. The AP CED says the West "dominated the global political order at the beginning of the 20th century" but that empires "gave way to new states by the century's end." What does this suggest about how the 20th century as a whole should be understood?
It suggests the 20th century was fundamentally a period of transition from a world organized around European empires to a world organized around independent nation-states. At the start (1900), Western Europe controlled approximately 84% of the earth's land surface and dominated global trade, finance, and military force. By the end (2000), the vast majority of the world's population lived in independent states, the result of collapse of the Ottoman, Russian, Austro-Hungarian, and Qing empires in the first quarter of the century, defeat of Nazi and Japanese imperialism in WWII, and decolonization of Africa and Asia in the 1950s–1970s. Topic 7.1 covers the first wave of this transition; later units cover the rest.
5. All three empires (Ottoman, Russian, Qing) had attempted modernization reforms before they collapsed. Why did reform fail to save these empires?
Reform failed for different reasons in each case, but a common pattern was that reforms were too slow, too partial, or too threatening to vested interests to transform the empire before external and internal pressures overwhelmed it. The Ottoman Tanzimat reforms (1839–1876) modernized the bureaucracy and military but couldn't overcome financial dependence on foreign loans or internal nationalist movements in the Balkans. China's Self-Strengthening Movement and later New Policies attempted industrialization but were blocked by conservative officials and couldn't undo the structural damage of unequal treaties. Russia's Stolypin reforms (1906–1911) modernized agriculture but came too late and didn't address the fundamental political problem of autocracy. Reform is hard under pressure: the time and resources needed to transform institutions are consumed by the crises that reform is supposed to prevent.

The Reading

🌍 The World in 1900: European Dominance. But Not for Long

In 1900, the world looked like a European project. Britain, France, Germany, and other Western powers controlled roughly 84% of the earth's land surface through formal empires and informal economic dominance. European science, technology, and military power seemed unstoppable. But beneath this picture of dominance, powerful forces were already at work, forces that would shatter multiple empires within a single generation.

The AP CED frames Unit 7 around a crucial observation: the West dominated global politics at the start of the 20th century, but both land-based and maritime empires gave way to new states by the century's end. Topic 7.1 covers the first wave of that transformation, the collapse of three great land-based empires and the emergence of revolution as a political force.

💡 Fun Fact
In 1905, Japan defeated Russia in the Russo-Japanese War, the first time in the modern era that an Asian power defeated a European power in a major conflict. The news shocked the world. It proved that European military dominance was not inevitable, and inspired anti-colonial movements from Egypt to India to China. Japan's victory was partly the result of industrialization: its navy used modern steel warships; its army used the most up-to-date artillery. Industrialization had created the technology gap, but industrialization had also begun to close it.

🕌 The Ottoman Empire: Slow Collapse of the "Sick Man"

By 1900, the Ottoman Empire had been in crisis for decades. European observers called it the "Sick Man of Europe", a massive but financially weak state that had been losing territory since the 18th century. The empire's problems were structural: it was a multi-ethnic state (Turks, Arabs, Armenians, Greeks, Bulgarians, Kurds, and dozens of other groups) in an age when nationalism was pulling those groups apart. It was also financially dependent on European loans, which gave European powers leverage over Ottoman policy.

The final decades of the empire saw two competing responses: reform and repression. The Young Turks (Committee of Union and Progress) staged a successful coup in 1908, pushing for modernization and constitutional government. But they increasingly defined the empire in Turkish nationalist terms, which alienated Arab, Armenian, and Greek subjects. WWI was fatal: the Ottomans joined the Central Powers, suffered massive military defeats, and saw their Arab territories rise in revolt (supported by Britain). The empire was formally dissolved in 1923, replaced by the Turkish Republic under Mustafa Kemal (Atatürk).

📝 AP Tip
The AP exam asks you to identify both internal AND external factors in the collapse of these empires. For the Ottomans: internal factors = nationalism, financial weakness, reformers vs. conservatives; external factors = military defeats (Balkan Wars, WWI), European imperial pressure, British support for Arab revolt. Always give both sides.

🐉 Qing China: Two Thousand Years Ends

The Qing Dynasty ruled China for nearly three centuries (1644–1912), but the 19th century had been a catastrophe. The Opium Wars (1839–42, 1856–60) forced China to open its ports to European trade and grant extraterritorial rights to foreign nationals, meaning British, French, and American citizens in China were exempt from Chinese law. The Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864), one of the deadliest civil wars in human history, with an estimated 20–30 million dead, left the dynasty financially devastated. The Boxer Rebellion (1900) and its crushing defeat by a multinational European army humiliated the Qing further.

China's reformers tried. The Self-Strengthening Movement (1861–1895) attempted to adopt Western military technology while preserving Chinese culture and institutions, "Chinese learning for the substance, Western learning for the application." But Japan's defeat of China in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95) proved the reform had not gone far enough. In 1911, military uprisings triggered the Republican Revolution: the last Qing emperor, the six-year-old Puyi, abdicated. Two thousand years of imperial rule in China ended.

🚩 Russia: From Empire to Communist Revolution

The Russian Empire in 1900 was the largest state in the world by territory, stretching from Poland to the Pacific, but it was economically underdeveloped compared to Western Europe. Industrialization had begun but was concentrated in a few cities; most Russians were still peasant farmers. The political system was autocratic: Tsar Nicholas II ruled by divine right and resisted any meaningful constitutional limits on his power.

Three crisis points broke the empire:

  • 1905 Revolution: Russia's humiliating defeat in the Russo-Japanese War triggered strikes, peasant uprisings, and the "Bloody Sunday" massacre (soldiers shot peaceful protesters). Nicholas II promised a parliament (Duma) to restore order, but then steadily undermined its power.
  • World War I (1914–1917): Russia suffered catastrophic losses, approximately 5 million casualties by 1917. The army began to disintegrate; food supply to cities collapsed. The tsar's personal command of the military made him personally responsible for every defeat.
  • The Revolutions of 1917: The February Revolution (February/March 1917), spontaneous workers' strikes in Petrograd, led to Nicholas II's abdication. A Provisional Government took power but made the fatal decision to continue WWI. The October Revolution (October/November 1917) brought the Bolsheviks under Vladimir Lenin to power. Lenin's promise of "Peace, Land, Bread", ending the war, redistributing land, feeding the cities, gave the Bolsheviks mass support. Russia withdrew from WWI, and the world's first communist state was born.
💡 Fun Fact
The Romanov dynasty had ruled Russia for 304 years when Nicholas II abdicated in 1917. The tsar, his wife Alexandra, and their five children (including the famous hemophiliac Alexei) were executed by Bolshevik guards in July 1918. DNA analysis in the 1990s finally confirmed the identity of all seven bodies. The Romanovs had survived countless crises, Napoleon's invasion, the Crimean War, the 1905 Revolution, but WWI proved fatal. Nicholas II's stubborn refusal to share power with parliament or listen to advisors is one of history's great examples of political self-destruction.

🌮 Mexico: Revolution from Political Crisis

Mexico's path to revolution was different from the imperial collapses. It was not a multi-ethnic empire but a republic, and the crisis was specifically political. Porfirio Díaz had ruled Mexico as a dictator for 35 years (the Porfiriato, 1876–1911), modernizing the economy through foreign investment while concentrating land in the hands of a small elite and using the army to suppress opposition. Mexico had railroads, mining operations, and export agriculture, but most Mexicans remained desperately poor.

When Díaz rigged the 1910 election to prevent reform candidate Francisco Madero from winning, the country exploded. The Mexican Revolution (1910–1920) became a complex, multi-sided conflict with competing visions:

  • Emiliano Zapata fought for indigenous villages' land rights in southern Mexico: "Land and Liberty."
  • Pancho Villa led a popular northern army demanding economic justice for peasants and workers.
  • Conservative elites fought to preserve their power and property.

The Revolution produced the Constitution of 1917, one of the most progressive constitutions in the world at the time. It guaranteed land reform, workers' rights (8-hour day, minimum wage), restrictions on foreign ownership of Mexican resources, and secular education. It was a genuinely radical document that shaped Mexican politics for the rest of the century.

📝 AP Tip
The Mexican Revolution is the AP's primary example of states "challeng[ing] the existing political and social order" (KC-6.2.II.D). Know that it was triggered by political crisis (Díaz's election fraud) but expanded into social revolution over land and labor. The Constitution of 1917 is the key outcome. Don't confuse it with the Russian Revolution. They were happening simultaneously (1910–1917) but had different causes and ideological roots.

  • Ideological opposition from communist revolutionary movements
  • Economic competition from Japan's newly industrialized economy
  • Military defeats by industrialized foreign powers that exposed structural weaknesses

Both the Ottomans and the Qing suffered humiliating military defeats by industrialized foreign powers (Ottomans: Balkan Wars, WWI; Qing: Opium Wars, Sino-Japanese War, Boxer Rebellion/multinational intervention) that exposed their inability to compete militarily and economically with industrial states. These defeats accelerated internal reform movements and eventually triggered collapse.
The Russian, Ottoman, and Qing empires all collapsed in the early 20th century. What does this pattern most directly support?
  • Industrialization created structural pressures that land-based multi-ethnic empires were unable to successfully navigate
  • Democratic ideology inevitably undermines all forms of imperial government
  • World War I was the sole cause of empire collapse in the early 20th century
  • Marxist revolutionary theory was primarily responsible for political instability across Eurasia
The common thread is structural: all three were large, multi-ethnic, land-based empires that struggled to industrialize fast enough to compete militarily and economically. Industrialization widened the gap; nationalism pulled diverse populations apart; and the financial and military strain of maintaining vast empires in the industrial age proved unsustainable.
Using specific evidence, explain ONE internal factor and ONE external factor that contributed to the collapse of the Russian Empire by 1917.
Internal factor: The Russian autocracy's refusal to share power created a political system with no legitimate mechanism for addressing grievances. Tsar Nicholas II undermined the Duma (parliament) after creating it in 1905, ensuring that reform demands had no outlet except revolution. Combined with widespread peasant poverty and urban worker exploitation, this political rigidity made the empire brittle under pressure. External factor: Russia's catastrophic performance in World War I was the proximate trigger of the 1917 revolutions. By early 1917, Russia had suffered approximately 5 million casualties, the army was collapsing from desertion and mutiny, food supply to major cities had broken down, and the tsar's personal command of the military made him personally responsible for every defeat. WWI turned a politically unstable situation into an acute crisis that the autocracy could not survive.
Evaluate the relative importance of internal versus external factors in causing the collapse of ONE of the following empires: the Ottoman Empire, the Qing Dynasty, or the Russian Empire.
While external shocks, especially Russia's catastrophic defeats in World War I, provided the immediate trigger for revolution in 1917, the deeper cause of the Russian Empire's collapse was internal: the autocratic political system's structural inability to accommodate modernization, reform demands, or the political participation of an increasingly urbanized and educated population, which left the regime with no reserves of legitimacy when military disaster struck.
Model answer: Multi-ethnic empires are held together by loyalty to a dynasty, religion, or imperial idea rather than to a shared ethnicity or national identity. When nationalism becomes the dominant framework for political legitimacy, when people believe that government should represent "a people" defined by language and ethnicity, multi-ethnic empires immediately face a legitimacy problem. Every minority ethnic group within the empire has grounds to claim its own state. The Ottoman Empire faced this in the Balkans (Greeks, Serbs, Bulgarians, Romanians all demanded independence); the Qing faced it from Han Chinese nationalists; the Russian Empire faced it from Poles, Ukrainians, Finns, and others. A nation-state defined around a single ethnic group doesn't have this problem, there's no minority demanding its own state. The more the empire tries to respond by emphasizing one group's identity (as the Young Turks did with Turkish nationalism), the more it alienates the other groups. It's a structural trap.
Model answer: The slogan reveals that the Bolsheviks succeeded by addressing the three most immediate and overwhelming needs of ordinary Russians in 1917. "Peace", because Russia had suffered catastrophic losses in WWI and most soldiers and their families desperately wanted the war to end. "Land", because most Russians were peasants who farmed land they didn't own, and land redistribution had been the central demand of Russian peasants for generations. "Bread", because the war had disrupted food supply to cities, causing genuine starvation in Petrograd and Moscow. The simplicity is itself revealing: the Bolsheviks' appeal was not primarily ideological (most Russian peasants had no idea what Marxism meant) but material. They offered concrete solutions to immediate crises. The Provisional Government failed precisely because it chose to continue the war (losing "Peace") and delayed land reform (losing "Land") while failing to stabilize food supply (losing "Bread").
Model answer: The most important difference is ideological: the Mexican Revolution was a nationalist-agrarian revolution that sought a more equitable Mexican capitalism, not the abolition of capitalism. The Constitution of 1917 guaranteed land reform and workers' rights but maintained private property and a mixed economy. The Russian Revolution was a Marxist revolution that explicitly sought to abolish capitalism and establish a communist state, eliminating private ownership of the means of production. Mexico produced a reformed capitalist republic; Russia produced a communist state. A secondary difference is in origin: the Mexican Revolution began as a political crisis (rigged elections) that revealed underlying social grievances; the Russian Revolution was accelerated by military catastrophe (WWI) that exposed an already unstable system. Both were responses to modernization's unequal distribution of its costs and benefits, but they produced very different models of how to organize society.
Model answer: Calling it a "global" crisis reflects the fact that the same underlying forces produced similar outcomes across very different regions simultaneously. Industrialization had widened the military and economic gap between industrial states and non-industrial ones, putting pressure on all traditional empires. WWI, itself a product of imperial competition, provided the military shock that broke states already weakened by this structural pressure. The Bolshevik Revolution inspired socialist movements in Germany, Hungary, and China simultaneously. The dissolution of the Ottoman, Russian, Austro-Hungarian, and Qing empires all happened within roughly a decade, not because they were connected by direct causation, but because they were all facing the same structural forces: nationalism, industrialization-driven military inequality, and the political crisis of autocracy in a modernizing world. Treating these as unrelated national stories misses the larger pattern.
Model answer: The word "eventually" captures the contingency of historical outcomes, the Bolshevik takeover was not inevitable just because the Russian Empire was weak. The February Revolution (1917) did not produce communism; it produced a liberal Provisional Government that could have moved toward parliamentary democracy. The October Revolution happened because the Provisional Government made specific bad choices (continuing WWI) and because the Bolsheviks were an unusually disciplined, ideologically committed minority party that outmaneuvered larger but less organized socialist rivals. A different set of decisions in 1917 could have produced a different outcome. History is full of cases where empires collapsed without producing communist revolutions. Mexico, the Ottoman Empire, and China all underwent imperial collapse without becoming communist (China came later, for separate reasons). The word "eventually" also signals a time delay: the empire's structural weaknesses developed over decades, but the specific trigger (WWI defeats) and the specific outcome (Bolshevik victory) depended on contingent events.
