6.2 State Expansion from 1750 to 1900

🏛️ Political💰 Economics🌍 Interactions
  • LO-A: From company to state control, corporations give way to governments: The British East India Company governed India for nearly two centuries before the 1857 Rebellion prompted Britain to transfer control to the Crown. Similarly, Leopold II’s personal Congo Free State became Belgian state property in 1908 after international outrage. This transition, non-state to state control, is KC-5.2.I.A. The Scramble for Africa and the Berlin Conference (1884–85): European powers carved up Africa so rapidly that they needed a formal conference in Berlin to divide territory without triggering war among themselves. By 1914, only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent. Britain dominated West Africa and East Africa; France controlled West and Central Africa; Belgium held the Congo. Asia and the Pacific. European, American, and Japanese expansion: Britain formalized control over India after 1857. The U.S. acquired the Philippines, Guam, and Puerto Rico after the 1898 Spanish-American War. Japan, after the Meiji Restoration, seized Taiwan (1895) and Korea (1910). Russia expanded eastward across Siberia to the Pacific. Settler colonies in New Zealand and Australia displaced indigenous peoples permanently. US and Russian land empires, continent-spanning expansion: American Manifest Destiny drove westward expansion across North America: the Mexican-American War (1846–48) added Texas, California, and the Southwest; the Indian Removal Act forced indigenous nations from their lands. Russia’s Trans-Siberian Railroad (completed 1916) bound its continental empire together. Both used warfare, diplomacy, and mass settlement to consolidate vast territories.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Why might a private trading company (like the British East India Company) eventually need to be replaced by direct government control?
Trading companies prioritize profit; governments must consider security, diplomacy, and political stability. A company might exploit a colony so aggressively that it provokes rebellion, exactly what happened in India in 1857, when the East India Company’s practices sparked the Sepoy Rebellion. A company also lacks the legitimacy and resources of a sovereign state when it comes to suppressing large-scale uprisings, negotiating international treaties, or making long-term infrastructure investments. As colonies grew more complex and contested, the limited mandate of a trading company became inadequate, full state power was needed.
2. The Berlin Conference of 1884–85 divided Africa among European powers. Why would European nations agree to divide Africa peacefully rather than fight over it?
European nations agreed to divide Africa at the conference because the alternative, war between industrial powers over colonial territory, would have been far more costly than negotiating shares. Bismarck (who hosted the conference) understood that European peace depended on managing colonial competition through diplomacy. Each power got something; no power got everything; conflict was avoided. The irony is that this “peaceful” division was only possible because African peoples’ interests were entirely ignored, the conference treated the continent as uninhabited, unclaimed territory despite the presence of hundreds of millions of people with their own political structures.
3. How was Japanese imperial expansion in Asia similar to and different from European imperial expansion in Africa?
Similar: Japan used military force and unequal treaties to seize territory from weaker neighbors, just as European powers did in Africa. Japan also used ideological justifications (a “Greater East Asia” narrative) to present expansion as regional leadership. Different: Japan was an Asian nation that industrialized rapidly and successfully competed with European powers. It was not itself colonized. Japan’s empire was geographically adjacent (Korea, Taiwan, Manchuria) rather than transoceanic. European expansion also involved settler colonialism on a much larger scale; Japanese expansion focused more on political and economic control over neighboring territories.
4. American expansion across North America is sometimes not called “imperialism.” What arguments support or challenge that description?
Arguments that it was imperialism: the U.S. used military force to displace indigenous populations, signed treaties it then violated, conducted the Trail of Tears and similar forced removals, and seized half of Mexico’s territory in the Mexican-American War. These are structural equivalents to what European powers did in Africa. Arguments against calling it imperialism: the U.S. expansion was contiguous (not transoceanic), and Americans framed it as national destiny rather than foreign conquest. However, the framing matters less than the structure: conquest of indigenous peoples, seizure of territory, and displacement of existing inhabitants are the defining features of imperialism regardless of geography.
5. What is the difference between a settler colony and an exploitation colony? Why does this distinction matter?
A settler colony (e.g., New Zealand, Australia, South Africa, Algeria) is one where large numbers of colonizers permanently relocate, often displacing indigenous populations from their land. An exploitation colony (e.g., British Nigeria, French Indochina) is one where a small colonial administration governs a large indigenous population primarily for economic extraction, without mass permanent settlement. The distinction matters because settler colonies produce permanent demographic transformation, indigenous peoples are not just governed but displaced and sometimes eliminated, while exploitation colonies maintain a larger indigenous population under foreign political control. The long-term consequences are different: settler colonial societies continue to wrestle with indigenous land rights and demographic displacement in ways exploitation colonies do not.

The Reading

🏢 From Company to Crown: The Transition from Private to State Empire

European expansion did not begin as a government project. For much of the 17th and 18th centuries, it was managed by joint-stock trading companies: the British East India Company, the Dutch East India Company (VOC), and similar enterprises that held royal charters granting monopoly trading rights. These companies were private corporations with their own armies, courts, and administrative systems.

By the mid-19th century, this arrangement was breaking down. The CED identifies this transition as KC-5.2.I.A: some states strengthened their control over existing colonies, including the transition from non-state actor control to direct state governance. The two most important examples are India and the Congo.

In India, the British East India Company had gradually extended control over the subcontinent since the Battle of Plassey (1757). By 1857, it governed 200 million people. But the Sepoy Rebellion of 1857, a massive uprising by Indian soldiers (sepoys) and civilians against Company rule, shocked the British government into action. Parliament dissolved the Company and transferred India to direct Crown control in 1858, beginning the era of the British Raj. India became the “jewel in the crown” of the British Empire.

In the Congo, the transition went the other direction: King Leopold II of Belgium acquired the Congo Free State as a personal possession (not a Belgian colony) at the Berlin Conference of 1885. Leopold’s regime used forced labor and extreme violence to extract rubber, killing an estimated 10 million people. International outrage, documented by journalist E.D. Morel and human rights activist Roger Casement, forced Leopold to cede the Congo to the Belgian state in 1908, making it the Belgian Congo.

💡 Fun Fact
The British East India Company had its own private army, at its peak, it commanded over 260,000 soldiers, twice the size of the British Army itself. The Company governed India for nearly 200 years, collecting taxes, administering courts, and fighting wars on behalf of profit. When the Sepoy Rebellion exposed the limits of corporate governance, Britain had to step in with its actual government.

🌍 The Scramble for Africa: The Berlin Conference

Between 1880 and 1914, European powers colonized almost all of Africa in what historians call the Scramble for Africa. The speed was astonishing: in 1880, Europeans controlled about 10% of Africa; by 1914, they controlled about 90%, with only Ethiopia and Liberia maintaining independence.

To manage this explosive competition without triggering a European war, Otto von Bismarck of Germany hosted the Berlin Conference in 1884–85. Fourteen European nations (and the United States as an observer) participated. Zero African representatives were invited or consulted. The conference established the principle of “effective occupation”: a nation could claim African territory only if it established actual administrative presence, not merely a flag on a coast. This accelerated the rush to send troops, administrators, and missionaries inland.

The resulting map bore almost no relationship to existing African political boundaries. European borders sliced through ethnic communities, separated related peoples, and lumped together groups with different languages, religions, and histories. These arbitrary borders remain one of the most lasting legacies of colonialism in Africa today.

Key acquisitions included: Britain in Nigeria, Kenya, Uganda, Egypt, and South Africa; France in West Africa and Central Africa; Belgium in the Congo; Germany in Namibia, Tanzania, and Cameroon; Portugal in Angola and Mozambique.

📝 AP Tip
Know the two major types of state expansion identified in KC-5.2.I: (1) European states expanded into Africa and Asia, often replacing existing company or local rule with direct state administration; and (2) the U.S., Russia, and Japan expanded by conquering neighboring territories (KC-5.2.II.B). This is a separate pattern. The AP exam may ask you to compare colonial expansion in Africa with continental expansion in the Americas or Eurasia.

🌏 Asia and the Pacific: Multiple Powers Compete

While Europe scrambled for Africa, imperial expansion in Asia and the Pacific involved more complex competition. The CED identifies KC-5.2.I.B: European states, the U.S., and Japan acquired territories in Asia and the Pacific.

British India became the model of colonial governance after 1858. Britain governed 300 million people through a combination of British administrators and Indian civil servants, building an extensive railway network (built with Indian labor for British benefit), and extracting cotton, tea, and opium. India was the foundation of British global power.

In Southeast Asia, the Dutch consolidated control over the Netherlands East Indies (Indonesia), France conquered Indochina (Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos), Britain took Burma and Malaya, and the U.S. acquired the Philippines after defeating Spain in 1898. The Spanish-American War transformed the U.S. from a continental republic into a Pacific empire almost overnight.

Japan was the most remarkable case. After the Meiji Restoration (1868), Japan deliberately industrialized and adopted Western military technology to avoid colonization. It then used those same methods to build its own empire: winning the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95) and seizing Taiwan; defeating Russia in the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05), which stunned the world by showing a non-Western nation defeating a European power; and annexing Korea in 1910.

🇺🇸 Settler Colonies: New Zealand and Beyond

The CED specifically identifies New Zealand as an example of a settler colony (KC-5.2.I.D). Unlike exploitation colonies that governed large indigenous populations, settler colonies involved the permanent migration of European populations who displaced indigenous peoples from their land.

In New Zealand, Britain signed the Treaty of Waitangi (1840) with Māori chiefs, promising to protect Māori land rights. Within decades, however, European settlers flooded in, and the colonial government found pretexts to seize Māori land through the Land Wars (1845–1872). Māori lost about 95% of their land. Similar patterns played out in Australia (Aboriginal Australians), South Africa (Zulu, Xhosa, and other peoples), and of course throughout North America.

Settler colonialism differed fundamentally from other forms of empire in its long-term consequences: it permanently transformed the demographic makeup of colonized territories, leaving indigenous peoples as minorities in lands their ancestors had inhabited for millennia.

🛤️ Continental Empires: U.S. and Russia Expand

The CED also recognizes the expansion of the U.S. and Russia as forms of imperial state expansion (KC-5.2.II.B): these states expanded by conquering neighboring territories, building land-based continental empires.

American Manifest Destiny drove expansion from the Atlantic to the Pacific. The Louisiana Purchase (1803) doubled U.S. territory. The Indian Removal Act (1830) forced the Five Civilized Tribes west of the Mississippi on the infamous Trail of Tears. The Mexican-American War (1846–48) added California, Texas, and the Southwest. By the 1890s, Frederick Jackson Turner declared the frontier “closed.” The U.S. then looked outward: Hawaii (1898), Puerto Rico and Guam (1898), the Philippines (1898).

Russia’s expansion moved eastward through Siberia and southward into Central Asia. The Trans-Siberian Railroad, begun in 1891 and completed in 1916, bound the vast Russian empire together and enabled the projection of military power to the Pacific. Russia’s expansion brought it into conflict with Japan, the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05), and established its control over Muslim-majority Central Asian khanates like Bukhara and Khiva.

💡 Fun Fact
When the Russo-Japanese War ended in 1905 with a Japanese victory, it was the first time in modern history that a non-Western nation had defeated a major European power in a war. The shock waves reached colonized peoples across Asia and Africa: if Japan could do it, why couldn’t they resist European domination? The war inspired nationalist movements from Egypt to India to Korea.

Answer parts A, B, and C. Each part requires a separate short response. Do not write a full essay.

Part A

Briefly describe ONE specific way in which state expansion in Africa differed from state expansion in Asia in the period 1750–1900.

In Africa, expansion was primarily new territorial acquisition by European powers, formalized through the Berlin Conference (1884–85), which divided the continent into European spheres without prior significant colonial presence in the interior. In Asia, many regions already had established colonial presences that were deepened and formalized rather than newly created: Britain had been operating in India through the East India Company since the 1600s, and the 1857 Rebellion simply transferred that existing control from a company to the Crown. Asian expansion was often the formalization and intensification of existing relationships; African expansion was the rapid creation of new ones.

Part B

Briefly explain ONE way Japan’s imperial expansion was similar to European imperial expansion in the period 1850–1910.

Like European imperial powers, Japan used industrialized military force backed by unequal treaties to seize territory from neighbors. After defeating China in the First Sino-Japanese War (1894–95), Japan imposed the Treaty of Shimonoseki, forcing China to cede Taiwan and grant Japan economic concessions, exactly the kind of unequal treaty European powers had imposed on China through the Opium Wars. Like European powers, Japan also used ideological justification for expansion, framing its empire as a “Greater East Asia” project that would modernize and protect Asian peoples, a regional civilizing mission.

Part C

Briefly explain ONE way U.S. expansion across North America (1800–1890) can be compared to European imperial expansion in Africa or Asia.

American expansion used military force to dispossess indigenous peoples of their land and integrate their territories into the expanding state, the same structural process as European colonization in Africa and Asia. The Indian Removal Act (1830) and the Trail of Tears forced the Cherokee and other nations from their ancestral territories into designated “Indian Territory,” just as European powers forced African peoples off their lands to make room for settler colonists. The ideological justification (Manifest Destiny) performed the same function as the civilizing mission: it framed conquest as destiny or duty rather than dispossession.
Compare the processes by which European states expanded their power in Africa with the processes by which the United States or Japan expanded their power in the period 1750–1900.

Write a full essay with a thesis, contextualization, evidence, and historical reasoning. Use comparison as your primary skill. A strong response will identify both similarities and differences and explain their significance.

Strong thesis (comparing Europe and Japan): “Although both European powers and Japan used industrialized military force and ideological justifications to build empires in the period 1850–1910, they differed fundamentally in geographic scope and in their relationship to the global imperial order: European expansion was transoceanic and built a world system of colonial domination, while Japan’s expansion was regional and represented a non-Western nation adopting imperial methods to survive within, and eventually challenge, that system.”

  • Contextualization: Industrial Revolution (Unit 5) created the military-technological gap that made large-scale empire feasible; nationalism and Social Darwinism (Topic 6.1) provided the ideological frameworks.
  • Similarities (Europe and Japan): Both used industrialized military force (rifles, artillery, steamships) against less-industrialized neighbors; Both used unequal treaties to formalize conquest (Treaty of Nanking for Britain; Treaty of Shimonoseki for Japan); Both developed ideological justifications (civilizing mission; “Greater East Asia”); Both extracted economic resources from colonized territories
  • Differences: European expansion was global and transoceanic; Japanese expansion was regional (East Asia); Japan had itself been a target of Western imperialism before becoming a colonizer; European expansion predated and created the global system; Japanese expansion occurred within that system; Scale: British Empire covered 25% of earth’s land surface; Japan’s empire was limited to East Asia
Model answer: The conference’s success at preventing a European war came entirely at Africa’s expense. European nations were only able to “peacefully” divide Africa because they treated the continent as unclaimed, uninhabited territory, despite the fact that hundreds of millions of Africans lived there within thousands of distinct political, social, and cultural communities. The conference prevented European violence by enabling colonial violence. From a strictly European diplomatic perspective, it was a success; from a perspective that takes African peoples’ rights and sovereignty into account, it was one of the largest acts of political dispossession in history. Whether “preventing war” justifies the outcome depends entirely on whose interests you prioritize in making that judgment.
Model answer: Japan’s dual role reveals that imperialism was not specifically European. It was a strategy available to any nation that industrialized sufficiently to gain military and economic advantages over its neighbors. Japan recognized this early: the Meiji Restoration deliberately borrowed European industrialization methods to avoid the fate of China and India. Having successfully industrialized, Japan used the same tools, military force, unequal treaties, economic extraction, to build its own empire in Korea and Taiwan. This demonstrates that the core dynamic driving imperialism was the military and economic power gap created by industrialization, not European race or culture.
Model answer: The distinction is largely rhetorical rather than structural. The process, military conquest of indigenous peoples, seizure of their lands, forced displacement, and integration of their territories into the expanding state, is structurally identical to what European powers did in Africa and Asia. The difference is framing: “Manifest Destiny” presented expansion as divinely ordained national destiny; “imperialism” was what other nations did. But indigenous nations like the Cherokee, Sioux, and Comanche experienced U.S. expansion in exactly the same way African and Asian peoples experienced European expansion: loss of sovereignty, land, and cultural autonomy at the point of superior military force. Calling it Manifest Destiny rather than imperialism is itself a product of the ideology that justified it.
