7.3 Conducting World War I

⚙️ Technology🏛️ Government Mobilization📣 Propaganda💀 Total War
  • LO-A: Total war: the whole nation fights, not just the army: WWI was the first "total war". It required mobilizing entire economies, societies, and populations for war production. Governments rationed food, nationalized industries, conscripted millions of men, and organized women into war work. The distinction between soldiers and civilians blurred: everyone contributed, and everyone was a target. Propaganda: governments manufactured consent: No government could fight a long war without sustained public support. Governments across Europe and their colonial empires used posters, newspapers, films, and public ceremonies to portray their cause as righteous and the enemy as barbaric. British propaganda about German atrocities in Belgium ("the rape of Belgium") helped bring the U.S. into the war. Colonial subjects in India, Africa, and Southeast Asia were mobilized through propaganda promising better treatment after the war. New technology = unprecedented death: WWI introduced industrial-scale killing: machine guns (one could fire 400–600 rounds/minute, the equivalent of 100 riflemen), poison gas (chlorine, phosgene, mustard gas), artillery barrages, tanks, airplanes, and submarines. The result was a war of attrition in which battles costing hundreds of thousands of casualties produced movements of a few miles. The Battle of the Somme (1916): 60,000 British casualties on the first day alone. Colonies mobilized: war was global: Britain, France, and Germany mobilized their colonial subjects for the war effort. India contributed 1.5 million soldiers. West Africa, North Africa, and Southeast Asia provided soldiers, laborers, and resources. The war's costs were paid disproportionately by colonial peoples who had no say in causing it, a key grievance fueling postwar anti-colonial movements.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. What makes a war "total", and why is WWI considered the first total war in history?
A "total war" is one that requires the mobilization of the entire society, not just its army, in support of the war effort, and in which the distinction between military and civilian targets blurs. WWI was the first total war because: (1) industrial weapons required massive industrial production (steel, chemicals, machinery), which required reorganizing entire economies for war; (2) attrition warfare required replacing millions of casualties, which required mass conscription of civilian populations; (3) blockades targeted civilian populations (Britain's naval blockade caused food shortages in Germany; Germany's submarine campaign targeted civilian shipping); (4) governments systematically organized propaganda, rationing, and industrial mobilization as state functions. Earlier wars had been devastating but were fought by professional armies with limited home-front mobilization. WWI required the home front as much as the battlefield.
2. Why did trench warfare produce such massive casualties without producing decisive victories?
Trench warfare was a product of the asymmetry between offensive and defensive military technology in WWI. Machine guns and artillery made advancing across open ground catastrophically costly, attackers advancing toward dug-in defenders were mowed down. But neither side could simply defend indefinitely; political and strategic logic required trying to break through. The result was repeated offensives that gained little ground at enormous cost. The Battle of the Somme (1916) is the classic example: after a week-long artillery bombardment (which failed to destroy German defenses), British infantry advanced and suffered 60,000 casualties on the first day. The combination of machine guns, barbed wire, and deep dugouts made defense overwhelmingly superior to offense, until the tank (introduced in 1916) began to change the balance. Trench warfare was fundamentally a problem of industrial defensive technology outpacing offensive tactics.
3. WWI governments used propaganda to mobilize populations for war. What does the need for propaganda tell us about the nature of democratic or semi-democratic governments fighting modern wars?
It reveals that modern industrial governments could not fight long wars without popular support, and that popular support had to be cultivated and maintained. It couldn't simply be commanded. Conscript armies require millions of ordinary citizens to risk their lives; this can't be achieved through pure coercion at the scale required. Industrial war production requires workers to accept long hours, dangerous conditions, and reduced wages for "the cause", again, requiring consent. The need for propaganda also reveals the power of print media, photography, and film to shape public opinion, a genuinely new political reality of the early 20th century. Governments recognized that the "home front" was as important as the military front: a population that lost faith in the war effort could destabilize the entire military effort (as happened in Russia in 1917).
4. Britain mobilized 1.5 million soldiers from India and hundreds of thousands more from Africa and other colonies. What implications did this colonial mobilization have for postwar anti-colonial movements?
Colonial mobilization for WWI created a profound contradiction that anti-colonial movements exploited after the war. Colonial subjects were asked to fight and die for empires whose right to rule them rested on claims of European racial and civilizational superiority. Soldiers from India, West Africa, and Senegal who had served in the trenches alongside European soldiers, been treated as equals in combat, and sacrificed their lives for Britain or France were returned after the war to colonial status, denied the political rights, economic equality, and self-determination that European leaders had proclaimed as the war's justification. This contradiction energized Indian nationalism (Mohandas Gandhi's early activism included organizing Indian ambulance corps in South Africa, and he expected postwar reforms), West African political movements, and Vietnamese nationalism. WWI created the conditions for postwar anti-colonial demands by making European claims of racial superiority impossible to sustain with the same confidence.
5. Poison gas was widely condemned as a barbaric weapon during and after WWI. Why did governments that condemned its use continue to use it?
Governments continued using poison gas because the logic of total war and attrition overrode moral condemnation. Once Germany introduced chlorine gas at Ypres in April 1915 and demonstrated its military effectiveness (temporarily breaking enemy lines), the Allies faced a choice: condemn it and accept military disadvantage, or develop their own gas capabilities and condemn its use as a precedent. They chose the latter. This is a classic "security dilemma", each side felt compelled to match the other's capabilities to avoid disadvantage, regardless of moral considerations. The total war context amplified this: when you are losing hundreds of thousands of soldiers to attrition, any weapon that might break the stalemate becomes attractive. WWI's gas warfare established the precedent that industrial nations would use massively destructive chemical weapons when militarily convenient, despite moral opposition, a precedent challenged by the 1925 Geneva Protocol and again in WWII when most combatants chose not to use gas (primarily because both sides could retaliate in kind).

The Reading

🪖 The First Total War: When Whole Nations Fight

Earlier wars had been fought by professional armies or conscripted armies that left most of civilian society relatively untouched. World War I was fundamentally different. Historians call it the first total war, a conflict in which governments mobilized entire economies, societies, and populations for the war effort. The distinction between "soldier" and "civilian" essentially collapsed.

What made total war necessary was the combination of industrial weapons (which required industrial-scale production) and attrition strategy (which required replacing millions of casualties). A machine gun used 400–600 rounds per minute; supplying it required factories, railways, and industrial labor on an unprecedented scale. Conscripting, training, equipping, feeding, and transporting millions of soldiers required reorganizing entire national economies. By 1916, Britain, France, and Germany had all nationalized key industries, introduced food rationing, mobilized women into war production, and brought their colonial populations into the war effort.

💡 Fun Fact
The Battle of Verdun (1916) lasted 303 days, nearly ten months, and produced approximately 700,000 casualties (killed, wounded, or captured) for almost no territorial change. The French and German armies each fired approximately 40 million artillery shells during the battle. It is considered one of the most devastating battles in human history. The French Army's losses were so severe that 40 of 112 French divisions mutinied in 1917, soldiers refused to participate in offensive operations, though they continued to defend their positions. The French government kept this mutiny secret for decades.

📣 Propaganda: Manufacturing Support for War

No government could sustain a multi-year war of attrition without maintaining public support. This required propaganda, the systematic use of media, art, and public messaging to shape public opinion. WWI governments across Europe developed propaganda techniques that became models for all future wartime information management.

Propaganda served several purposes:

  • Justifying the war: Every belligerent portrayed its cause as defensive and righteous. Britain emphasized German "militarism" and atrocities in Belgium; Germany portrayed itself as defending European culture against Russian barbarism.
  • Demonizing the enemy: Propaganda portrayed enemy soldiers and civilians as subhuman barbarians. British propaganda called Germans "Huns" (deliberately invoking Attila's destructive 5th-century invasions).
  • Encouraging enlistment: Posters like Britain's "Your Country Needs YOU" (featuring Lord Kitchener pointing directly at the viewer) appealed to patriotic duty and social pressure. Women were encouraged to give white feathers (symbols of cowardice) to men not in uniform.
  • Mobilizing colonies: Britain and France used propaganda to recruit soldiers and laborers from their colonial empires, promising (often insincerely) better treatment after the war. India alone contributed 1.5 million soldiers to the British war effort.
📝 AP Tip
The AP CED says governments used "political propaganda, art, media, and intensified forms of nationalism" to mobilize populations, including "both in the home countries and the colonies." This colonial dimension is important for AP questions. Colonial subjects were mobilized to fight for empires they had no voice in governing, a contradiction that fueled postwar anti-colonial movements (Topic 7.5).

💀 New Technology, Unprecedented Casualties

WWI introduced the full destructive power of the Industrial Revolution to warfare. The result was a leap in killing efficiency that military tactics had not yet adapted to. The key technological innovations:

  • Machine guns: A single machine gun could fire 400–600 rounds per minute, equivalent to 100 riflemen. Attacking infantry advancing across open ground against dug-in machine gun positions suffered catastrophic casualties. This more than any single factor produced the trench warfare stalemate.
  • Artillery: Industrial-scale artillery barrages could fire millions of shells, destroying fortifications and killing defenders in their trenches. The problem: barrages also destroyed the terrain, making it impossible to advance quickly through the resulting moonscape of craters and mud.
  • Poison gas: Germany introduced chlorine gas at the Second Battle of Ypres (April 1915). Subsequent gases, phosgene, mustard gas, were even more deadly or disabling. Gas could penetrate dugouts, causing suffocation, blindness, and chemical burns. All major powers eventually used gas; it killed approximately 100,000 soldiers and injured over a million.
  • Tanks: Introduced by Britain in 1916, tanks could cross barbed wire and trenches, providing protection from machine gun fire while advancing. Initially unreliable and slow, by 1918 they were contributing to the Allied breakthrough.
  • Airplanes: Initially used for reconnaissance, aircraft evolved into fighters (dogfights) and bombers. The first strategic bombing of civilian targets occurred in WWI (German zeppelin raids on London).
  • Submarines: Germany's Unterseebooten (U-boats) waged submarine warfare against Allied shipping, eventually sinking civilian ships including the British Lusitania (1915, killing 1,198 people including 128 Americans). Unrestricted submarine warfare was a key factor in bringing the U.S. into the war in 1917.

🌍 A Global War: Mobilizing Colonies and Empires

WWI was not just a European war. It was a world war because the belligerent powers mobilized their global empires. Britain's empire stretched across India, Africa, Australia, Canada, and the Pacific; France's empire covered West Africa, North Africa, and Southeast Asia. Both powers recruited soldiers, laborers, and resources from their colonial subjects.

  • India: 1.5 million Indian soldiers served in the British army, fighting in France, Mesopotamia, East Africa, and Palestine. Indian leaders like Mohandas Gandhi supported the war effort, expecting political concessions in return.
  • West and North Africa: France's tirailleurs sénégalais, Senegalese Riflemen, included soldiers from across French West Africa. Approximately 600,000 African soldiers served in the French army. Britain recruited from West Africa, East Africa, and South Africa.
  • The Middle East: The Ottoman Empire's entry into the war on the German side transformed the Middle East into a theater of war. Britain supported the Arab Revolt (1916) against Ottoman rule, promising Arab independence, a promise that was not kept.
  • The Pacific: Japan, allied with Britain, seized German colonies in the Pacific (the Marshall Islands, the Carolines, the Marianas) and in China (Qingdao). Australia and New Zealand fought at Gallipoli and on the Western Front.

The global scope of the war was significant not just militarily but politically. Colonial subjects who fought and died for European empires returned home with changed expectations. The contradiction between wartime promises of democracy and self-determination and postwar continuation of colonial rule fueled the anti-colonial movements of the 1920s–1960s.

💡 Fun Fact
The Christmas Truce of December 1914 is one of WWI's most famous stories. On Christmas Eve, German soldiers along parts of the Western Front began placing Christmas trees on their trench parapets and singing carols. British soldiers responded with their own carols. Spontaneously, soldiers from both sides climbed out of their trenches, met in "no man's land," exchanged cigarettes and food, and in some sectors played impromptu soccer games. The informal truce held in many sectors through Christmas Day. Officers on both sides tried to stop it, fraternizing with the enemy was a military offense. High command ordered that it never happen again; it largely didn't. The truce is a remarkable reminder that wars are fought by human beings who don't always hate each other, even when their governments do.

According to the AP CED, governments used "political propaganda, art, media, and intensified forms of nationalism" to mobilize populations for WWI. This strategy was necessary because
  • total war required sustained popular support that could not be maintained through coercion alone, since armies and war production both depended on mass participation
  • most European populations initially opposed the war and had to be persuaded to support it
  • the Geneva Convention prohibited governments from conscripting soldiers without their consent
  • colonial subjects would only fight if they understood the ideological justifications for the war
The core reason governments invested in propaganda was that total war required mass participation, millions of conscripts willing to fight, workers willing to produce under difficult conditions, families willing to accept sacrifice. At this scale, pure coercion is insufficient; governments needed populations to internalize the war's legitimacy. Propaganda was the tool for creating and maintaining that popular buy-in across a multi-year conflict.
Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare policy, which involved sinking civilian ships including the Lusitania, was most significant because
  • it ended British trade with North America and forced Britain to seek peace
  • it constituted the first use of a new military technology in naval warfare
  • it violated the alliance agreement between Germany and the Ottoman Empire
  • it contributed to the United States' decision to enter WWI in 1917
Germany's resumption of unrestricted submarine warfare in February 1917 (sinking any ship in the war zone, including American vessels) was one of the two main reasons the U.S. entered WWI in April 1917 (the other was the Zimmermann Telegram, in which Germany secretly proposed a German-Mexican alliance against the U.S.). U.S. entry provided the Allies with fresh manpower and industrial resources that proved decisive in 1918.
Explain how the new military technology of World War I led to increased casualties and, in turn, required governments to mobilize their societies in new ways.
New military technology, especially machine guns, heavy artillery, and poison gas, made WWI catastrophically lethal. Machine guns firing 400–600 rounds per minute made frontal infantry assaults suicidal; the Battle of the Somme (1916) produced 60,000 British casualties on its first day alone. Artillery barrages fired millions of shells, devastating both defenders and the terrain. Poison gas penetrated dugouts, causing blindness and suffocation. These casualty levels forced governments to mobilize entire societies to replace fallen soldiers and sustain war production. Mass conscription brought millions of civilians into uniform. Governments nationalized industries, rationed food, and organized women into war production. Colonial populations in India, West Africa, and elsewhere were recruited as soldiers and laborers. The unprecedented scale of death required unprecedented mobilization. This is what makes WWI the "first total war."
Evaluate the extent to which World War I represented a new kind of war compared to earlier military conflicts, with specific attention to the methods governments used to conduct it.
World War I represented a fundamentally new kind of warfare because the combination of industrial military technology (machine guns, artillery, chemical weapons) and total war mobilization (propaganda, conscription, colonial recruitment, nationalized industry) required governments to organize entire societies for war in ways unprecedented in human history, producing a conflict that was global in scope, industrial in method, and catastrophic in human cost.
Model answer: Propaganda in total war is both understandable and problematic. It's understandable because total war genuinely requires popular support. You cannot conscript millions of men and reorganize entire economies without explaining why the sacrifice is worth it. Some propaganda was essentially accurate: Germany did commit atrocities in Belgium (though British propaganda exaggerated them); the Allies' stated war aims (opposing militarism, defending smaller nations) had genuine merit. But much WWI propaganda was deliberately misleading: manufactured atrocity stories, dehumanization of enemy civilians, and false promises to colonial subjects. The deeper issue is that propaganda in a democracy treats citizens not as rational decision-makers who deserve accurate information, but as an audience to be managed. When people discover they were deceived, as many did in the postwar disillusionment, they lose trust in government. WWI propaganda's cynical manipulation contributed significantly to the political cynicism and extremism of the 1920s and 1930s.
Model answer: It suggests that military technology doesn't automatically translate into military advantage. It depends on whether tactics and strategy have adapted to the new technology. In WWI, defensive technology (machine guns, barbed wire, artillery) had outpaced offensive tactics, producing a stalemate. Military commanders initially tried to use Napoleonic tactics (massed infantry assaults) against industrial weapons, with predictably catastrophic results. The war lasted four years partly because it took that long for military thinking to catch up with technology: combined arms tactics (coordinating infantry, tanks, artillery, and airplanes) that emerged in 1918 eventually broke the stalemate. This pattern, technology outrunning doctrine, recurs throughout military history. The lesson: military effectiveness depends not just on possessing better weapons but on developing the organizational capacity and tactical thinking to use them effectively.
Model answer: The broken promises had profound long-term consequences for anti-colonial movements. Colonial soldiers who had served alongside European troops, been treated as equals in combat, and sacrificed their lives for empires returned home with two experiences: (1) proof that European claims of racial superiority were unconvincing. They had demonstrated equal or superior courage and capability in combat; (2) a concrete grievance. They had been promised better treatment in exchange for their service, and the promises hadn't been kept. Indian veterans who returned from the Western Front or Mesopotamia and found British colonial rule unchanged were significantly more receptive to Gandhi's non-cooperation movement. West African veterans became key figures in early African nationalist movements. The Amritsar Massacre (1919), in which British troops killed hundreds of peaceful Indian protesters, was particularly devastating because it occurred so shortly after Indian soldiers had bled for the Empire. WWI's colonial mobilization helped create the conditions for the decolonization movement of the 1940s–1960s.
Model answer: The Christmas Truce reveals the gap between individual soldiers' feelings and the political-institutional forces driving the war. Soldiers didn't continue fighting primarily because of personal hatred of enemy soldiers. They continued because: (1) governments and military command enforced continuation through military discipline (desertion was punishable by execution); (2) national identity and social pressure made stopping seem like betrayal; (3) once the machinery of industrial war was set in motion, conscript armies, weapons contracts, strategic plans, it developed its own momentum that individual feelings couldn't stop; (4) there was genuine disagreement about peace terms. Germany controlled occupied territory (Belgium, northern France) and wasn't willing to simply leave; the Allies weren't willing to accept German territorial gains. The truce showed that wars are not driven primarily by the emotional state of individual soldiers. They are driven by political decisions, institutional forces, national identities, and strategic calculations, factors that operate largely independently of whether individual combatants personally hate each other.
Model answer: The contrast shows that the conduct of war is shaped by both moral norms and strategic calculations, and that these can align to produce genuine changes in behavior. After WWI, the 1925 Geneva Protocol prohibited the use of gas in warfare; this established a norm. But norms alone don't explain WWII's restraint, both Nazi Germany and the Allies violated other norms freely (strategic bombing of cities, treatment of POWs). The strategic calculation was equally important: in WWII, both sides possessed large chemical weapons stockpiles and the capacity to deliver them. Any side that used gas would face immediate retaliation in kind. This mutual deterrence, each side could retaliate effectively, made gas use strategically counterproductive. The combination of moral norm (gas = barbaric) and strategic deterrence (retaliation would hurt us more than them) kept WWII largely gas-free. This pattern, where norm + strategic calculation together produce different behavior than either alone, is important for understanding how international agreements and deterrence work.
