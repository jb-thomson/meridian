AP World History · Unit 7: Global Conflict

7.9 Causation in Global Conflict

🔍 Causation💡 Historical Reasoning⚖️ Argumentation🌍 Synthesis
  • LO-A: Causation skill: not just what happened, but why: The AP exam tests causation at multiple levels: identifying causes, explaining the mechanisms connecting cause and effect, distinguishing proximate (immediate) from deeper structural causes, and ranking causes by relative importance. Topic 7.9 is about practicing this skill across the entire unit. Three levels of causation in Unit 7: (1) Structural: industrialization created military-technological gaps; imperial competition created great-power tensions; nationalism undermined multi-ethnic empires. (2) Political: alliance systems, appeasement, totalitarian regimes. (3) Individual: specific decisions by specific leaders (Hitler's blank check, Stalin's Five Year Plans, Churchill's refusal to negotiate with Hitler). Strong AP essays explain causation at all three levels. The WWI–WWII causal chain is the most important synthesis: WWI's unsatisfactory outcome (Versailles) directly caused the conditions for WWII: German resentment → Nazi rise → fascist expansion → WWII. This is the clearest causal chain in AP World History and appears frequently on LEQ and SAQ prompts about "continuity and change" across the first half of the 20th century. Technology as a recurring cause of change in Unit 7: Industrial technology enabled total war (machine guns, tanks, gas in WWI; strategic bombing, atomic bomb in WWII); communication technology enabled propaganda at scale; transportation technology (railways) enabled both industrial mobilization and Holocaust logistics. Rapid science and technology is a KC-6.1 theme, connect it to specific Unit 7 examples for essay questions.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. The AP exam's "causation" skill requires you to rank causes by relative importance. If you had to identify the SINGLE most important cause of WWI and WWII respectively, what would you argue, and how would you defend your choice?
For WWI, the strongest case for "most important" is the alliance system, because it was the mechanism that converted a regional Austro-Serbian dispute into a world war. The underlying causes (nationalism, imperialism, militarism) created the tension; but without the alliance system's automatic escalation mechanism, the assassination of Franz Ferdinand might have remained a localized conflict. For WWII, the strongest case is the rise of Nazi Germany under Hitler, specifically Hitler's personal ideology (Lebensraum, racial war) and willingness to gamble on war. The structural conditions (Versailles, Depression, fascism) were necessary but not sufficient; other fascist leaders (Mussolini, Franco) operated in similar conditions without initiating world war at the same scale. Hitler's specific combination of ideology and strategic aggression is what distinguished WWII from "just another European war." Both arguments can be defended; the point is to make a specific, ranked claim and support it with evidence, not to list all the causes without ranking them.
2. Unit 7 covers the period "after 1900." How does this unit connect to Unit 6 (Consequences of Industrialization, 1750–1900)?
Unit 7 is in many ways the conclusion of Unit 6's story. Unit 6 showed how industrialization created the military-technological gap, economic demand, and ideological justifications (Social Darwinism, nationalism) for imperial expansion. Unit 7 shows what happens when that system of imperial competition, combined with the ideology, alliances, and nationalism it generated, produced catastrophic world war. The connection is direct: the same imperial competition that drove the Berlin Conference (Unit 6) produced the alliance rivalries that caused WWI (Unit 7). The industrial weapons that made 19th-century imperial conquest cheap produced the machine guns and artillery that made WWI catastrophically lethal. The social Darwinism that justified imperialism mutated into Nazi racial ideology. And the anti-colonial movements that grew in the interwar period (Topic 7.5) directly continue into Unit 8's decolonization story. Unit 7 is the hinge between the age of imperialism and the post-WWII world.
3. The period 1900–1945 produced two world wars and multiple genocides. Was this level of violence an aberration, a temporary catastrophe, or does it reveal something fundamental about the combination of modern states and industrial technology?
The evidence supports the "reveals something fundamental" interpretation more than the "aberration" interpretation. The pattern is too consistent to be purely accidental: modern industrial states demonstrated repeatedly that they are capable of organizing mass killing at scales previously impossible. The Holocaust, the Holodomor, and the Armenian Genocide all used state bureaucratic and logistical capacity to kill at industrial scale. WWI and WWII used industrial weapons to kill tens of millions. This suggests that modernization, specifically the combination of ideological totalism (the belief that the state can and should reorganize society according to a blueprint) with industrial organizational capacity, creates a specific risk of mass violence that previous societies didn't have. The aberration view holds that the specific ideologies (Nazism, Stalinism, extreme nationalism) were contingent, different political choices could have avoided them. The "fundamental" view holds that as long as states have the technical capacity for mass killing and ideologies emerge that define large groups as enemies, mass atrocities are a recurring risk. Post-WWII international institutions (UN, ICC, R2P) represent an attempt to manage this risk, with mixed results.
4. How does the role of individual leaders (Lenin, Stalin, Hitler, Churchill, Roosevelt, Gandhi) compare to structural forces (industrialization, nationalism, imperialism, the alliance system) in explaining Unit 7's major developments?
The strongest historical analysis acknowledges both individual and structural causation without reducing everything to either. Structural forces set the boundaries of what was possible: without the Great Depression, Hitler probably couldn't have achieved mass electoral support; without the alliance system, WWI might have remained regional; without industrialization, total war would have been impossible. But within those structural constraints, individuals made specific choices that determined specific outcomes: Hitler's blank check to Austria-Hungary escalated the July Crisis into WWI (structural opportunity + individual choice); Stalin's decision to continue grain extraction during the Holodomor famine (structural capacity + individual choice = mass death); Churchill's refusal to negotiate with Hitler in 1940 (structural situation + individual decision = continuation of the war). Gandhi's non-violence strategy similarly worked within structural constraints (British democratic opinion mattered; massive Indian population could be mobilized) while representing a specific strategic choice that shaped how the independence movement developed. The AP exam's causation skill requires recognizing both levels, structural forces enable and constrain; individual decisions determine specific outcomes within those boundaries.
5. The period 1900–1945 ended with the creation of the United Nations, the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, and the nuclear age simultaneously. What does this combination reveal about what the period had taught humanity?
The simultaneous creation of these three things reveals the double lesson that two world wars and genocide had taught: the need for international cooperation to prevent future catastrophic wars (UN, international human rights framework) combined with the terrifying recognition that humanity now possessed weapons capable of destroying civilization (nuclear age). The UN and Universal Declaration represent the optimistic lesson: if states cooperate through international institutions and respect universal human rights, catastrophic war and genocide can be prevented. The nuclear age represents the pessimistic lesson: industrialization had produced weapons so destructive that even "winning" a nuclear war would be civilization-destroying, making future total war between nuclear powers effectively impossible, but also meaning that the threat could never be fully eliminated. Together, these three developments defined the post-WWII world: international cooperation and human rights norms as the framework for preventing catastrophe; nuclear deterrence as the backup mechanism; and the constant awareness that the mechanisms that had produced WWII's violence, industrial technology, totalitarian ideology, great-power competition, hadn't disappeared, but were now constrained by new institutional and technological realities.

Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: A conflict that mobilizes all of a society's resources, military, economic, industrial, and psychological, toward the war effort, targeting civilian industries and morale as well as armies. Significance: Both WWI and WWII were total wars; industrial mobilization, propaganda, rationing, and strategic bombing of civilians were all features. Total war was only possible after industrialization.
  • Definition: A network of mutual defense treaties. Triple Alliance (Germany, Austria-Hungary, Italy) and Triple Entente (France, Russia, Britain), that obligated states to support each other in case of attack. Significance: The alliance system was the mechanism that converted the assassination of Franz Ferdinand into a world war; it created an automatic escalation dynamic that individual leaders could not easily stop.
  • Definition: The policy of making concessions to an aggressive power to avoid war, most associated with British and French tolerance of Hitler's treaty violations and territorial expansions in the 1930s. Significance: Appeasement allowed Hitler to rearm, reoccupy the Rhineland, annex Austria, and absorb the Sudetenland without military resistance, emboldening further aggression and contributing to WWII.
The Reading

🔍 What Is the "Causation" Skill, and Why Does the AP Test It?

Every unit in AP World History ends with a "causation" or "synthesis" topic that shifts from content to historical thinking. Topic 7.9 isn't a new set of facts to memorize, it's an opportunity to practice the most important analytical skill on the AP exam: causation.

The AP exam tests causation at multiple levels:

The LO for this topic is to "explain the relative significance of the causes of global conflict", which means you need to be able to rank, not just list. The difference between a 5 essay and a 3 essay is often exactly this: one makes a specific, ranked causal argument supported by evidence; the other lists causes without explaining which mattered most and why.

🗝️ Unit 7 Key Concepts Review

Before practicing causation reasoning, here's a quick-reference summary of Unit 7's key concepts (KC-6.1, KC-6.2):

⚙️ Connecting Unit 7 to Its Context: Technology as a Root Cause

The AP CED's KC-6.1 identifies "rapid advances in science and technology" as the first major key concept of Period 6 (1900–present). This isn't an accident, technology is the root-level cause that makes most of Unit 7 possible. Consider how technology connects to every major Unit 7 development:

  • WWI's devastation: Industrial weapons (machine guns, artillery, poison gas) made warfare catastrophically lethal. Without industrialization, WWI would have been a conventional war with conventional casualties.
  • Total war: Industrial production capacity enabled governments to mobilize entire economies for war. This was only possible in industrial societies.
  • Soviet industrialization: Stalin's Five Year Plans sought to build industrial capacity at any cost, technology acquisition was the explicit goal.
  • WWII's global reach: Airplanes, submarines, aircraft carriers, and rapid-fire weapons made WWII genuinely global. The atomic bomb represented the ultimate application of industrial chemistry and physics to warfare.
  • The Holocaust: Railway logistics and industrial gas chambers made the Holocaust's scale possible. Dehumanizing propaganda used radio and film technology to reach mass audiences simultaneously.
  • Communication: Radio propaganda (Roosevelt's Fireside Chats, Nazi Volksempfänger "people's receiver," Rwandan RTLM) used communication technology to mobilize populations on a scale impossible before broadcasting.
📝 AP Tip
For Unit 7 LEQ and SAQ questions, always consider connecting your argument to KC-6.1 (rapid advances in science and technology). The AP exam rewards contextualization, placing Unit 7 events in their broader context of industrial technological change. A sentence like "The industrial weapons technology developed during the 19th century's industrial revolution gave WWI's combatants unprecedented killing capacity..." contextualizes any argument about WWI causes or conduct.

🔗 The WWI → WWII Causal Chain: Unit 7's Most Important Connection

The clearest and most frequently tested causal chain in Unit 7 is the direct connection from WWI's outcome to WWII's causes. This is not a coincidence. It is one of the most direct cause-and-effect sequences in AP World History:

Each step in this chain was caused by the previous step. The chain begins at Versailles, which was itself the consequence of WWI. The period 1914–1945 can be understood as one continuous "Thirty Years' War" in which the first war's unresolved issues directly produced the second.

💡 Fun Fact
The armistice ending WWI was signed on November 11, 1918, at 11:00 a.m., the eleventh hour of the eleventh day of the eleventh month. But the actual ceasefire order had been sent hours earlier. Between the signing (5:00 a.m.) and the ceasefire (11:00 a.m.), approximately 11,000 men were killed or wounded, more casualties than on D-Day, because commanders on both sides continued ordering attacks to avoid ending on a note of inaction. The last American soldier killed in WWI was shot at 10:59 a.m., one minute before the armistice. These final casualties served no military purpose. The decision to enforce a symbolic hour rather than an immediate ceasefire reflects the same militarism that had produced the war, and is a reminder that the human costs of historical causation are always real, specific people making decisions that affect real, specific other people.

⚖️ Ranking Causes: A Framework for AP Essays

When the AP asks you to evaluate the "relative significance" or "relative importance" of causes, here's a framework for constructing a strong argument:

Step 1: Identify the causes. List the causes the prompt is asking about (for WWI: MAIN; for WWII: the four CED causes).

Step 2: Determine a ranking criterion. What makes one cause "more important" than another? Options include: (a) Which cause was necessary vs. sufficient? (Some causes were necessary but not sufficient; the "most important" cause might be the one that was most proximate or most enabling.); (b) Which cause had the widest effects? (c) Which cause would, if removed, have changed the outcome most decisively?

Step 3: Make a specific ranked claim. "The most important cause of WWI was the alliance system, because it was the mechanism that converted a regional crisis into a world war, without it, the assassination of Franz Ferdinand might have produced a localized Austro-Serbian conflict rather than a global conflict."

Step 4: Address the counter-argument. "Although nationalism created the intense rivalries that made compromise difficult, nationalism alone didn't trigger war. It was the combination of nationalist tensions with the automatic escalation mechanism of the alliance system that produced the specific world war of 1914."

Step 5: Return to your claim. This structure, claim, evidence, counter-argument, return to claim, is the basic structure of an AP LEQ body paragraph on causation.

An AP student argues that the most important cause of WWII was the Great Depression, since without mass unemployment, Hitler couldn't have achieved electoral success. A second student argues that the most important cause was the Versailles Treaty, since it created the resentments that all Depression-era extremists exploited. Which response best evaluates these arguments?
  • Both arguments are valid, but the strongest analysis would show how both causes worked together. Versailles created the grievances; the Depression provided the mass desperation that made those grievances electorally powerful
  • The second student is correct because the Versailles Treaty preceded the Depression and therefore must be causally prior
  • The first student is correct because economic factors are always more fundamental than political ones
  • Neither argument is valid because personal factors (Hitler's charisma) were more important than structural causes
The most sophisticated AP answer recognizes that "most important" isn't necessarily an either/or choice: causes often work in combination. Versailles created the grievances; the Depression provided the conditions that made those grievances electorally decisive. Neither was sufficient alone. Versailles without Depression might have produced revisionist nationalism without mass support; Depression without Versailles might have produced economic radicalism without the specific nationalist character of Nazism. The combination was what made Hitler's specific political project succeed.
The causal chain from WWI → Versailles → Depression conditions → Hitler's rise → WWII is a central argumentative framework for Unit 7. What is the strongest qualification or limitation of this argument?
  • The Depression didn't affect Germany significantly, so the economic argument doesn't apply
  • The causal chain assumes inevitability but ignores the contingent decisions (different responses to Hitler's early violations, different appeasement choices) that could have broken the chain at multiple points
  • Versailles was actually lenient by the standards of previous peace settlements, so resentment was irrational
  • WWI was actually caused by WWII through reverse causation, making the chain incoherent
The strongest qualification is that the causal chain implies inevitability but is actually highly contingent. At each link, different choices were available: a less humiliating Versailles, Western military response to the Rhineland, abandoning appeasement earlier. The chain is real and important, but it was not a mechanical deterministic sequence. It required specific bad decisions at multiple points. This is the "nuance" or "modification" that AP expects in a sophisticated argument.
Which of the following best demonstrates the "causation" historical thinking skill as defined by the AP exam?
  • "WWI was caused by the alliance system, nationalism, imperialism, and militarism."
  • "WWI happened in 1914 when Archduke Franz Ferdinand was assassinated in Sarajevo, triggering military responses across Europe."
  • "The alliance system was the most important cause of WWI because it was the specific mechanism that converted the July Crisis from a bilateral Austro-Serbian dispute into a world war involving 30 countries."
  • "Without WWI, WWII would not have happened, showing that wars cause other wars."
This response demonstrates the causation skill because it: (1) makes a specific, ranked claim (alliance system was MOST important), (2) explains the mechanism (it converted a bilateral dispute into a world war by activating mutual defense commitments), and (3) provides specific, accurate evidence (30 countries, July Crisis, Austro-Serbian dispute). Choice A lists causes without ranking; choice B describes what happened without explaining causation; choice D states a causal relationship without explaining the mechanism.
The AP CED's KC-6.1 states that "rapid advances in science and technology altered the understanding of the universe and led to advances in communication, transportation, industry, agriculture, and medicine." For Unit 7, the most directly relevant application of this key concept is
  • industrial military technology (machine guns, tanks, poison gas, atomic bomb) that enabled total war at unprecedented scale and casualty levels
  • advances in agricultural technology that enabled governments to feed larger conscript armies
  • the development of germ theory, which reduced WWI casualties through better medical treatment
  • communications technology that allowed diplomats to prevent escalation through rapid telegraph communication
For Unit 7 specifically, KC-6.1's technology theme is most directly illustrated by the industrial military technology that enabled both WWI and WWII to be conducted at unprecedented scale and lethality. Machine guns, poison gas, tanks, strategic bombers, and the atomic bomb were all products of industrialization and scientific advance, and they are what made 20th-century warfare uniquely destructive compared to pre-industrial conflicts.
Using TWO specific pieces of evidence from different topics in Unit 7, explain how the outcomes of World War I contributed to the causes of World War II.
Evidence 1, Versailles and German resentment (Topics 7.5, 7.6): The Treaty of Versailles imposed war guilt and $33 billion in reparations on Germany, creating widespread resentment that Hitler exploited. His Nazi Party won only 2.6% of the vote in 1928 when Germany was stable; after the Depression hit, it won 37% in 1932. Hitler's central promise was to tear up the "humiliation" of Versailles, a promise with mass appeal precisely because Versailles was experienced as unjust. Without the Versailles settlement's specific terms, Hitler's revisionist nationalism would have had far less electoral purchase. Evidence 2, League of Nations weakness (Topics 7.5, 7.6): WWI's peace settlement created the League of Nations as a collective security institution, but the U.S. never joined, and the League lacked enforcement mechanisms. When Japan seized Manchuria (1931) in violation of international agreements, the League condemned the action and Japan simply withdrew. This demonstrated that the postwar order had no effective mechanism to stop great-power aggression, emboldening Hitler's subsequent violations (Rhineland, Austria, Czechoslovakia) and the appeasement policy that allowed them to proceed unchallenged.
Evaluate the relative significance of the causes of global conflict in the period 1900–1945, with specific attention to whether structural forces or individual decisions were more important in determining the scale and character of that conflict.
While the structural forces of industrialization, nationalism, and great-power imperial competition created the conditions for unprecedented global conflict between 1900 and 1945, individual decisions, the blank check to Austria-Hungary in 1914, the Versailles settlement's specific terms in 1919, and Hitler's strategic gambles in the 1930s, were more important in determining the specific character and scale of both world wars, because different choices at these decision points could have produced fundamentally different outcomes even given the same structural conditions.
Model answer: WWII was not inevitable after WWI, the causal chain required multiple contingent decisions at each step. A more lenient Versailles settlement might not have produced the German resentment that fascism exploited. Different American economic policy might have reduced the Depression's severity in Germany. German democratic politics could have produced different electoral outcomes. Hitler came to power with 37% of the vote; a more resilient Weimar Republic might have survived. Different British and French responses to Hitler's early violations (reoccupying the Rhineland in 1936 with a tiny force), responding militarily would likely have ended Hitler's expansion, could have prevented war. Appeasement was a choice, not an inevitability. What the causal chain demonstrates is that each bad decision made the next worse outcome more likely: Versailles raised the probability of revisionist nationalism; the Depression raised the probability of Hitler's victory; Hitler's success raised the probability of aggressive foreign policy; appeasement raised the probability that aggression would proceed. The chain produced WWII not inevitably but probabilistically, each link narrowed the range of likely futures until war was overwhelmingly probable. This is the difference between "inevitable" (certain to happen given initial conditions) and "highly contingent" (dependent on many specific decisions along the way).
Model answer: The connections are direct and pervasive. The borders of most Middle Eastern countries were drawn by British and French mandatory powers (Topic 7.5) and remain the source of conflicts today. The nuclear deterrence structure developed after 1945 continues to shape global politics. Russia, the U.S., China, India, Pakistan, North Korea, and Israel possess nuclear weapons, and the threat of their use is a constant background of international relations. The international institutions created after WWII (UN, World Bank, IMF, NATO) continue to structure global politics. The Nuremberg Principles are the foundation of ongoing ICC prosecutions of war crimes in the DRC, Sudan, Ukraine, and elsewhere. The Israeli-Palestinian conflict has direct roots in the Balfour Declaration and British Mandate. The genocides of the 20th century produced the international human rights framework that governs discussions of China's treatment of Uyghurs, Myanmar's treatment of Rohingya, and Russia's conduct in Ukraine. The most important connection, however, may be conceptual: Unit 7 demonstrates that the conditions for catastrophic violence, extremist ideology, economic crisis, great-power competition, failure of international institutions, can recur, and that recognizing those conditions is the prerequisite for preventing their consequences.
Model answer: Technology functions as a "root cause" that enabled and shaped conflicts without directly causing them, it's what made the specific forms of violence possible and what determined their scale. Without the Industrial Revolution's weapons technology, WWI would have been a conventional war; without railways and bureaucratic organization, the Holocaust would have been regionally limited; without the nuclear program, WWII's Pacific conclusion would have been an invasion costing millions of casualties. Technology also enabled propaganda at scale (radio, film) that made totalitarian mobilization possible. The key distinction for AP essays: technology is a necessary but not sufficient cause. Machine guns alone didn't cause WWI, nationalist leaders, alliance systems, and specific decisions were also required. The atomic bomb alone didn't cause WWII, ideology, economics, and political decisions were required first. Technology is the enabler that operates in the background of all Unit 7 conflicts, setting the range of what was possible and the scale of what occurred. The strongest AP essays on Unit 7 causation mention technology as structural context while giving more analytical weight to the specific political and ideological decisions that determined whether technological capacity was used for peace or destruction.
Model answer: The pattern suggests a fundamental tension at the heart of modern politics: states are both the primary providers of security and rights (through law, police, courts) and the primary perpetrators of mass violence against populations (through war, genocide, and political repression). All of Unit 7's mass violence was state-organized or state-enabled. This is not an accident, states have the organizational capacity, weapons, and authority that make mass violence at this scale possible. Individual violence cannot produce a holocaust; only state power can. This creates a fundamental dilemma for international relations: how do you protect people from state violence when states are also the actors that provide legal protection? The post-WWII international response, the UN, Genocide Convention, ICC, Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Responsibility to Protect, represents an attempt to create international institutions that can constrain state behavior and protect individuals from their own governments. The recurring failures (Rwanda, Srebrenica, Darfur) suggest that this institutional architecture is insufficient when major powers are unwilling to enforce it. The tension between state sovereignty (the right of states to govern their own populations without external interference) and universal human rights (the principle that some protections are owed to all people regardless of their government's choices) is the defining unresolved question of the contemporary international order.
Model answer: This is a genuine argumentation question with multiple defensible answers, the key is making a specific, justified choice rather than a vague one. Some defensible options: (1) A more lenient Versailles peace settlement (1919): eliminating the war guilt clause and reducing reparations might have produced a stable Weimar Republic, reducing Nazi electoral appeal, potentially preventing the conditions for WWII. This is the decision with the widest downstream impact, since it sits at the beginning of the WWI→WWII causal chain. (2) Allied military response to the Rhineland remilitarization (1936): Germany entered the Rhineland with only three battalions with orders to retreat if France responded militarily. French action would almost certainly have ended Hitler's expansion and his government, potentially preventing WWII and the Holocaust without a major conflict. (3) UN authorization of Rwandan intervention (1994): UNAMIR commander Dallaire's request for authority to act could have stopped a genocide that killed 500,000–800,000 people in 100 days. For AP argumentation purposes, the strongest answer makes a specific claim, explains the mechanism by which that decision would have changed outcomes, and acknowledges what couldn't have been predicted or controlled, demonstrating that historical agency is real but operates within structural constraints.
