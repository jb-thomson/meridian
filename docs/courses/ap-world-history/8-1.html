AP World History · Unit 8: Cold War and Decolonization

8.1 Setting the Stage for the Cold War and Decolonization

🏛️ Governance💣 Conflict🌍 Decolonization⚖️ Self-Determination
  • LO-A: WWII reshuffled global power: The US and USSR emerged from WWII as superpowers while the old European empires (Britain, France, the Netherlands) were economically exhausted. This power shift created the conditions for both the Cold War and decolonization simultaneously. WWII raised anti-imperial expectations: The Atlantic Charter (1941) pledged self-determination for all peoples. Colonial soldiers fought for their empires and returned home expecting change. The defeat of Japan (an Asian power) by Western technology weakened the myth of European invincibility. Two competing superpowers, two competing ideologies: The US promoted liberal democracy and capitalism; the USSR promoted communist authoritarianism. Both claimed to oppose colonialism, but for strategic reasons, not principled ones. Their rivalry defined world politics for 45 years. The stage was set for everything: Topic 8.1 is context-building. The economic and technological gains of the WWII victors (KC-6.2.IV.C.i) and the unfulfilled promises after WWI vs. the new reality after WWII (KC-6.2.II) set the stage for every development in Unit 8.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Why did WWII, rather than WWI, finally trigger the collapse of European empires?
WWI weakened European empires and raised expectations of self-determination (Wilson's Fourteen Points, Atlantic Charter forerunners), but European powers were still strong enough to suppress independence movements afterward. WWII changed two things fundamentally: (1) It so exhausted Britain, France, and the Netherlands economically that they could no longer afford to maintain empires militarily or financially. (2) The ideological context had shifted, the US and USSR both claimed to oppose colonialism, putting colonial powers on the defensive in global opinion. The defeat of colonial powers by Japan in 1941–42 also shattered the myth of European invincibility across Asia.
2. How did "technological and economic gains" during WWII shift global power?
The US in particular emerged from WWII with its industrial infrastructure completely intact (the war was fought elsewhere), its economy expanded by wartime production, and a monopoly on nuclear weapons. The US produced roughly 50% of world industrial output in 1945. The Soviet Union, despite massive losses (26 million dead), had industrialized rapidly and emerged with the world's largest land army and, by 1949, nuclear weapons. Together, these two powers dwarfed the traditional European great powers, Britain, France, Germany, whose economies, cities, and armies had been devastated.
3. The CED says hopes for self-government were "largely unfulfilled" after WWI. What happened that was different after WWII?
After WWI, Wilson's self-determination principle was applied selectively, to Europe (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia were created) but not to Asia and Africa, where colonial subjects remained under imperial control. Their appeals at the Paris Peace Conference were ignored. After WWII, the combination of exhausted colonial powers, superpower pressure, mass nationalist movements, and the new United Nations framework made it impossible to simply restore the old imperial order. The decolonization that didn't happen after WWI happened rapidly after WWII, most of Asia gained independence by the mid-1950s, and Africa largely by the mid-1960s.
4. Why is Topic 8.1 labeled "Setting the Stage" rather than being a substantive topic on its own?
Because it covers the structural context and conditions that made the Cold War and decolonization possible, not the events themselves. Like a movie's opening scene, it establishes the world after WWII: US and Soviet power, exhausted European empires, raised expectations among colonial peoples, and a new ideological divide. Without this context, it's hard to understand WHY the Cold War happened, WHY decolonization accelerated, or WHY events unfolded as they did. On the AP exam, "contextualization" points often come from exactly this type of broad framing.
Learning Objective
  • Explain the historical context of the Cold War after 1945, including how WWII shifted global power and why anti-imperialist sentiment accelerated the dissolution of empires
Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: A state with extraordinary global military and economic power, able to project force anywhere in the world. Significance: After WWII, only the US and USSR qualified, dwarfing traditional European great powers.
  • Definition: A joint declaration by FDR and Churchill stating war aims including self-determination for all peoples. Significance: Raised colonial peoples' expectations that independence would follow the war, expectations that were not fully honored by the European powers.
  • Definition: The process by which colonial peoples gained independence from imperial powers, primarily after WWII. Significance: One of the defining global transformations of the 20th century, over 50 new nations formed between 1945 and 1975.
  • Definition: The ideological and geopolitical rivalry between the US (capitalism/democracy) and USSR (communism) from 1945 to 1991, characterized by proxy wars and nuclear standoff rather than direct military conflict. Significance: The dominant framework of world politics for nearly half a century.
  • Definition: Opposition to the practice of one country controlling and exploiting another. Significance: WWII dramatically intensified anti-imperialist sentiment, both because colonial people had served in the war and expected freedom, and because both superpowers rhetorically supported independence.
  • Definition: The principle that peoples have the right to choose their own government and political status. Significance: First broadly proclaimed after WWI (Wilson's Fourteen Points) but applied mainly to Europe; after WWII it became the basis for decolonization worldwide.
The Reading

🌍 A World Transformed: The Aftermath of WWII

When World War II ended in 1945, the world looked nothing like it had in 1939. The war had been the most destructive in human history, an estimated 70–85 million people died, including soldiers, civilians, and Holocaust victims. The economies of Europe and Asia lay in ruins. The map of global power had been completely redrawn.

Two nations stood apart from the wreckage: the United States and the Soviet Union. They had been allies in defeating Nazi Germany and Imperial Japan. But they had fundamentally different visions of what the post-war world should look like, and within months of victory, they were locked in a rivalry that would shape global politics for the next 45 years.

💡 Fun Fact
In 1945, the United States produced roughly 50% of the world's entire industrial output. Its mainland had been untouched by bombing, its factories had run at full capacity throughout the war, and it had gone from the Great Depression to the world's most powerful economy in less than a decade. The contrast with Europe, whose cities, factories, and infrastructure lay in ruins, could not have been more stark. This economic dominance was the material foundation of American power in the Cold War era.

⚡ The Shift in Global Power: KC-6.2.IV.C.i

The AP CED identifies a key development: "Technological and economic gains experienced during World War II by the victorious nations shifted the global balance of power." Let's unpack what that means.

The United States entered the war late (1941) but mobilized with extraordinary speed. American factories produced more aircraft, ships, tanks, and weapons than any other nation. The wartime economy ended the Great Depression and transformed the US into the world's dominant industrial power. Most importantly, the US developed the atomic bomb, a weapon of unprecedented destructive power, first tested in July 1945 and used against Japan that August.

The Soviet Union suffered catastrophically, an estimated 26 million Soviet citizens died, and the western USSR was devastated by German occupation. But the USSR had industrialized rapidly in the 1930s, and its war production was extraordinary. The Red Army that defeated Germany was the world's largest and most battle-hardened military force. By 1949, the USSR had its own nuclear weapons, establishing the nuclear standoff that defined the Cold War.

Meanwhile, the traditional European great powers, Britain, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, were financially ruined. Britain had borrowed heavily from the US; France had been occupied for four years. These powers could no longer sustain their global empires. The age of European dominance was ending.

📝 AP Tip
The CED skill for Topic 8.1 is contextualization, placing historical developments in a broader context. On the AP exam, contextualization means explaining HOW and WHY a historical context relates to the development you're analyzing, not just listing it. For Unit 8, strong contextualization connects WWII's outcome (exhausted European empires, two superpowers, raised expectations) to the specific events of the Cold War and decolonization that follow.

🌐 Unfulfilled Promises: From WWI to WWII, KC-6.2.II

The AP CED notes that "hopes for greater self-government were largely unfulfilled following World War I; however, in the years following World War II, increasing anti-imperialist sentiment contributed to the dissolution of empires and the restructuring of states." This contrast between WWI and WWII is essential context.

After WWI, U.S. President Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points promised self-determination for all peoples. Colonial subjects across Asia and Africa took this seriously. Leaders like Ho Chi Minh (Vietnam) even attended the Paris Peace Conference hoping to petition for independence. They were ignored. The victorious European powers carved up the defeated Ottoman and German empires, but kept their own colonies firmly under control. The League of Nations' "mandate system" simply transferred German and Ottoman territories to British and French control under a thin veneer of international oversight.

The result? Deep resentment and radicalization of nationalist movements across the colonial world. Leaders who had hoped for peaceful reform turned to more radical strategies. Over the next two decades, nationalist movements organized, grew, and, after WWII, were ready.

After WWII, the situation was fundamentally different. Colonial soldiers had fought for their empires. Indian troops had served under British command across multiple theaters; African troops had fought for France; Vietnamese and Indonesians had been mobilized by European powers. They returned home with military training, organizational skills, and, critically, the expectation that their service would be rewarded with greater rights or independence. The Atlantic Charter (1941), signed by FDR and Churchill, had pledged "the right of all peoples to choose the form of government under which they will live." Colonial peoples cited this document constantly in their independence campaigns.

Sample Answers: a) The United States emerged from WWII with approximately 50% of global industrial output, an intact economy, and a monopoly on atomic weapons, making it the world's dominant power. Simultaneously, the Soviet Union had industrialized rapidly and fielded the world's largest land army. These two powers dwarfed the traditional European great powers, whose economies and infrastructures were devastated by the war, creating a bipolar power structure that set the stage for superpower rivalry. b) After WWI, European powers were weakened but not broken, and could still militarily and financially sustain their empires. Colonial peoples' appeals for self-determination at Paris (such as Ho Chi Minh's) were simply ignored. After WWII, Britain and France were so economically exhausted that they had to borrow from the US and could no longer sustain the military expenditure required to suppress independence movements across Asia and Africa, meaning decolonization became economically inevitable even when politically resisted. c) The United States often supported European colonial powers when they served Cold War strategic interests, for example, initially providing aid to France's effort to retain Vietnam because France was a NATO ally and the Viet Minh were communist. This showed that US anti-colonialism was subordinate to anti-communism rather than a genuine principle. Similarly, the USSR maintained strict imperial control over Eastern Europe, crushing the Hungarian uprising (1956) and Czech reform movement (1968), demonstrating that Soviet anti-colonialism applied to Western empires but not to Soviet ones.
Evaluate the extent to which World War II created the conditions for both the Cold War and the acceleration of decolonization in the period after 1945.
Model Thesis: World War II created the central conditions for both the Cold War and decolonization by simultaneously exhausting the European colonial powers that had dominated global politics, elevating two ideologically opposed superpowers into a rivalry that would reshape world politics, and intensifying anti-imperialist sentiment among colonial peoples whose service and sacrifices demanded a reckoning, making the post-1945 world a fundamentally different stage than the one that had existed before 1939.
Model answer: WWII simultaneously created two interconnected sets of conditions. First, it produced two superpowers (US and USSR) with incompatible ideologies and global ambitions, creating the structural precondition for the Cold War. Second, it exhausted the old European colonial powers (Britain, France, Netherlands) while simultaneously raising colonial peoples' expectations of independence through wartime service and promises like the Atlantic Charter. These two dynamics reinforced each other: the Cold War gave independence movements leverage (both superpowers rhetorical supported anti-colonialism), while decolonization gave the Cold War new theaters (newly independent states became arenas of superpower competition).
Model answer: After WWI, European powers were weakened but not broken. They could still suppress independence movements. Self-determination was applied in Europe but not in Asia and Africa. Colonial peoples' appeals at Paris were ignored. After WWII, three things had changed: (1) European powers were economically incapable of maintaining empires. Britain, for example, owed the US enormous war debts; (2) Both superpowers rhetorically supported decolonization, putting colonial powers on the defensive in international forums; (3) Nationalist movements had grown stronger and more organized over 25 additional years. The will, capacity, and international support for maintaining empires had all collapsed.
Model answer: The US often supported European colonial powers when they served Cold War strategic interests, helping France suppress the Vietnamese independence movement early on, supporting pro-Western authoritarian regimes in Iran and Guatemala, backing colonial allies in NATO. Its rhetoric of freedom didn't prevent support for dictators who opposed communism. The USSR, while verbally championing anti-colonial movements, maintained its own imperial system over Eastern Europe through force (crushing uprisings in Hungary 1956, Czechoslovakia 1968), and supported independence movements primarily when they showed signs of becoming communist, not out of principled anti-colonialism. Both superpowers used the language of freedom strategically.
Model answer: Atomic weapons fundamentally changed the nature of great-power competition. Direct military conflict between the US and USSR risked nuclear annihilation, a prospect so catastrophic that both sides avoided direct confrontation, hence "cold" (not hot) war. Nuclear weapons also concentrated power: only states that possessed them had true superpower status, which the USSR achieved in 1949 and which motivated both sides to prevent other nations from acquiring them. Nuclear deterrence forced the superpowers to compete indirectly, through proxy wars, propaganda, economic aid, and ideological competition, rather than through direct military confrontation.
