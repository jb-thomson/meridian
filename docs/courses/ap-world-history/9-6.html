AP World History · Unit 9: Globalization

9.6 Globalized Culture After 1900

🎭 Cultural Development🎵 Global Arts🛒 Consumer Culture📱 Social Media
  • LO-A: Politics and social change transformed the arts: The upheavals of the 20th century, world wars, decolonization, civil rights, the Cold War, drove explosive creativity in literature, music, film, and visual art. Artists responded to social change, challenged conventions, and used new media to reach global audiences. Popular culture went global in the second half of the century: Hollywood films, American rock and roll, British pop, Jamaican reggae, Indian Bollywood, and global sports (World Cup soccer, the Olympics) created shared cultural experiences across national borders. Television and radio, then the internet, accelerated this cultural integration. Consumer culture transcended borders: Global brands (Toyota, Coca-Cola), online commerce (Alibaba, eBay), and multinational retail brought similar products to consumers worldwide. A teenager in Seoul, São Paulo, or Lagos might wear the same jeans, drink the same soda, and watch the same streaming shows as one in New York. Globalization of culture was not homogenization: Cultural globalization didn't erase local diversity. Local cultures adapted, mixed with, and resisted global influences, creating new hybrid forms. Bollywood was Indian but had global audiences; K-pop was Korean but became a worldwide phenomenon. Culture flowed in multiple directions, not just from West to Rest.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. How did the 20th century's political upheavals shape artistic and cultural expression?
The traumatic events of the 20th century, WWI's devastation of European confidence, the Holocaust, Stalinist repression, colonial brutality, nuclear terror, profoundly shaped artists' responses to their world. Modernist movements in literature (James Joyce, Virginia Woolf), art (Picasso, Dali), and music (Stravinsky) broke with 19th-century conventions, embracing fragmentation, ambiguity, and the unconscious as ways to represent a world where traditional certainties had been destroyed. WWII produced responses from Paul Celan's Holocaust poetry to Joseph Heller's satirical Catch-22. The Cold War produced both propaganda art on both sides and artists who challenged their own societies. Soviet dissident writers, American beat poets, African novelists confronting colonialism's legacy. Social movements generated their own cultural expressions: the Harlem Renaissance (African-American literary and artistic flowering in the 1920s), Negritude literature, feminist art. Politics didn't just influence culture. It was inseparable from it.
2. How did Bollywood become a global cultural phenomenon while remaining distinctly Indian?
Bollywood (the Hindi-language film industry based in Mumbai) became one of the world's largest film industries by output and audience size, producing over 1,000 films annually and reaching audiences not just in India but across South Asia, the Middle East, Africa, and the South Asian diaspora in Europe, North America, and Australia. It became global precisely because it remained distinctly Indian, audiences around the world sought out something different from Hollywood's cultural vocabulary. Bollywood's distinctive combination of elaborate song-and-dance sequences, melodramatic storytelling, family themes, and specific visual aesthetics attracted not just Indian diaspora audiences but also African audiences who found Indian themes (joint families, romance, honor) resonant with their own cultural values. This illustrates a key point: cultural globalization doesn't mean everything becomes American or Western. It means multiple cultural forms can reach global audiences simultaneously, each maintaining its own distinctive character while mixing with others.
3. How did Coca-Cola become a global brand and what does this tell us about consumer culture globalization?
Coca-Cola's global spread illustrates several mechanisms of consumer culture globalization. Originally an American product, it expanded globally through military distribution (US troops drank it during WWII, and Eisenhower ordered bottling plants built in every country American forces occupied), then through aggressive marketing and franchising. By the 21st century, Coca-Cola was sold in every country except (for periods) Cuba and North Korea. Its marketing explicitly associated the product with American values, youth, freedom, optimism, which were aspirational to many people globally. But "global brands" don't spread uniformly: Coca-Cola adapted its marketing to local cultures, partnered with local bottlers, and competed with strong local beverages. Its global spread was both a triumph of American cultural influence and an example of the hybrid adaptation that characterizes true cultural globalization, as the company became genuinely embedded in local economies and cultures worldwide.

The Reading

🎨 Political Change Transforms Art and Culture (KC-6.3.IV.i)

The 20th century's extraordinary political upheavals, two world wars, totalitarianism, decolonization, the Cold War, civil rights struggles, transformed the arts as artists responded to, reflected, and challenged the world around them. The AP CED identifies this development: "Political and social changes of the 20th century led to changes in the arts and in the second half of the century, popular and consumer culture became more global."

The carnage of WWI shattered European confidence in civilization and progress, producing a wave of literary and artistic modernism that rejected traditional forms. Writers like Virginia Woolf and James Joyce experimented with stream-of-consciousness narration; painters like Picasso fragmented figures into cubist geometries; composers like Stravinsky abandoned harmonic conventions. These formal innovations expressed a world where old certainties, about progress, reason, and order, had been destroyed by industrial-scale slaughter.

WWII and the Holocaust produced further transformations. The German-Jewish poet Paul Celan wrote poetry that grappled with the impossibility of expressing the Holocaust in language. Existentialist writers like Jean-Paul Sartre and Albert Camus confronted the absurdity of human existence in a world capable of Auschwitz. Post-colonial literature emerged as African and Asian writers, from Chinua Achebe (Nigeria) to Gabriel García Márquez (Colombia), gave voice to experiences outside the European literary tradition, challenging the cultural assumptions that had framed colonialism as "civilization."

💡 Fun Fact
Reggae music, originating in Jamaica in the late 1960s, provides one of history's best examples of a small, poor nation's culture achieving global influence far exceeding its size. Jamaica has a population of under 3 million, yet Bob Marley's music is recognized worldwide, reggae rhythms have influenced pop music across every continent, and Rastafarian imagery appears in cultures from West Africa to Japan to Brazil. Marley's album Legend (1984) remains one of the best-selling albums of all time. Cultural influence is not proportional to economic or military power.

🌍 Arts and Entertainment Reflect Global Society (KC-6.3.IV.ii)

In the second half of the 20th century, arts and entertainment became increasingly globalized, both reflecting and driving the interconnectedness of human society. The CED lists several illustrative examples spanning different media and cultural forms: music (reggae), movies (Bollywood), social media (Facebook, Twitter), television (BBC), and sports (World Cup soccer, the Olympics).

Reggae, emerging from Jamaica's Black working class in the late 1960s, carried themes of social justice, anti-colonialism, and spiritual liberation. Bob Marley's music crossed national and cultural boundaries to achieve global popularity, his image became one of the most recognized in the world. Reggae's global spread demonstrated that musical forms from peripheral, non-Western cultures could achieve worldwide reach, not just American or British popular music.

Bollywood, India's massive film industry, produced far more films annually than Hollywood and reached audiences not only in India but across South Asia, the Middle East, East Africa, and among South Asian diaspora communities worldwide. Its distinctive combination of elaborate musical sequences, melodramatic storytelling, and specific visual aesthetics created a global cultural form that was unmistakably Indian yet internationally beloved.

Social media platforms, Facebook (founded 2004), Twitter (2006), later Instagram, YouTube, and TikTok, accelerated cultural globalization to an unprecedented degree. A video, meme, or idea created anywhere could go viral globally within hours. Social media connected diaspora communities, enabled global cultural conversation, and created new forms of celebrity, activism, and cultural production that transcended national boundaries.

Television, represented by the BBC in the CED, had already created cross-national audiences for news and entertainment from the 1950s onward. The BBC World Service reached hundreds of millions of listeners globally; BBC programming was watched worldwide. Global sports events, World Cup soccer (watched by a cumulative 3.5 billion people in 2018) and the Olympics, created shared spectacles that billions across the globe experienced simultaneously, creating rare moments of genuine global cultural participation.

🛒 Consumer Culture Goes Global (KC-6.3.IV.iii)

The globalization of popular culture was inseparable from the globalization of consumer culture, the world of branded goods, retail experiences, and identity-through-consumption that characterized 20th-century modernity. The CED identifies this as a distinct development: "Consumer culture became globalized and transcended national borders."

Online commerce, represented by Alibaba (China, founded 1999) and eBay (US, 1995), transformed retail by enabling consumers anywhere in the world to buy goods from sellers anywhere in the world. Alibaba became not just China's dominant e-commerce platform but a global marketplace; eBay connected buyers and sellers across 190 countries. Online commerce eliminated the geographic constraints that had previously limited markets to local or national scales. A craftsperson in rural Morocco could now sell directly to buyers in Canada; a factory in Guangzhou could reach consumers in Brazil.

Global brands, the CED lists Toyota and Coca-Cola as illustrative examples, became recognized symbols worldwide. Toyota, founded in Japan in 1937, grew into one of the world's largest automakers, with factories on every continent and cars in every major market. Coca-Cola, an American invention from 1886, became arguably the most globally recognized brand in history, sold in virtually every country on Earth and consumed by approximately 1.9 billion people daily. These global brands didn't just sell products; they sold lifestyles, aspirations, and cultural associations that crossed national and cultural boundaries.

The globalization of consumer culture raised significant questions. Critics worried about cultural homogenization, the fear that everywhere would come to look the same, with the same fast food restaurants, brand-name clothing, and entertainment content replacing local diversity. Others saw consumer culture globalization as a new form of cultural imperialism, particularly American cultural imperialism, imposed on the world through economic power. The next topic (9.7) explores the various forms of resistance these concerns generated.

💡 Fun Fact
The FIFA World Cup final in 2018 attracted a global television audience of approximately 1.12 billion viewers. The total cumulative audience for the entire 2018 World Cup tournament was estimated at 3.572 billion, roughly half the world's population. No other recurring event in human history has been watched by so many people simultaneously. The World Cup is perhaps the clearest single example of what the CED means by arts and entertainment "increasingly reflecting the influence of a globalized society."

🔄 Cultural Globalization: Synthesis, Not Just Spread

A crucial nuance often missed in discussions of cultural globalization: it was not simply the spread of Western (especially American) culture to the rest of the world, erasing local diversity. While American cultural influence was enormous, Hollywood, American music, American consumer brands, cultural globalization was a multi-directional process that created new hybrid forms.

Reggae spread from Jamaica and influenced British punk, American hip-hop, and African popular music, while itself being influenced by American rhythm and blues. Bollywood became globally popular and influenced Hollywood, which began incorporating Bollywood-style musical sequences. K-pop (Korean pop music) emerged in the late 1990s drawing on American hip-hop, Japanese pop, and Korean musical traditions, then achieved genuine global popularity, with Korean groups like BTS selling out stadiums worldwide. Telenovelas (Latin American soap operas) became globally popular, particularly in Africa. Culture moved in multiple directions simultaneously, creating not uniformity but a complex, layered global cultural landscape in which local identities persisted and mixed with global influences.

A. The global spread of American consumer brands, including Coca-Cola and McDonald's, provides some support for the cultural homogenization argument. By the late 20th century, these brands were recognizable in virtually every country on Earth, and the shopping malls, fast food restaurants, and entertainment complexes that spread with them created visually similar consumer landscapes from Shanghai to São Paulo to Lagos. Originating in Jamaica, a small island nation with a population of under 3 million, reggae drew on African musical traditions brought by enslaved Africans, American rhythm and blues spread by radio, and specifically Jamaican social and religious experiences. Bob Marley's music then spread globally, influencing British punk, American hip-hop, and African popular music, while also being influenced by them in subsequent iterations. Evaluate the extent to which globalization changed cultural interactions in the period 1900 to the present.
Globalization significantly changed cultural interactions in the period 1900 to the present by enabling arts, entertainment, and consumer culture to transcend national borders at unprecedented scale, but rather than creating cultural homogenization, globalization primarily produced new hybrid cultural forms as local cultures adapted to, mixed with, and sometimes resisted global influences, maintaining significant cultural diversity alongside shared global cultural experiences.
Model answer: The 20th century's political catastrophes, WWI, the Holocaust, colonialism, the Cold War, forced artists to grapple with questions about civilization, violence, and human nature that traditional cultural forms were inadequate to address. WWI's mechanized slaughter shattered faith in progress, producing modernist fragmentation in literature (Joyce, Woolf), art (Picasso), and music (Stravinsky). The Holocaust produced profound literary responses questioning whether art was even possible after such horror. Paul Celan's haunted poetry, Elie Wiesel's memoir, Hannah Arendt's political philosophy. Decolonization produced post-colonial literature from writers like Chinua Achebe (Things Fall Apart) and Frantz Fanon (The Wretched of the Earth) that challenged the European literary tradition's claim to universality and gave voice to non-Western experience. Civil rights movements produced an explosion of African-American art, literature, and music. Political change and cultural change were inseparable throughout the century.
Model answer: Bollywood illustrates the difference powerfully. Cultural homogenization would mean that as globalization spread Western (especially American) popular culture worldwide, local film industries would either imitate Hollywood or disappear. Bollywood did the opposite. It maintained its distinctive Indian cultural identity (elaborate song-and-dance sequences, melodramatic family sagas, specific aesthetic sensibilities derived from Indian classical traditions and mythology) while becoming globally successful. It didn't become more like Hollywood to succeed internationally; it succeeded precisely because it offered something different. African audiences, South Asian diaspora communities, and eventually mainstream audiences in many countries found Bollywood's cultural vocabulary appealing. This illustrates that globalization creates competition among multiple cultural forms rather than simply spreading one dominant culture, and that audiences worldwide actively seek diverse cultural experiences, not just Western ones.
Model answer: Earlier communication technologies, radio and television, were essentially broadcast media: a small number of producers created content that large audiences consumed passively. Cultural globalization through these media was largely one-directional: major cultural producers (Hollywood studios, BBC, major music labels) distributed content globally. Social media fundamentally changed this by enabling anyone with internet access to create and distribute content globally, democratizing cultural production. A Kenyan musician posting videos on YouTube could reach global audiences without a record label. A Turkish activist could organize internationally through Twitter. Social media also enabled real-time global participation in cultural events, people worldwide could comment on the same sports match, political event, or viral video simultaneously. This created a more genuinely interactive global culture, though it also enabled misinformation, political manipulation, and new forms of cultural conflict, as the next topic (9.7) addresses.
Model answer: The global spread of Toyota and Coca-Cola illustrates several important dynamics of consumer culture globalization. First, it demonstrates that consumer culture spread from multiple national origins, Toyota from Japan, Coca-Cola from the US, so global consumer culture was not purely American cultural imperialism, even if American brands were the most numerous and prominent. Second, global brands adapted to local contexts: Coca-Cola marketed differently in different countries, worked with local bottlers, and adjusted products for local tastes; Toyota built factories locally, hired local workers, and designed some vehicles for specific regional markets. Third, consumer goods became associated with aspirations and identities: buying Toyota or Coca-Cola in many markets signaled participation in modernity, prosperity, and global connectedness. Consumer culture globalization thus both spread similar products worldwide AND created new forms of local meaning around those products, as each community incorporated global brands into their own cultural contexts.
