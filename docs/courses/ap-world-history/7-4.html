7.4 Economy in the Interwar Period

💰 Economics🏛️ Government Role⚙️ Soviet Industrialization🌎 Global Depression
  • LO-A: The Great Depression shattered faith in laissez-faire capitalism — The 1929 stock market crash triggered a global depression: U.S. GDP fell 30%, unemployment hit 25% in the U.S. and even higher in Germany. The economic catastrophe discredited the 19th-century idea that free markets self-correct. Across the world, governments moved to take a more active role in managing their economies — through different ideological frameworks. Three models of government economic intervention emerged — (1) Liberal democracy: the U.S. New Deal (FDR) used government spending, jobs programs, and financial regulation to stimulate recovery without abandoning capitalism. (2) Fascist corporatism: Italy and Germany's fascist states directed private industry through state-corporate partnerships, combined with rearmament spending. (3) Soviet communism: Stalin's Five Year Plans eliminated private ownership entirely, using state planning to industrialize rapidly — at enormous human cost. The Soviet Five Year Plans: rapid industrialization, repressive costs — Stalin launched the First Five Year Plan in 1928, collectivizing agriculture and directing all investment to heavy industry. The USSR transformed from an agricultural society to an industrial power in one generation. The cost was catastrophic: forced collectivization caused a famine (especially in Ukraine, the Holodomor, 1932–33) that killed 3–7 million people; millions more died in labor camps (the Gulag); political purges eliminated potential opposition. AP exam comparison: governments' responses to the Depression — The AP CED illustrative examples are: the New Deal (U.S.), fascist corporatist economy (Italy/Germany), and governments with strong popular support in Brazil and Mexico. Know that all three represent different responses to the same crisis — different ideologies, different methods, different costs — but all involved expanding the state's role in the economy beyond pre-Depression limits.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Before the Great Depression, most Western governments followed "laissez-faire" economic policy — the idea that governments should not interfere in markets. Why did the Great Depression destroy confidence in this approach?
Laissez-faire policy assumed that markets were self-correcting: if prices fell too far or unemployment rose, market mechanisms would eventually restore equilibrium. The Great Depression proved this assumption catastrophically wrong at the worst possible moment. U.S. unemployment reached 25% by 1933; the economy had contracted 30%; thousands of banks had failed, wiping out people's savings. The self-correction mechanism wasn't working — or if it would eventually work, the political and human cost of waiting was unacceptable. Herbert Hoover's adherence to laissez-faire principles while the economy collapsed destroyed confidence in the approach not just among academics but among ordinary people who were losing their jobs, homes, and savings. The Depression created political space for very different ideas: Keynesian stimulus spending (New Deal), fascist state direction of the economy, and communist central planning — all of which promised government action where laissez-faire offered none.
2. Stalin's Five Year Plans transformed the Soviet Union from an agricultural country to an industrial power in roughly one generation. Why did rapid industrialization require such repressive methods?
Rapid industrialization requires massive capital investment — money to build factories, machines, and infrastructure. In a market economy, this capital comes from savings, investment, and profits over time. Stalin's problem was that the USSR was a poor agricultural country with little capital and he wanted to industrialize in years, not decades. The only way to generate investment capital was to extract it from agriculture — paying peasants less for their grain than it was worth, and using the difference to fund industrial investment. Collectivization forced peasants onto collective farms where the state could control grain output and prices. Peasants who resisted (the "kulaks" — better-off peasants) were killed, imprisoned, or deported. When collectivization disrupted grain production and the state continued extracting grain at high rates, famine resulted. The repression was not incidental — it was the mechanism by which the state extracted the resources needed for industrialization from a population that would not have given them voluntarily.
3. The New Deal, fascist corporatism, and Soviet planning all involved the government taking a more active role in the economy. What important difference distinguishes these three approaches?
The crucial distinction is the relationship between state and private property. The New Deal preserved capitalism — private ownership of businesses, farms, and property — while using government spending and regulation to stimulate the economy and protect individuals from the worst effects of market failure. It was capitalism with a safety net and government demand-stimulus. Fascist corporatism also preserved private property and private business, but directed business activity through state-industry partnerships and massive government rearmament spending; private owners kept their property but operated under strong state guidance. Soviet central planning eliminated private ownership entirely — the state owned all factories, farms, and resources and directed all economic activity through Five Year Plans. The fundamental ideological divide is capitalism vs. communism; fascism is a variant of state-directed capitalism. These differences had enormous implications for political freedom: Soviet and fascist systems required political repression to enforce their economic models; the New Deal operated within a democratic framework.
4. The CED mentions "governments with strong popular support in Brazil and Mexico" as examples of economic intervention during the interwar period. Why might economic crisis specifically generate governments with strong popular support?
Economic crises generate popular support for active government because they create large populations of desperate people who are suffering visibly and who attribute their suffering to the failure of the existing economic order. These populations become receptive to leaders who promise decisive action, scapegoating of elites or foreign interests, and state-directed solutions. In Brazil, Getúlio Vargas (in power 1930–1945) built popular support through labor protections, industrialization programs, and nationalist economic policies that reduced dependence on foreign capital. In Mexico, Lázaro Cárdenas (1934–1940) nationalized oil companies (PEMEX), distributed land to peasants, and built government social programs. Both leaders combined economic nationalism, state direction of the economy, and political mobilization of previously excluded groups (workers, peasants). Economic crisis also delegitimizes existing elites who are associated with the failed economy, creating space for new political coalitions.
5. The Great Depression began in the United States (1929 stock market crash) but quickly became a global economic crisis. What does this suggest about the degree of global economic integration by 1929?
It reveals that by 1929 the global economy was so integrated — through trade, finance, and investment — that a financial collapse in one country (the U.S.) could rapidly transmit across the entire world. The mechanisms of transmission: (1) American banks called in foreign loans, withdrawing capital from European and Latin American economies; (2) the U.S. enacted the Smoot-Hawley Tariff (1930), raising import tariffs, which prompted retaliatory tariffs from other countries, collapsing global trade; (3) the gold standard tied currencies to gold, meaning countries couldn't independently devalue or stimulate their economies without running out of gold reserves; (4) financial panic spread through interconnected banking systems. This global integration meant that the Depression's solutions also had to be at least partially global — but the political response was largely nationalist (trade barriers, currency devaluations) which deepened the crisis. The interwar period's failure to coordinate international economic responses contributed directly to WWII by enabling the economic conditions in which fascism thrived.

The Reading

📉 The Great Depression: When the World Economy Collapsed

On October 29, 1929 — "Black Tuesday" — the U.S. stock market crashed, triggering the worst economic crisis in modern history. By 1933, U.S. GDP had fallen 30%, unemployment had reached 25%, and approximately 9,000 banks had failed, wiping out people's savings. But the Depression wasn't just an American crisis: through trade and finance, the collapse spread globally. Germany — which had been borrowing American money to pay WWI reparations — was especially hard hit; unemployment reached 30% by 1932. Latin American commodity exports collapsed. Industrial production in Britain and France fell sharply.

The Depression was more than an economic crisis — it was a political crisis. Governments that failed to respond effectively lost legitimacy. The economic catastrophe discredited laissez-faire capitalism — the pre-war consensus that governments should leave markets alone. New models of government economic intervention filled the vacuum. The AP CED identifies three distinct models: liberal democratic (the U.S. New Deal), fascist corporatism (Italy and Germany), and Soviet communist planning (the Five Year Plans). In Latin America, nationalist governments like those in Brazil and Mexico also expanded state economic roles.

💡 Fun Fact
In 1932, at the height of the Great Depression, approximately one-third of all Americans were either unemployed or working reduced hours. In some industrial cities, unemployment reached 50–60%. "Hoovervilles" — shantytowns of makeshift shelters built by homeless people — appeared in major cities and were named mockingly after President Herbert Hoover, who opposed direct federal relief. When Franklin Roosevelt was inaugurated in March 1933, he said in his famous speech: "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself" — but also "the money changers have fled from their high seats in the temple of our civilization." It was a direct attack on the financial establishment whose laissez-faire policies had failed.

🇺🇸 The New Deal: Saving Capitalism from Itself

When Franklin D. Roosevelt took office in March 1933, he immediately launched the New Deal — a series of programs designed to provide relief for the unemployed, recovery for the economy, and reform to prevent future crises. The New Deal didn't end the Depression (WWII ultimately did that), but it stabilized the economy, restored public confidence, and permanently changed the relationship between the U.S. government and its citizens.

Key New Deal programs included:

  • Federal Emergency Relief Administration (FERA): Direct cash payments to unemployed families — the first federal welfare program.
  • Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC): Employed young men in national parks and forests, planting trees, building trails, and fighting fires.
  • Public Works Administration (PWA) and Works Progress Administration (WPA): Large-scale government construction projects (dams, highways, post offices, schools) creating millions of jobs.
  • Social Security Act (1935): Created the Social Security pension system and unemployment insurance — programs that still exist today.
  • Banking regulation: The Glass-Steagall Act separated commercial and investment banking, preventing the risky speculation that had contributed to the crash.

Crucially, the New Deal preserved capitalism — private property, private businesses, and market mechanisms remained. Roosevelt's goal was not to replace capitalism but to save it from its own excesses through government intervention. This is the key distinction from fascism and communism.

☭ Soviet Five Year Plans: Industrialization by Decree

While the West struggled with depression, the Soviet Union was attempting something unprecedented: industrializing a poor agricultural country through central state planning at breakneck speed. In 1928, Joseph Stalin launched the First Five Year Plan — a comprehensive blueprint for transforming the Soviet economy, setting specific production targets for steel, coal, electricity, machinery, and other industries.

The results in industrial terms were remarkable. By the late 1930s, the USSR had built massive steel mills (Magnitogorsk produced as much steel as the entire pre-revolutionary Russian Empire), hydroelectric dams (Dnieper Dam was the largest in the world), and a modern military-industrial complex. The Soviet Union industrialized in a decade what had taken Western countries half a century.

The human cost was catastrophic. Collectivization — forcing peasants onto state-run collective farms — met massive resistance from peasants who burned crops and slaughtered livestock rather than surrender them to the state. The resulting disruption of agriculture, combined with the state's continued extraction of grain for export, produced the Holodomor famine of 1932–33, which killed an estimated 3–7 million Ukrainians. Millions more were sent to the Gulag (forced labor camps) for resisting collectivization or failing to meet production quotas. The AP CED notes directly that Five Year Plans were "often implementing repressive policies, with negative repercussions for the population."

📝 AP Tip
The AP exam frequently asks you to compare different governments' economic responses to the Depression. The key comparison framework: New Deal = government intervention within capitalism, democratic, no mass violence; Fascist corporatism = state-directed capitalism, authoritarian, rearmament-driven; Soviet Five Year Plans = state replaces capitalism entirely, totalitarian, rapid industrialization through mass coercion. All three represent expanded state economic roles — but with radically different political structures and human consequences.

⚡ Fascist Corporatism: The "Third Way"

Fascist leaders in Italy and Germany presented their economic systems as a "third way" between capitalism and communism: private property would be preserved, but the state would direct the economy through state-industry partnerships called corporatism. Workers' unions and employers' associations were reorganized into state-controlled "corporations" that would set wages and prices under government guidance.

In practice, fascist economic policy relied heavily on rearmament spending. Germany under Hitler invested massively in military production — tanks, planes, ships, and munitions. This government spending created jobs and drove the German recovery, reducing unemployment from 30% in 1932 to under 5% by 1938. But it was military Keynesianism: the spending was building weapons, not schools or hospitals, and it was financed by deficit spending that would ultimately require territorial conquest and plunder to sustain. The economic "miracle" of Nazi recovery was essentially preparation for war.

🌎 Brazil and Mexico: Nationalist Alternatives

The interwar economic crisis also reshaped Latin American politics. In both Brazil and Mexico, governments with strong popular support expanded state economic roles in nationalist directions. Getúlio Vargas in Brazil (1930–1945) nationalized the steel industry, protected Brazilian manufacturers through tariffs, and built a labor movement loyal to his government. Lázaro Cárdenas in Mexico (1934–1940) — implementing the promises of the 1910 Revolution — nationalized foreign oil companies in 1938, creating PEMEX (which still exists), distributed land to peasants, and built a governing coalition of workers and peasants. Both leaders combined economic nationalism, state-directed industrialization, and popular mobilization without adopting fascism or communism — a specifically Latin American model of development.

💡 Fun Fact
When Lázaro Cárdenas nationalized Mexico's foreign-owned oil companies in 1938, it was a political sensation. British and American oil companies had been extracting Mexican oil for decades; their workers were often paid less than their foreign counterparts. When Cárdenas announced the nationalization, ordinary Mexicans — many of whom were very poor — voluntarily donated jewelry, livestock, and savings to help compensate the foreign companies. Schoolchildren brought coins to schools. Indigenous communities donated chickens and turkeys. It was a remarkable display of popular nationalism: people who had almost nothing were willing to sacrifice to ensure that Mexico's oil belonged to Mexico.

The AP CED states that following WWI and the Great Depression, "governments began to take a more active role in economic life." This development was most directly a response to
  • the failure of laissez-faire economic policy to prevent or address the Great Depression's mass unemployment and bank failures
  • the spread of Marxist ideology from the Soviet Union to Western democratic nations
  • pressure from organized labor movements that had been strengthened by wartime mobilization
  • international agreements requiring all League of Nations members to adopt stimulus spending
The Great Depression's catastrophic failure — 25% unemployment, thousands of bank failures, 30% GDP contraction — demonstrated that unregulated markets did not self-correct as laissez-faire theory predicted. This failure created both the political demand for government action and the intellectual space for alternatives to laissez-faire policy across the political spectrum.
According to the AP CED, Stalin's Five Year Plans were "often implementing repressive policies, with negative repercussions for the population." The most direct example of these negative repercussions was
  • the Soviet Union's defeat in the Russo-Japanese War, which discredited the industrialization program
  • the mass famine caused by forced collectivization, particularly in Ukraine, which killed millions of people
  • the collapse of Soviet industrial production due to inefficient central planning
  • widespread strikes by Soviet workers who rejected the collectivization of industrial factories
The Holodomor famine (1932–33) in Ukraine, caused by forced collectivization and continued grain extraction during famine conditions, killed an estimated 3–7 million people. This is the primary "negative repercussion for the population" referenced in the CED. The Gulag labor camp system and political purges were also repressive consequences, but the famine represents the most direct human cost of collectivization policy.
The New Deal (United States), fascist corporatism (Italy/Germany), and the Soviet Five Year Plans all involved governments taking an active role in the economy. The most important difference between these three approaches was
  • only the New Deal reduced unemployment; the other two failed economically
  • only the New Deal used deficit spending; the others relied on taxation
  • the New Deal preserved capitalism and democracy, while fascism maintained capitalism with authoritarianism, and Soviet planning eliminated capitalism entirely
  • the New Deal addressed the Depression through military rearmament, while the others used civilian job programs
The defining distinctions are (1) whether private property and capitalism were preserved, and (2) whether democratic governance was maintained. The New Deal kept both capitalism and democracy; fascism kept capitalism but eliminated democracy; Soviet communism eliminated both private capitalism and democratic governance. These are fundamental differences in political-economic systems, not just policy variations.
Mexico's nationalization of foreign oil companies (1938) under Cárdenas is best understood as an example of
  • Soviet-style communist revolution spreading to Latin America
  • the New Deal model of government stimulus being adapted for developing economies
  • fascist corporatism applied to the Latin American context
  • economic nationalism, in which a state asserted control over strategic resources previously owned by foreign companies
Mexico's oil nationalization was explicitly nationalist: foreign companies (British and American) had been extracting Mexican resources for decades, and the nationalization asserted Mexican sovereignty over its own natural wealth. It was not communist (Mexico did not abolish private property generally), not fascist (it was a democratic government), and not a New Deal copy (it was about resource sovereignty, not Depression stimulus).
Which of the following best explains why fascist economic policies in Germany reduced unemployment rapidly in the 1930s?
  • Massive government spending on military rearmament created jobs in defense industries and related sectors
  • The Nazi regime's removal of Jewish workers from the labor force freed up jobs for unemployed Germans
  • Germany's fascist government successfully negotiated debt relief from the U.S. and Britain, providing capital for economic investment
  • Germany's corporatist economic system reorganized businesses to increase efficiency, raising private sector employment
Nazi Germany's rapid unemployment reduction was driven primarily by massive deficit-financed government spending on military rearmament — building the Wehrmacht, Luftwaffe, Kriegsmarine, and their supporting industries. This was essentially military Keynesianism: government spending creating demand and jobs, but directed entirely toward preparing for war rather than social infrastructure. While choice B occurred (anti-Jewish persecution removed Jewish professionals), this was not an economic cause of recovery — Jews constituted less than 1% of the German population.
Compare ONE similarity and ONE difference between the United States' New Deal and Stalin's Five Year Plans as responses to economic crisis in the 1930s.
Similarity: Both the New Deal and the Five Year Plans represented a significant expansion of the government's active role in managing the national economy, departing from the pre-war liberal assumption that governments should leave markets alone. Both involved large-scale state-directed investment in infrastructure and productive capacity as instruments of economic management. Difference: The New Deal preserved private ownership of the economy and operated within a democratic political framework — businesses remained privately owned, and Roosevelt's policies were subject to Congressional approval and judicial review. Stalin's Five Year Plans eliminated private ownership entirely (collectivizing agriculture and nationalizing all industry) and were implemented through totalitarian coercion, including forced collectivization that killed millions through famine and the Gulag labor camp system.
Evaluate the relative importance of economic and ideological factors in explaining the differences among governments' responses to the Great Depression in the period 1929–1939.
Although all Depression-era governments faced the same economic crisis, ideological frameworks were more important than economic conditions in determining the character of their responses: the United States' pre-existing commitment to democratic capitalism produced the regulatory New Deal; Germany's fascist nationalism produced military rearmament; and the Soviet Union's communist ideology produced forced collectivization — demonstrating that the same economic problem can produce radically different policy responses when filtered through different political ideologies.
Model answer: The phrase reveals that Roosevelt was not a socialist or a communist — he genuinely believed in capitalism and private property. But he understood that unregulated capitalism had produced the Depression and that without government intervention, capitalism might collapse entirely — creating conditions for revolution or fascism (which he saw happening in Europe). His goal was to reform capitalism enough to restore public confidence and prevent political radicalization. "Save capitalism from itself" means: the market system was its own worst enemy when left completely unregulated; government intervention (financial regulation, unemployment insurance, jobs programs) could preserve the essential capitalist framework while preventing the extreme instability that was destroying it. This is a fundamentally different position from laissez-faire (government shouldn't intervene) or socialism (government should replace capitalism). It's the position that became the dominant framework for Western liberal democracies for the rest of the 20th century.

Arguments that the industrialization was "worth it": the USSR's industrial capacity allowed it to defeat Nazi Germany in WWII — without the tanks, planes, and steel produced by the Five Year Plans, Hitler might have conquered the Soviet Union and potentially all of Europe. If you accept this counterfactual, you might argue that the deaths from collectivization were "smaller" than the deaths that would have occurred under Nazi occupation of the USSR. Arguments that it wasn't worth it: the Holodomor and collectivization were not inevitable — they were the result of specific policy choices (continuing grain extraction during famine); a more gradual industrialization was possible; the political repression killed and imprisoned millions beyond those killed by famine; and the Soviet system created by Stalin's methods produced decades of totalitarian oppression. A useful framework: distinguish between the goal (industrialization) and the method (forced collectivization and mass repression) — was the method necessary for the goal, or were alternatives available? Most historians now argue the extreme brutality was not necessary and reflected Stalin's political goals as much as economic requirements.
Model answer: This is precisely the kind of question historians and ethicists grapple with, and it's important to think carefully about it. Economic recovery was real and did significantly improve the daily lives of many ordinary Germans in the 1930s — which helps explain why Hitler had genuine popular support. But several things complicate "economic success" as a moral standard. First, the recovery was built on rearmament spending that was explicitly preparation for aggressive war and genocide — the economic success was the foundation of the Holocaust and WWII, not a separate achievement. Second, it was financed through deficit spending and theft (of Jewish-owned property and businesses after anti-Semitic laws stripped Jews of economic rights) — not sustainable economics. Third, economic metrics like unemployment don't capture who benefits and at whose expense: German Jews, political opponents, and others were systematically excluded and destroyed. An economic framework that judges regimes purely by GDP or unemployment rates is incomplete. Moral evaluation requires asking: who benefits? At whose expense? What were the methods? What were the ultimate uses of the economic power generated?
Model answer: Nationalization (state ownership of industries) is a tool that can serve very different ideological goals. In Mexico, nationalization of oil was specifically nationalist in character: it was about reclaiming Mexican natural resources from foreign (British and American) corporations that were extracting wealth and paying below-market wages. PEMEX remained a state-owned company in an otherwise capitalist economy — Mexico did not nationalize all industries, did not abolish private property, and did not follow a communist economic model. The goal was economic sovereignty (control of strategic resources) and popular legitimacy (demonstrating that the Revolution had delivered tangible benefits). In the USSR, nationalization of all industries was ideologically Marxist: the point was to abolish private ownership of the means of production entirely, eliminating the capitalist class as a category. The Soviet model was totalizing; the Mexican model was selective. Same tool (nationalization), completely different political projects.
Model answer: Liberal democratic capitalism ultimately won — the fascist model was destroyed in WWII, and Soviet communism collapsed in 1989–1991. But the victory wasn't guaranteed and wasn't primarily due to economic performance alone. After WWII, the American-led liberal democratic model was associated with military victory (the Allies defeated fascism) and with post-war prosperity (the Marshall Plan rebuilt Western Europe). The Soviet model appeared to be succeeding economically through the 1950s–60s (rapid industrialization, space program), but ultimately stagnated because central planning is inefficient at allocating resources in a complex economy — it can build tanks and steel mills but struggles with consumer goods, innovation, and adaptation. The Soviet collapse in 1989–1991 was triggered by economic failure more than military defeat. Fascism was eliminated militarily. But it's worth noting that "liberal democratic capitalism" after WWII incorporated many New Deal-style elements — social safety nets, financial regulation, labor rights — that were themselves a response to the Depression. The competition changed all three models: the "winner" looked different in 1980 than it had in 1938.
