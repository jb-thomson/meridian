1.1 The World in 1750: Political and Social Structures on the Eve of Revolution

🌍 Global
  • Land-based empires ran the world: In 1750, most political power sat with large, land-based agrarian empires and monarchies, Qing China, the Mughal Empire in South Asia, the Ottoman Empire, and absolutist states in Europe like France and Russia. Nearly all depended on farming, taxed peasants, and a small ruling elite.
  • Hierarchy was the default, not the exception: Almost every major society organized itself into a strict social order that people were mostly born into. Europe had its estates system, South Asia had caste, China had a Confucian hierarchy topped by scholar-officials, and social mobility was rare nearly everywhere.
  • A global economy was already forming: Long before any revolution began, trade routes were tying distant regions together, most visibly the Atlantic system, which linked Europe, West Africa, and the Americas through the forced labor of enslaved Africans. That growing interconnection helped set the stage for the political upheavals to come.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. What did most of the world's major political systems in 1750 have in common?
Most were land-based agrarian empires or monarchies that relied on farming and taxing a mostly peasant population, ruled by a small hereditary or educated elite, whether that was a European absolutist monarch, the Qing emperor, the Mughal emperor, or the Ottoman sultan.
2. Name two different examples of rigid social hierarchy in 1750 and briefly describe how each one organized people.
Europe's estates system divided French society by law into clergy, nobility, and everyone else, while South Asia's caste system divided people into hereditary groups tied to occupation and ritual status; China's Confucian hierarchy placed scholar-officials at the top based on passing civil service exams.
3. How was the Atlantic system already connecting distant parts of the world before any of the major revolutions began?
The Atlantic system linked Europe, West Africa, and the Americas through a triangular trade route that carried manufactured goods, enslaved Africans, and cash crops like sugar and tobacco between continents, showing that a global economy was forming well before 1750.
Learning Objectives
  • Describe how different world regions were ruled in 1750.
  • Explain how social class decided who held power, and who did not.
  • Explain how these old systems helped set up the revolutions that followed.
Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: A system of government in which a monarch holds total, unchecked political power, typically justified as a right granted by God rather than by the consent of the people. Significance: The dominant model of political authority across much of Europe and Asia in 1750, and the exact target that Enlightenment thinkers and later revolutionaries would attack. Example: Louis XIV of France, who famously (if perhaps apocryphally) is said to have declared, "L'État, c'est moi" ("I am the state").
  • Definition: The political and religious doctrine holding that a monarch's authority comes directly from God, not from the people, making the monarch answerable to God alone. Significance: Provided the ideological justification for absolutism; challenging divine right was a radical, dangerous idea before the Enlightenment made it mainstream. Example: European monarchs styled themselves as ruling "by the grace of God," a phrase still found on old coins and royal decrees.
  • Definition: The legal division of French society into three fixed orders, or estates: the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate), and everyone else (Third Estate), which included peasants, urban workers, and the middle class. Significance: A clear example of how European societies formalized inequality into law; the Third Estate paid nearly all taxes despite having the least power, a tension that would explode in the French Revolution. Example: A wealthy merchant in the Third Estate could pay significant taxes while a poor rural noble in the Second Estate paid almost none, simply because of birth.
  • Definition: The educated ruling class in Qing China who earned their elite status and government positions by passing rigorous civil service examinations based on Confucian texts, rather than through military conquest or hereditary title alone. Significance: Shows that not every 1750 hierarchy was based purely on birth; China's system rewarded education, though in practice wealth still made passing the exams far easier. Example: A young man from a landowning family might study for a decade to pass the imperial exams and earn a position as a district magistrate.
  • Definition: A rigid social hierarchy in South Asia that divided people into hereditary groups tied to occupation and ritual status, with almost no possibility of moving between groups. Significance: One of the most durable and restrictive social hierarchies in world history, shaping daily life, marriage, and occupation for the vast majority of the South Asian population under Mughal and later British rule. Example: A family born into a caste associated with leatherworking would typically remain in that occupation and social status for generations.
  • Definition: The network of trade connecting Europe, West Africa, and the Americas from roughly the 1500s through the 1800s, built around the forced transport and enslavement of millions of Africans to produce cash crops like sugar and tobacco. Significance: One of the clearest examples of an emerging global economy already in place by 1750, and a system whose profits helped fund the very European states that would soon be shaken by revolution. Example: A ship might carry manufactured goods from Europe to West Africa, enslaved people from West Africa to the Caribbean, and sugar from the Caribbean back to Europe, a route historians call the triangular trade.
The Reading

A World of Land-Based Empires

Picture a world map from 1750. You would not see the countries we know today. Instead, you would see a few giant, land-based empires, each run by a small ruling group at the top. In East Asia, the Qing dynasty ran the largest, richest empire on Earth. In South Asia, the Mughal Empire still held power, though it was weakening. The Ottoman Empire stretched across North Africa and the Middle East, ruling many different religions and cultures. And across Europe, kings and queens practiced absolutism: claiming almost total control over their people.

Nearly all of these empires ran on the same fuel: farming. Most people in 1750, whether in France, China, or the Ottoman Empire, worked the land. Whoever controlled that land, and the taxes it produced, controlled the state. And that control usually passed down through family, not through any kind of vote.

📝 Study Tip
Do not assume Europe was automatically the most powerful region in 1750. Qing China had a bigger population, a bigger economy, and arguably a sharper government than any European state. Europe's global dominance came later. It was not the 1750 starting point.

Hierarchy Was the Rule, Not the Exception

Almost every major society in 1750 sorted people into a strict hierarchy they were mostly born into. In France, the Estates System divided everyone by law into three groups: the clergy, the nobility, and everyone else. The clergy and nobility held huge privileges, including paying almost no taxes, while everyone else, about 97 percent of the population, paid nearly all of them.

  • Europe (Estates System): Legal status split into clergy, nobility, and commoners. The top two groups paid almost no taxes.
  • South Asia (Caste System): Hereditary groups tied to occupation and status, with almost no way to move between them.
  • China (Confucian Hierarchy): Scholar-gentry earned top status by passing exams on Confucian texts, though wealthy families still had the edge.
  • Ottoman Empire (Millet System): Religious communities, called millets, largely ran their own affairs under the sultan's rule.

These systems looked different, but they did the same job: they told you, at birth, roughly what role you would play and how much power you could ever hold. That kept these societies stable for centuries. It also meant peasants, women, enslaved people, and lower castes had almost no say in their own government.

💡 Fun Fact
In pre-revolutionary France, a struggling peasant could pay a higher tax rate than a wealthy noble living at the palace of Versailles. That imbalance became one of the sparks of the French Revolution just a few decades later.
📝 Study Tip
When comparing hierarchies across regions, be specific. Instead of writing "Europe had classes and India had castes," explain the actual mechanism: legal birth into an estate versus birth into a hereditary occupation group, and how much movement each system allowed.

An Emerging Global Economy

Even though most people in 1750 lived locally, trade was already pulling the world together, especially through the Atlantic System. European ships carried goods to West Africa, traded them for enslaved Africans, then carried those enslaved people across the Atlantic to work on plantations. Ships then carried crops like sugar and tobacco, grown by enslaved labor, back to Europe. Historians call this the triangular trade, and it made European merchants and monarchies enormously wealthy.

This new wealth did not erase the old hierarchies of 1750, but it did put pressure on them. Merchants grew rich but had no formal political power under systems like the Estates System. New ideas traveled the same trade routes as goods did. And the brutality of the slave trade would soon become a moral and political flashpoint of its own. Rigid hierarchies, land empires under new economic strain, and a growing web of global trade: the world in 1750 was already set up for major change.

Respond to Parts A, B, and C. Each response should be a complete sentence; bullet points alone are not acceptable.

Part A

Identify the name of the broader system that legally divided French society into these three groups.

This is the Estates System, sometimes called the Old Regime, which divided French society by law into the clergy, the nobility, and everyone else.

Part B

Explain one reason this tax imbalance created political tension in pre-revolutionary France.

The imbalance created resentment because the group with the least formal political power and privilege, the Third Estate, was forced to fund the state almost entirely, while the wealthiest and most privileged groups contributed comparatively little, making the system feel deeply unjust to the majority of the population.

Part C

Explain how a similarly rigid hierarchy functioned in one other region of the world in 1750, and identify one key difference from the French example.

In South Asia, the caste system divided people into hereditary groups tied to occupation and ritual status; unlike the French Estates System, which was primarily a legal and tax classification, caste governed nearly every aspect of daily and religious life, and unlike the Third Estate, movement between castes was essentially impossible regardless of wealth.
Evaluate the extent to which rigid social hierarchies across different world regions in 1750 shared common causes and functions, despite their different specific forms.

Write a full essay with thesis, contextualization, evidence, and historical reasoning. This prompt rewards comparing at least two specific hierarchies (such as France's Estates System, South Asia's caste system, or China's Confucian hierarchy) rather than describing them in isolation.

Strong thesis: "Although the Estates System in France, the caste system in South Asia, and the Confucian hierarchy in China took different specific forms, all three functioned to concentrate political and economic power in a small hereditary or educational elite while limiting the vast majority of the population to a fixed social role, reflecting a broader global reliance on agrarian economies that rewarded stability and control over social mobility."

  • Contextualization: Describe the shared agrarian, land-based foundation of most major world societies in 1750, which relied on stable, hierarchical labor systems to function.
  • Evidence, Europe: Explain the structure and function of France's Estates System, including the tax burden placed on the Third Estate despite its lack of formal privilege.
  • Evidence, South Asia and/or China: Explain the structure and function of the caste system and/or the Confucian scholar-gentry hierarchy, noting where mobility was possible (as with civil service exams) versus essentially impossible (as with caste).
  • Historical reasoning: comparison: Directly compare the systems, identifying at least one genuine similarity (concentrating power, limiting mobility) and one genuine difference (legal estate versus hereditary caste versus merit-based exam), and explain what that comparison reveals about how 1750-era societies organized power.
Model answer: A strong answer explains that agrarian economies depend on a stable, predictable labor force tied to the land, and that a fixed hierarchy, whether by estate, caste, or birth, made it easier for ruling elites to guarantee that labor and tax revenue year after year, discouraging the kind of social mobility that might disrupt that stability.
Model answer: A strong answer acknowledges the genuine difference, an exam-based path to status versus a purely inherited one, while also noting the limits: wealthy families could better afford the years of study needed to pass China's civil service exams, meaning the system still favored the already-privileged even though it was not purely hereditary.
Model answer: A strong answer explains that Atlantic trade wealth strengthened monarchies and merchant classes in the short term, but also created a growing merchant class with money but no formal political power under systems like the Estates System, and eventually fueled moral and political arguments against the very hierarchies and practices that had generated that wealth.
Model answer: Answers will vary, but a strong response identifies a specific region (such as France, given its tax imbalance and Enlightenment ideas, or the Americas, given colonial tensions) and connects a specific 1750-era condition, like rigid hierarchy or economic pressure, to a plausible reason it might break down.
