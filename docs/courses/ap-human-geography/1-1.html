AP Human Geography · Unit 1: Thinking Geographically

1.1 Introduction to Maps

🗺️ Spatial
  • Two basic map types: Reference maps show the location of physical or political features (like a road atlas). Thematic maps show the spatial pattern of a specific topic or variable, like population density or election results.
  • Maps reveal spatial patterns: distance and direction (absolute or relative), clustering (grouped closely), dispersal (spread apart), and elevation (height above sea level).
  • Every map distorts something: Because the Earth is a sphere and a map is flat, every map projection must trade off accuracy in shape, area, distance, or direction. No projection can preserve all four at once.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. What is the key difference between a reference map and a thematic map?
A reference map shows the general location of physical and human features, like roads or borders, while a thematic map shows the spatial pattern of a specific topic or variable, like income or disease rates.
2. What is the difference between a clustered pattern and a dispersed pattern?
A clustered pattern shows similar things grouped closely together in one area, while a dispersed pattern shows things spread far apart across an area.
3. Why can no map projection preserve shape, area, distance, and direction all at the same time?
Because the Earth is a three-dimensional sphere and a map is a two-dimensional flat surface, translating one to the other always requires some mathematical distortion, so every projection has to sacrifice accuracy in at least one of those four properties.
Learning Objectives
  • Identify types of maps geographers use to represent data.
  • Identify the types of spatial patterns and relationships portrayed in maps.
  • Explain the strengths and limitations associated with different map projections.
Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: A map designed to show the absolute location of physical and human-made features, such as roads, boundaries, cities, and rivers. Significance: The most familiar type of map; used when the goal is simply finding where something is located. Example: A road atlas or a political map of the world showing country borders.
  • Definition: A map designed to show the spatial distribution or pattern of a specific topic, theme, or variable, rather than general location. Significance: The main tool geographers use to visualize and analyze data spatially; most AP Human Geography stimulus maps are thematic. Example: A choropleth map shading each county by median income, or a dot density map showing where population actually clusters within a state.
  • Definition: The exact, measurable distance between two places (in miles or kilometers) and the precise compass direction from one to the other. Significance: Gives a fixed, objective answer that does not depend on who is asking or how they are traveling. Example: Chicago is 790 miles due east of Denver.
  • Definition: Distance and direction described in relation to another place or factor, such as travel time, cost, or a landmark, rather than in exact units. Significance: Often matters more than absolute distance for real decisions, since a place that is close in miles can feel far if it is hard to reach. Example: "The store is a quick bus ride north of downtown" describes relative distance and direction, not an exact figure.
  • Definition: A spatial pattern in which similar things are grouped closely together in one area. Significance: Clustering shows up constantly in human geography, from industries to population to disease outbreaks, and spotting it is a core map-reading skill. Example: Technology companies clustering in Silicon Valley.
  • Definition: A spatial pattern in which things are spread far apart from one another across an area. Significance: The opposite of clustering; recognizing which pattern a map shows is often the first step in explaining why it exists. Example: Cattle ranches spread thinly across the Great Plains.
  • Definition: The height of a location above sea level, typically shown on maps through color shading or contour lines. Significance: A spatial pattern that shapes settlement, agriculture, and climate, and appears often on both reference and thematic maps. Example: A physical map shading mountain ranges brown and lowlands green to show elevation at a glance.
  • Definition: A mathematical method for representing the curved surface of the Earth on a flat map, which always introduces some form of distortion. Significance: Every map you have ever seen uses some projection, and understanding what that projection distorts is essential to reading maps critically. Example: The Mercator projection preserves direction and shape but dramatically distorts the size of landmasses near the poles.
  • Definition: A map projection developed in 1569 that preserves angles and shape, making it useful for navigation, but severely distorts the relative size of landmasses, especially near the poles. Significance: The most historically dominant world map projection, but also the most criticized for making high-latitude regions like Greenland and Russia look far larger than they actually are relative to equatorial regions. Example: On a Mercator map, Greenland appears roughly the same size as Africa, even though Africa is actually about fourteen times larger.
The Reading

Two Kinds of Maps, Two Different Jobs

Geographers use maps constantly, but not all maps do the same job. A reference map answers a simple question: where is this? It shows physical features like mountains and rivers, or human-made features like roads, cities, and borders. A road atlas, a subway map, and a world political map are all reference maps.

A thematic map answers a different question: how does one topic change across space? Instead of showing everything in a region, it isolates one variable, like population density, income, or election results, and shows how that variable changes from place to place. A choropleth map (shading regions by color) and a dot density map (placing dots to show concentration) are both common ways to build a thematic map. Most maps you will see in this course, and on the AP exam, are thematic maps, since they are the tool geographers use to make an argument about spatial patterns.

📝 AP Tip
If an exam question shows you a map and asks what TYPE of map it is, look at what the map is actually trying to communicate. If it is just showing you where things are, it is a reference map. If it is showing you how a specific variable changes across space (using color shading, dots, or symbols tied to data), it is a thematic map. This distinction shows up constantly in Unit 1 multiple choice questions.

Reading Spatial Patterns on a Map

Beyond just showing where things are, maps reveal patterns in how things are arranged across space. Absolute distance and direction give an exact, measurable answer, a specific number of miles, a precise compass bearing. Relative distance and direction describe location in relation to something else instead, often in terms of travel time, cost, or a landmark, rather than an exact figure. A destination can be close in absolute miles but feel relatively far if the roads are bad or traffic is heavy.

  • Clustering is a pattern where similar things group closely together, like technology companies concentrated in Silicon Valley or hospitals concentrated in a city's medical district.
  • Dispersal is the opposite pattern, things spread thinly across a wide area, like cattle ranches scattered across the Great Plains.
  • Elevation, a location's height above sea level, is its own spatial pattern, usually shown through color shading (green lowlands fading to brown mountains) or contour lines connecting points of equal height.
📝 AP Tip
Exam questions often show a map and ask you to describe the spatial pattern it displays. Practice naming the pattern precisely: is it clustered or dispersed? Is a direction or distance given in absolute terms (an exact number) or relative terms (compared to something else)? Precise vocabulary earns credit; vague description ("there's a lot in one area") often does not.

Why Every Map Lies a Little

Here is a problem geographers cannot avoid: the Earth is a sphere, and a map is flat. There is no way to turn a curved surface into a flat page without distorting something. That translation is called a map projection, and every projection has to pick which properties to keep and which to sacrifice: shape, area, distance, or direction. No projection can keep all four at once. Maps are also always selective: no map can show everything in a place, so every mapmaker chooses what to include and what to leave out.

The best-known example is the Mercator projection, made in 1569 for ocean navigation. It preserves angles and shape, which made it useful for sailors plotting a straight compass course. But that accuracy has a cost: it badly distorts the size of land near the poles. On a standard Mercator map, Greenland looks about the same size as Africa. In reality, Africa is about fourteen times larger.

💡 Fun Fact
Because the Mercator projection was the default classroom map for so long, it shaped how generations of students pictured the size of different regions, inflating places like Europe and Russia while shrinking Africa and South America. Other projections, like the Gall-Peters, were built to fix this size distortion, though they introduce distortions of their own.
📝 AP Tip
You do not need to memorize the names of every map projection for the exam, but you should be able to explain, in general terms, that all projections distort something, and be able to identify what a GIVEN projection sacrifices (shape, area, distance, or direction) based on how it visually distorts a specific region, especially at high latitudes near the poles.

One Stimulus
A classroom wall map uses the Mercator projection. On this map, Greenland appears about the same size as Africa. Africa's actual land area is about fourteen times larger than Greenland's.

Respond to parts A, B, C, and D.

A

Identify the property of the Earth-to-map translation that this projection preserves well.

The Mercator projection preserves angles and shape, which made it useful for navigation and straight-line compass courses.

B

Describe the distortion shown by the Greenland-Africa comparison.

The map badly inflates land area at high latitudes, so Greenland looks far larger relative to Africa than it really is.

C

Explain why Greenland is distorted more than Africa on this map.

Mercator stretches areas near the poles to keep angles straight. Greenland sits at a much higher latitude than Africa, so that stretching inflates Greenland more.

D

Explain one possible consequence of using this map as a default classroom world map.

Students may leave with a skewed sense of how large high-latitude regions are compared with equatorial regions, which can quietly inflate the perceived size and importance of places like Greenland, Russia, or Europe relative to Africa.
