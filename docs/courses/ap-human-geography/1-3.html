AP Human Geography · Unit 1: Thinking Geographically

1.3 The Power of Geographic Data

🗺️ Spatial
  • Maps are never neutral: Every map reflects choices, what data to include, how to draw boundaries, which colors to use, and those choices can shape how a viewer understands or judges a place, sometimes intentionally.
  • Gerrymandering shows this clearly: Politicians can redraw legislative district boundaries, using packing and cracking, to manipulate election outcomes without changing a single vote, purely by changing how voters are grouped on a map.
  • The boundaries you choose change the pattern you see: The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem shows that the exact same underlying data can appear to show completely different spatial patterns depending on how the areas used to group that data are drawn.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. What is the basic difference between packing and cracking as gerrymandering techniques?
Packing concentrates opposition voters into a small number of districts so their votes are wasted on huge winning margins in those few districts, while cracking spreads opposition voters thinly across many districts so they never form a majority in any single one.
2. Explain, in your own words, what the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem shows about spatial data.
It shows that the exact same underlying data can appear to reveal completely different spatial patterns depending on how the boundaries used to group that data are drawn, meaning the choice of boundary is not a neutral technical decision but one that can genuinely change what the data seems to say.
3. Why is redistricting, a normal and legally required process, also the moment when gerrymandering typically occurs?
Redistricting is the specific legal window when district boundaries can be redrawn, so it is also the opportunity for whichever party controls that process to draw boundaries strategically in their own favor, using techniques like packing and cracking.
Learning Objectives
  • Explain how maps and data can be used to communicate information and can also be manipulated to serve a particular purpose.
  • Explain the process and political consequences of gerrymandering.
  • Explain the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem and its effect on spatial data analysis.
Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: The practice of drawing electoral district boundaries to favor a particular political party or group, rather than drawing them based on neutral criteria like population balance or geographic compactness. Significance: A concrete, high-stakes example of how map boundaries can be manipulated for political advantage without changing a single actual vote. Example: A state legislature redrawing a congressional district into an oddly shaped strip specifically to include or exclude certain neighborhoods based on their voting history.
  • Definition: A gerrymandering technique that concentrates voters of one party into as few districts as possible, so that party "wastes" votes winning those districts by overwhelming margins instead of winning more districts by smaller margins. Significance: One of the two core gerrymandering strategies tested on the AP exam, alongside cracking. Example: Drawing one oddly shaped urban district that combines several majority-opposition neighborhoods into a single district the opposing party wins with 90 percent of the vote, wasting many of those votes.
  • Definition: A gerrymandering technique that splits a concentrated group of voters across multiple districts, diluting their influence so they cannot form a majority in any single district. Significance: The other core gerrymandering strategy tested on the AP exam, working in the opposite direction from packing. Example: Splitting a city's opposition-leaning population across four different districts, each of which also contains a larger number of the favored party's rural or suburban voters.
  • Definition: The statistical and cartographic problem that the same underlying spatial data can produce different, sometimes contradictory, apparent patterns depending on how the boundaries used to group that data are drawn. Significance: Explains why the choice of district, county, or region boundaries is never a neutral technical decision, it can genuinely change what conclusion the data appears to support. Example: A city's crime data might look evenly distributed when grouped by large districts, but reveal sharp clustering in specific blocks when grouped by smaller units.
  • Definition: The process of redrawing electoral district boundaries, typically required after each national census to reflect population changes. Significance: The formal process through which gerrymandering usually occurs, since it is the legally required moment when district lines can be legitimately redrawn. Example: Following the U.S. Census every ten years, states redraw congressional and state legislative districts to reflect population shifts.
  • Definition: The distortion of a viewer's understanding of data caused by deliberate or accidental choices in how that data is displayed, such as misleading color scales, cropped axes, or inappropriate map projections. Significance: A broader category covering how the ideas behind gerrymandering and MAUP apply to data presentation generally, beyond just electoral maps. Example: A choropleth map using a color scale that makes a small statistical difference between regions look dramatic, or an enormous difference look minor.
The Reading

Data Can Inform, or It Can Persuade

So far, this unit has treated maps as neutral tools: here is where things are, here is how a variable is spread out. But maps are made by people, and people make choices, what data to include, how to draw boundaries, which colors to use. Those choices can shape how a viewer understands, judges, or even votes about a place. Sometimes that happens by accident. Sometimes it is entirely intentional.

The clearest example of this power, and one the AP exam returns to often, is gerrymandering: drawing electoral district boundaries to favor one political party or group instead of using neutral criteria like equal population or sensible shapes.

How Gerrymandering Actually Works

Gerrymandering relies on two core strategies. Packing concentrates voters of one party into as few districts as possible. Say an opposition party's voters are spread across a city. Instead of letting them naturally win several districts, mapmakers draw one oddly shaped district that combines nearly all of them. That party might win the packed district with a huge 90 percent margin, but every vote past the 51 percent needed to win is wasted, votes that could have won other districts instead.

Cracking works the opposite way: instead of concentrating opposition voters, it splits them apart across several districts, each also holding more of the favored party's voters. In every cracked district, the opposition stays a minority and cannot win a single seat, even if their total numbers across the region are large.

📝 AP Tip
Packing and cracking are often tested as a pair. If a question describes voters concentrated into one oddly shaped district that wins by a landslide, that is packing. If it describes a group split apart so it loses everywhere, that is cracking. Learn both terms together, since exam questions often ask you to tell them apart using a map or scenario.
💡 Fun Fact
The term "gerrymander" comes from Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry, who in 1812 signed off on a state senate district so oddly shaped a newspaper cartoonist compared it to a salamander. "Gerrymander" blends Gerry's name with "salamander," and the practice has continued in American politics for over two centuries.

Gerrymandering usually happens during redistricting, the formal process of redrawing district boundaries, required by law after each national census to reflect population changes. Redistricting itself is normal and necessary. Gerrymandering is what happens when whoever controls that process draws lines for partisan advantage instead of neutral, population-based criteria.

The Modifiable Areal Unit Problem

Gerrymandering is one example of a bigger geography problem called the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem, or MAUP. MAUP is the fact that the exact same underlying data can show different, even contradictory, patterns depending only on how the boundaries used to group that data are drawn.

Take a city's crime data. Grouped by a few large districts, crime might look evenly spread across the city, since high-crime blocks get averaged together with low-crime blocks. Grouped by many small units, like individual blocks, sharp clustering might suddenly appear: crime concentrated in a few specific spots, invisible in the large-district version. No data changed. Only the boundaries changed, and the pattern changed with them.

📝 AP Tip
MAUP is a favorite concept for free-response questions because it connects directly to gerrymandering: both show boundary choice is never a neutral detail. If a prompt asks why the same data can support two different conclusions, MAUP is very likely the concept being tested, even if the question does not use that exact term.

Reading Maps and Data Critically

None of this means maps and data cannot be trusted. It means they need to be read critically, the same way a careful reader questions the source of a written argument. A useful habit: whenever you see a map or data visualization, ask who made it, what choices they made about boundaries, scale, and color, and whether a different, equally honest set of choices could tell a different story with the same data. That is not cynicism. It is the exact skill this unit is building, since nearly every map you analyze in this course was made by someone who made choices, and understanding those choices is part of understanding the map.

One Stimulus
A state legislature redraws U.S. House districts after the census. In District 4, nearly all voters from one party are packed into a single oddly shaped district. In the surrounding districts, the remaining voters from that party are cracked into slim minorities. The statewide popular vote is close, but the map produces a lopsided House delegation.

Respond to parts A, B, C, and D.

A

Identify the political process described in the scenario.

The scenario describes gerrymandering, specifically packing and cracking voters by party when district lines are drawn.

B

Describe the difference between packing and cracking as used in the scenario.

Packing concentrates one party's voters into a single district so they win that seat by a huge margin. Cracking splits the rest of those voters across other districts so they cannot win those seats.

C

Explain how this map uses geographic data to affect an election without changing any individual vote.

The same voters still live in the same places and can still cast the same ballots. Changing the boundaries changes which voters are grouped together, so the same votes produce a different set of winners.

D

Explain one reason maps like this show that geographic data is not neutral.

Someone chose those boundaries for a political result. A different set of lines, using the same census data, could produce a different delegation, so the map is a decision, not a transparent picture of the state.
Two Stimuli
Stimulus 1. A map of a metropolitan area using county boundaries shows one solid color for "moderate" average income across the whole metro.
Stimulus 2. A map of the same metro using census-tract boundaries shows a sharp split: high-income tracts on one side of a river and low-income tracts on the other.

Respond to parts A, B, C, and D. Use both stimuli.

A

Identify the change in scale from Stimulus 1 to Stimulus 2.

Stimulus 1 uses a coarser, county scale. Stimulus 2 uses a finer, census-tract scale for the same metro area.

B

Describe how the spatial pattern of income looks different on the two maps.

At the county scale, income looks evenly moderate. At the tract scale, income looks sharply unequal, with rich and poor areas divided by the river.

C

Explain why the same underlying data can produce two different patterns.

This is the modifiable areal unit problem: grouping the same points into different areas (counties versus tracts) changes the average in each unit, so the map can hide or reveal inequality depending on the boundaries.

D

Explain one decision that could be distorted if a planner used only Stimulus 1.

A planner who saw only the county map might conclude the metro does not have a serious income divide and skip targeted services, even though the tract map shows a clear rich-poor split that county averages had smoothed away.
