AP Human Geography · Unit 1: Thinking Geographically

1.2 Geographic Data

🗺️ Spatial
  • Qualitative vs. quantitative data: Quantitative data is numerical and can be measured (population counts, income levels). Qualitative data describes qualities, patterns, or experiences that are harder to reduce to a single number (interview responses, cultural practices).
  • Modern data collection tools: GPS pinpoints exact locations using satellites. Remote sensing collects data about the Earth's surface from satellites or aircraft without physical contact. Both feed into GIS, which stores and analyzes the data.
  • Data still needs a display method: Once collected, geographic data has to be turned into a specific kind of map, choropleth, dot density, graduated symbol, or isoline, and each display method fits certain kinds of data better than others.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Give one example of quantitative geographic data and one example of qualitative geographic data.
Quantitative data could be the population of a city (a specific number). Qualitative data could be residents' described sense of community pride in a neighborhood survey (a quality or experience, not a number).
2. How does remote sensing allow geographers to study areas that would otherwise be difficult to research?
Remote sensing collects data from satellites or aircraft without requiring anyone to physically visit the location, making it possible to study huge, remote, or dangerous areas, like deep rainforest or active wildfire zones, quickly and repeatedly.
3. Why might a dot density map reveal a clustering pattern that a choropleth map of the same data would hide?
A choropleth map applies a single average color to an entire predefined area (like a whole county), which can smooth over internal variation, while a dot density map places individual dots based on actual concentration, revealing precisely where a population or variable clusters within that same area.
Learning Objectives
  • Explain the differences between qualitative and quantitative geographic data.
  • Explain how geographers collect data using tools such as GPS, remote sensing, and field observation.
  • Explain how different types of data are best represented using different types of thematic maps.
Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: Data expressed in numerical form that can be measured, counted, or statistically analyzed. Significance: The backbone of most thematic maps in this course, since numerical data can be sorted into ranges and mapped using color, size, or symbols. Example: Median household income by county, or the number of people per square mile in a city.
  • Definition: Data that describes qualities, characteristics, or experiences that are not easily reduced to a single number, often collected through interviews, observation, or open-ended survey responses. Significance: Captures cultural context and lived experience that pure numbers can miss, complementing quantitative data rather than replacing it. Example: Interview responses describing why residents of a neighborhood feel unsafe walking at night, which a crime statistic alone would not fully explain.
  • Definition: A satellite-based navigation system that allows a receiver to determine its precise location on Earth's surface using signals from multiple orbiting satellites. Significance: Provides the precise location data that underlies most modern mapping, from smartphone navigation apps to scientific field research. Example: A biologist using a handheld GPS unit to record the exact coordinates where an endangered species was spotted.
  • Definition: The collection of data about the Earth's surface from a distance, typically using satellites or aircraft equipped with cameras or sensors, without physical contact with the area being studied. Significance: Allows geographers to gather data over huge, difficult-to-access, or even hazardous areas quickly and repeatedly over time. Example: Satellite imagery used to track deforestation in the Amazon rainforest year over year.
  • Definition: A thematic map that uses different colors or shading to represent the value of a variable within predefined areas, such as countries, states, or counties. Significance: One of the most common thematic map types, well suited to quantitative data that is already organized by administrative boundary. Example: A U.S. election results map shading each state red or blue based on which candidate won.
  • Definition: A thematic map that uses individual dots, each representing a set quantity of something, to show the distribution and relative concentration of a variable across space. Significance: Useful for showing precise clustering and density patterns that a choropleth map's solid color shading would smooth over or hide. Example: A map where each dot represents 1,000 people, visually clustering more densely in cities than in rural areas.
The Reading

Two Kinds of Data Geographers Work With

Before geographers can make a map, they need data, and geographic data comes in two basic forms. Quantitative data is numerical: it can be counted, measured, and statistically analyzed. Population counts, average rainfall, median income, and unemployment rates are all quantitative. This kind of data is what most thematic maps are built from, since numbers can be sorted into ranges and displayed using color, size, or symbols.

Qualitative data, by contrast, describes qualities, characteristics, or lived experiences that are harder to reduce to a single number. Interview responses, descriptions of local customs, and open-ended survey answers are all qualitative. A crime rate is quantitative data; a resident's description of why she feels unsafe walking home at night is qualitative data. Both are legitimate and valuable in geography. Quantitative data tells you the scale and pattern of something. Qualitative data often tells you why that pattern exists, or what it actually feels like to live inside it.

📝 AP Tip
A common exam trap is assuming quantitative data is automatically "more objective" or "better" than qualitative data. Resist that framing. The correct AP Human Geography position is that qualitative and quantitative data answer different kinds of questions, and strong geographic research often combines both rather than treating one as superior.

How Geographers Actually Collect Data

Modern geography relies on several major technologies for collecting spatial data. GPS, the Global Positioning System, uses a network of orbiting satellites to let any GPS receiver, a phone, a car, a handheld field unit, determine its precise location on Earth's surface. GPS is the reason a smartphone can tell you exactly where you are and guide you turn by turn to a destination, and it is also a basic field research tool: a biologist recording exact coordinates for wildlife sightings, or a geologist marking precise sample locations, both depend on GPS.

Remote sensing takes a different approach: collecting data about the Earth's surface from a distance, using satellites or aircraft equipped with cameras and sensors, without any physical contact with the location being studied. Remote sensing lets geographers monitor huge or hard-to-reach areas repeatedly over time. A single research team could never physically survey the entire Amazon rainforest every year to track deforestation, but a satellite can photograph the same region on a regular schedule, letting researchers compare images over time and measure exactly how much forest cover has been lost.

💡 Fun Fact
The first GPS satellite was launched in 1978, but the system was originally built for U.S. military navigation, not civilian use. Selective Availability, a feature that deliberately degraded GPS accuracy for civilian receivers, was not turned off until the year 2000, instantly making every civilian GPS device roughly ten times more accurate overnight.

Geographers also still rely on more traditional methods: field observation, physically visiting a location to record data firsthand, and large-scale surveys like a national census, a comprehensive count and demographic survey of a country's population, usually conducted every ten years in the United States. Unlike GPS or remote sensing, these methods often capture qualitative detail, direct observation, resident interviews, that satellite data alone cannot provide.

Turning Data Into a Map

Collecting data is only half the job. Geographers then have to choose how to visually represent that data, and different data types and questions call for different map types.

  • A choropleth map shades predefined areas, states, counties, countries, using different colors to represent the value of a variable in each area. It works well for data that is already organized by administrative boundary, like an election map shading each state by winning party, but it can visually overstate the importance of large, sparsely populated areas.
  • A dot density map places individual dots, each representing a set quantity, across the map to show where a variable actually concentrates. It reveals clustering that a choropleth map's flat color would smooth away, since population, for example, is rarely spread evenly within a single state or county.
  • Other thematic map types, like graduated symbol maps (using differently sized symbols) and isoline maps (using connected lines of equal value, like a weather map's temperature contours), each fit specific kinds of data better than others.
📝 AP Tip
If an exam question gives you raw data and asks which type of thematic map would best represent it, ask yourself two questions: is the data organized by a fixed boundary (favors choropleth), and does the data cluster unevenly within that boundary in a way worth showing (favors dot density)? Getting this reasoning right, not just naming a map type, is what the rubric is looking for.

No Stimulus

Respond to parts A, B, C, and D. Use complete sentences.

A

Define quantitative geographic data.

Quantitative geographic data is numerical information that can be measured or counted, such as population, income, or rainfall totals.

B

Define qualitative geographic data.

Qualitative geographic data describes qualities, meanings, or experiences that are not easily reduced to a single number, such as interview answers, field observations, or descriptions of cultural practice.

C

Explain how GPS data differs from remote-sensing data.

GPS gives a precise location on Earth's surface using satellite signals. Remote sensing collects information about the surface from satellites or aircraft without touching it, such as land cover or temperature, rather than just a point coordinate.

D

Explain why a geographer would use GIS after collecting that data.

GIS stores the data in layers and lets the geographer combine, compare, and map those layers, which is how raw location or satellite data becomes an analyzable thematic map.
One Stimulus
County-level data used to make a map of a U.S. state - Median household income (dollars) - Number of hospitals per 100,000 people - Average drive time to the nearest hospital (minutes) - Results from 40 resident interviews about access to care

Respond to parts A, B, C, and D.

A

Identify which items in the list are quantitative data.

Median household income, hospitals per 100,000 people, and average drive time are quantitative. The interviews are qualitative.

B

Describe one thematic map that could be made from a quantitative item in the list.

A choropleth map of median household income by county, with darker shades for higher income, would show the spatial pattern of wealth across the state.

C

Explain one limitation of mapping only the quantitative items.

Numbers like drive time do not capture why people skip care, whether they trust hospitals, or how they describe access. The interview data would add that meaning, which a purely numeric map would miss.

D

Explain how a GIS user could combine two of the quantitative layers to support a planning decision.

Overlaying low-income counties with long drive times to a hospital could identify places where a new clinic would serve people who are both poorer and farther from existing care.
Model answer: A strong answer names a specific question (such as why a neighborhood is gentrifying) where numbers alone (rising rents, changing demographics) cannot capture the full picture, and identifies a specific qualitative source (resident interviews, oral histories) that could add the missing context of lived experience and motivation.
Model answer: It suggests that many geographic and mapping technologies are first developed for government, military, or strategic purposes and only later become widely available for civilian and commercial use, a pattern worth watching for in other current or emerging spatial technologies.
Model answer: A strong answer would likely favor a different approach than either default choice, but if forced to choose, could argue for dot density (showing precise fire locations as they are detected) over choropleth (which would falsely imply uniform fire coverage across an entire administrative area), while acknowledging real-time fire maps often actually use other specialized techniques like heat-intensity color gradients.
