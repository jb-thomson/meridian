AP Government · Unit 1: Foundations of American Democracy

1.1 Ideals of Democracy

🏛️ Political
  • Natural rights and the social contract: Enlightenment thinkers like Locke argued that people are born with natural rights (life, liberty, property) and form governments by consent to protect those rights. If a government fails to protect them, the people may alter or replace it.
  • Five conceptions of democracy: Participatory democracy emphasizes broad citizen involvement, pluralist democracy emphasizes group competition for influence, and elite democracy emphasizes limited popular control run by educated, capable representatives. The U.S. system blends all three.
  • Applying the theories: Foundational documents and institutions can each be read through more than one democratic lens. Knowing which theory a given example supports, and why, is a core AP Gov skill.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. According to Locke, what happens if a government fails to protect the natural rights of its citizens?
The people have the right to alter or abolish that government and form a new one, since the government's legitimacy depends entirely on protecting the rights it was created to secure.
2. Which democratic theory best explains the existence of thousands of registered lobbying organizations in Washington, D.C.?
Pluralist democracy, since it holds that organized groups compete to influence policy and that this competition, not direct citizen participation, is how the public's varied interests get represented.
3. How would a participatory democrat and an elite democrat likely disagree about low voter turnout in local elections?
A participatory democrat would see low turnout as a crisis of legitimacy, since direct citizen involvement is the whole point. An elite democrat would be less alarmed, since they see capable representatives, not mass participation, as what actually makes government work well.
Learning Objectives
  • Explain the theoretical linkages between natural rights, the social contract, and limited government.
  • Explain how the different perspectives on the role of government self-interest, the common good, and personal liberty influenced the U.S. political system.
  • Explain how democratic ideals are reflected in the U.S. Constitution.
Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: Rights that belong to all people simply by virtue of being human, existing independent of government. Locke identified life, liberty, and property as the core natural rights. Significance: The Declaration of Independence is built on this idea; government exists to secure these rights, not grant them. Example: "Life, Liberty, and the pursuit of Happiness" in the Declaration of Independence directly echoes Locke's formulation.
  • Definition: The theory that legitimate government arises from an agreement among individuals who consent to give up some freedoms in exchange for protection of their rights. Significance: Justifies both the creation of government and the people's right to overthrow one that breaks the agreement. Example: The Declaration of Independence lists grievances against George III to argue Britain broke the social contract with the colonies.
  • Definition: A political system in which government power is restricted by law, usually through a written constitution, to protect individual rights. Significance: Distinguishes the American system from absolute monarchy and is enforced through separation of powers, checks and balances, and federalism. Example: The Bill of Rights limits what Congress can do, even with a legislative majority.
  • Definition: A model of democracy that emphasizes broad, direct participation by citizens in politics and civil society, not just through voting. Significance: Justifies institutions like town meetings, referenda, and grassroots organizing as central, not optional, to democratic legitimacy. Example: New England town meetings, where residents vote directly on local budgets and ordinances.
  • Definition: A model of democracy in which multiple competing groups, interest groups, unions, advocacy organizations, bargain and compete for influence over policy, and government acts as a referee among them. Significance: Explains why interest groups and lobbying are treated as a normal, even healthy, part of American democracy rather than corruption. Example: Environmental groups and industry associations both lobbying Congress over the same energy bill.
  • Definition: A model of democracy in which a small number of educated, wealthy, or well-positioned individuals hold most political power, with limited direct participation by ordinary citizens. Significance: Explains institutional design choices, like the original method of electing senators, that insulated decisions from direct popular control. Example: The Electoral College, which allows electors rather than a national popular vote to formally select the president.
The Reading

Where American Ideas About Government Came From

The delegates who wrote the Declaration of Independence and, later, the Constitution were not inventing political theory from scratch. They were applying ideas from the European Enlightenment, a period of the 17th and 18th centuries when philosophers began arguing that political authority should rest on reason and consent rather than on the divine right of kings.

The most influential of these thinkers for American purposes was the English philosopher John Locke. Locke argued that in a hypothetical "state of nature," before governments existed, individuals already possessed natural rights, life, liberty, and property, simply because they were human. Government, in Locke's view, was not the source of these rights. It was a tool people created to protect rights they already had.

📝 AP Tip
When an exam question asks you to "explain the theoretical linkages" between natural rights and limited government, always work in this order: natural rights exist first, people consent to government to protect them (the social contract), and government is therefore limited to only the powers needed for that protection. Skipping a step is a common way to lose credit on this learning objective.

The Social Contract and the Declaration of Independence

Locke's theory of the social contract holds that legitimate government arises when free individuals agree, implicitly or explicitly, to give up a portion of their personal freedom in exchange for protection of their natural rights. Critically, Locke argued this agreement runs in both directions: if government violates the contract by failing to protect natural rights, or by actively abusing them, the people retain the right to alter or abolish that government.

This is precisely the argument Thomas Jefferson makes in the Declaration of Independence. The document opens with a near-paraphrase of Locke ("Life, Liberty, and the pursuit of Happiness" in place of Locke's "life, liberty, and property"), then spends most of its length listing specific grievances against King George III. That list is not decoration. It is the evidence required by social contract theory: proof that the British government had broken its side of the agreement, which is why the colonies were justified in forming a new one.

💡 Fun Fact
Locke's original formulation protected "life, liberty, and property," not "the pursuit of happiness." Historians still debate why Jefferson made the substitution. One common explanation is that Jefferson wanted to avoid appearing to defend slaveholders' property rights in enslaved people at the exact moment he was accusing the king of violating natural rights, though the document itself never resolves this contradiction.

Limited Government as the Practical Result

If government exists only to protect rights that pre-exist it, then government power must be limited to that purpose and no further. This is the idea of limited government: political authority that is constrained, usually by a written constitution, so that it cannot simply do whatever a temporary majority wants. The Framers built limited government into the Constitution's structure itself, through the separation of powers among three branches, a system of checks and balances between them, and federalism dividing power between national and state governments. The Bill of Rights, added in 1791, listed specific things government could never do, regardless of majority opinion, reinforcing the same principle in a different form.

Five Ways to Define "Democracy"

"Democracy" is not one single idea. AP Gov asks you to work with three competing models of what democratic government should actually look like in practice, and each one justifies different institutions.

  • Participatory democracy holds that broad citizen involvement, not just voting but organizing, protesting, attending public meetings, is what makes government legitimate. It justifies institutions like New England town meetings, ballot initiatives, and grassroots political movements.
  • Pluralist democracy holds that individuals rarely influence government alone; instead, organized groups, interest groups, unions, professional associations, compete and bargain for influence, and government's job is to referee that competition fairly. It justifies the constitutional right to petition government and explains why lobbying is treated as normal political activity rather than corruption.
  • Elite democracy holds that most citizens lack the time, information, or expertise to make good policy decisions directly, so government works best when educated and capable representatives make decisions on the public's behalf, with popular control limited mostly to selecting those representatives. It justifies institutions that insulate decision-making from direct majority pressure, like an independent judiciary or (originally) the state-legislature election of U.S. senators.

None of these three theories fully describes the American system on its own. The Constitution blends all three: participatory elements (direct election of the House), pluralist elements (protected rights to assemble, petition, and organize), and elite elements (the Electoral College, lifetime-appointed federal judges, and, until the Seventeenth Amendment in 1913, indirect election of senators).

📝 AP Tip
A released free-response question will often describe a specific institution or scenario and ask you to identify which democratic theory it best supports, and explain why. Practice this both directions: given an institution, name the theory; given a theory, generate your own example institution. The College Board explicitly tests both.

Part A

Describe the concept of the social contract as Locke presents it in the passage above.

Locke argues that because all people are naturally free and equal, no government can legitimately hold power over them unless they have consented to it; political authority is created by an agreement among free individuals, not imposed on them from outside.

Part B

In the context of the scenario, explain how the ideas in the passage are reflected in a specific provision of the Declaration of Independence.

The Declaration's statement that governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed" directly restates Locke's consent principle, and the document's long list of grievances against King George III functions as the evidence needed to justify withdrawing that consent.

Part C

Explain how the perspective in the passage differs from an elite democratic view of political legitimacy.

Locke's passage grounds legitimacy in the consent of every individual member of society, while elite democratic theory grounds legitimacy in capable representatives making sound decisions on the public's behalf, with much less emphasis on the ongoing, individual consent of each citizen.
