1.4 Federalist No. 10 and Brutus No. 1

🏛️ Political
  • Federalist No. 10 (Madison): A large republic controls the dangers of faction better than a small direct democracy, because a bigger, more diverse society makes it harder for any single majority faction to form and dominate everyone else.
  • Brutus No. 1 (Anti-Federalist): A republic covering a territory as large as the United States cannot work; representatives will be too few and too distant to understand ordinary citizens, and a strong central government will inevitably grow at the expense of the states and individual liberty.
  • Same problem, opposite conclusions: Both documents worry about the same danger, government power abused against the people, but Madison trusts a large, structurally divided republic to contain that danger, while Brutus trusts only a small, closely watched government to do it.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. According to Madison in Federalist No. 10, why is a large republic better than a small direct democracy at controlling the dangers of faction?
In a large republic, society contains so many different factions with competing interests that no single majority faction can easily form, coordinate, and dominate everyone else, unlike in a small democracy where a majority faction can organize quickly and oppress the minority.
2. What is the core worry expressed in Brutus No. 1 about a national government covering a territory as large as the United States?
Brutus argues that representatives in a government that large will be too few and too distant to genuinely understand or reflect the interests of ordinary citizens, and that a strong central government will inevitably absorb power that should belong to the states.
3. Both Madison and Brutus worry about the same underlying danger. What is it, and how do their proposed solutions differ?
Both worry about government power being abused against the people. Madison's solution is to make the republic large and structurally divided so no single faction can dominate it, while Brutus's solution is to keep government small, local, and closely watched by the people it governs.
Learning Objectives
  • Explain the political philosophy underlying Federalist No. 10.
  • Explain the political philosophy underlying Brutus No. 1.
  • Explain how the perspectives in Federalist No. 10 and Brutus No. 1 reflect a divide over the ideal size and scope of the federal government.
Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: A group of citizens, whether a majority or a minority, united by a shared interest or passion that Madison believed was often adverse to the rights of others or the interests of the community as a whole. Significance: The entire argument of Federalist No. 10 is built around how to control the dangers factions pose without destroying liberty. Example: Madison's own examples include groups organized around debtors versus creditors, or landed versus manufacturing interests.
  • Definition: A series of 85 essays written by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay under the pen name "Publius" to persuade New York voters to ratify the Constitution. Significance: Federalist No. 10, written by Madison, is one of the most frequently cited primary sources on the AP exam. Example: Federalist No. 10 and Federalist No. 51 are the two Federalist Papers most likely to appear as an exam stimulus.
  • Definition: Opponents of ratifying the Constitution who feared it created a national government too powerful and too distant from the people, at the expense of the states and individual liberty. Significance: Brutus No. 1, one of the most important Anti-Federalist essays, is Federalist No. 10's required counterpart on the exam. Example: Anti-Federalist pressure during ratification is a major reason the Bill of Rights was added in 1791.
  • Definition: A system of government in which power is held by the people but exercised through elected representatives, rather than directly by citizens themselves. Significance: Madison's central claim in Federalist No. 10 is that a large republic, not a small direct democracy, is the best defense against the dangers of faction. Example: Madison distinguishes a "pure democracy," where citizens govern directly, from a "republic," where they govern through representatives.
  • Definition: The risk that in a democracy, a majority faction can use its numbers to pass laws that violate the rights of a minority or work against the public good. Significance: This is the exact danger Madison sets out to solve in Federalist No. 10 without simply abolishing majority rule. Example: Madison worried that in a small democracy, a majority faction could easily coordinate to seize power and oppress everyone else.
  • Definition: The constitutional clause (Article I, Section 8) giving Congress the power to pass laws needed to carry out its other enumerated powers. Significance: Brutus No. 1 specifically warned this clause, combined with the Supremacy Clause, would let the federal government expand its power almost without limit. Example: Brutus argued the clause was written so broadly that state governments would eventually be swallowed by it.
  • Definition: The constitutional clause (Article VI) declaring that the Constitution and federal laws made under it are "the supreme law of the land," binding on every state, even when a state law conflicts with them. Significance: The provision Brutus No. 1 paired with the necessary and proper clause to argue the national government would inevitably grow at the states' expense. Example: A state law that directly conflicts with a valid federal law is overridden by the Supremacy Clause, regardless of when either law was passed.

Two Essays, One Argument About Size

During the ratification debate of 1787 to 1788, Americans split into two camps. Federalists supported ratifying the new Constitution and its stronger national government. Anti-Federalists opposed it, fearing it concentrated too much power too far from the people. Each side produced influential written arguments, and the AP exam expects you to know two of them by name: Federalist No. 10, written by James Madison, and Brutus No. 1, an Anti-Federalist essay usually attributed to Robert Yates.

Both essays worry about the same danger: a government, or a majority within that government, abusing its power against the rights of citizens. Where they sharply disagree is on the solution. Madison argues that a large, structurally complex republic is the safest design. Brutus argues that only a small, closely accountable government can be trusted at all.

Federalist No. 10: The Problem of Faction

Madison opens Federalist No. 10 by naming the danger he intends to solve: faction, which he defines as any group of citizens, majority or minority, united by a shared interest or passion that works against the rights of others or the good of the community. Madison saw factions as an unavoidable feature of free societies; as he put it, the causes of faction are "sown in the nature of man," and the only way to eliminate faction entirely would be to eliminate liberty itself, a cure Madison considered far worse than the disease.

Since faction cannot be removed, Madison argues it must instead be controlled. His solution rests on two structural choices already built into the proposed Constitution. First, a republic, where citizens govern through elected representatives rather than directly, refines and filters public opinion through those representatives rather than acting on raw, unmediated passion. Second, and more distinctively, Madison argues that a large republic is safer than a small one. In a small democracy, a single faction can more easily become a majority, coordinate quickly, and oppress everyone else. In a large republic covering many states and containing many competing economic, religious, and regional interests, so many different factions exist that no single one can easily dominate the rest. Faction is not eliminated. It is diluted until it becomes manageable.

Among the numerous advantages promised by a well-constructed Union, none deserves to be more accurately developed than its tendency to break and control the violence of faction... By a faction, I understand a number of citizens, whether amounting to a majority or a minority of the whole, who are united and actuated by some common impulse of passion, or of interest, adverse to the rights of other citizens, or to the permanent and aggregate interests of the community. There are two methods of curing the mischiefs of faction: the one, by removing its causes; the other, by controlling its effects. There are again two methods of removing the causes of faction: the one, by destroying the liberty which is essential to its existence; the other, by giving to every citizen the same opinions, the same passions, and the same interests... Liberty is to faction what air is to fire, an aliment without which it instantly expires... The inference to which we are brought is, that the causes of faction cannot be removed, and that relief is only to be sought in the means of controlling its effects... A republic, by which I mean a government in which the scheme of representation takes place... promises the cure for which we are seeking... [It serves] to refine and enlarge the public views, by passing them through the medium of a chosen body of citizens... Extend the sphere, and you take in a greater variety of parties and interests; you make it less probable that a majority of the whole will have a common motive to invade the rights of other citizens; or if such a common motive exists, it will be more difficult for all who feel it to discover their own strength, and to act in unison with each other.

📝 AP Tip
If a released FRQ or MCQ stimulus quotes Madison arguing that "extend the sphere" (his phrase for enlarging the republic) makes it "less probable" that a majority faction will form, that is Federalist No. 10, and the answer they want is almost always some version of: a large, diverse republic contains so many competing factions that none can dominate.
💡 Fun Fact
Madison originally intended Federalist No. 10 mainly to answer a specific, well-known objection: the widely accepted political theory of the time, drawn largely from Montesquieu, held that republics could only survive in small territories, since a large one would be too diverse to govern as a single political community. Madison's essay flips that conventional wisdom on its head, arguing that size is a strength, not a weakness.

Brutus No. 1: The Case Against a Large Republic

Brutus No. 1 makes almost the opposite argument. Brutus agreed with the conventional wisdom Madison was trying to overturn: a republic can only function well in a small territory, where representatives are close enough to the people to actually understand and reflect their interests. A republic as large and diverse as the proposed United States, Brutus warned, would inevitably produce a government too distant from ordinary citizens to represent them faithfully.

Brutus's second major concern was about the scope of national power itself. He focused specifically on the necessary and proper clause, which gives Congress power to pass laws needed to carry out its other constitutional powers, combined with the Constitution's Supremacy Clause, which makes federal law superior to conflicting state law. Brutus argued this combination was written so broadly that the national government would inevitably expand its own power over time, gradually absorbing authority that properly belonged to the states, until state governments existed in name only.

This government is to possess absolute and uncontrollable power, legislative, executive and judicial, with respect to every object to which it extends, for by the last clause of section 8th, article 1st, it is declared "that the Congress shall have the power to make all laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into execution the foregoing powers..." And by the 6th article, it is declared "that this constitution, and the laws of the United States, which shall be made in pursuance thereof... shall be the supreme law of the land..." The authority to lay and collect taxes, duties, imposts, and excises, is the most important of any power that can be granted; it connects with it almost all other powers... It is obvious the legislature of the United States will have power to extend this power of taxation to every object that it will easily preclude the state legislatures from raising funds for their own support... A power to raise and support armies at pleasure, as well in peace as in war, tends to the destruction of liberty... If respect is to be paid to the opinion of the greatest and wisest men... we shall be constrained to conclude, that a free republic cannot succeed over a country of such immense extent... In a large republic, the public good is sacrificed to a thousand views; it is subordinate to exceptions and depends on accidents. In a small one, the interest of the public is easier perceived, better understood, and more within the reach of every citizen.

Brutus was not simply predicting tyranny for its own sake. His underlying assumption was the same one held by most republican theorists of the era: government works best, and stays most accountable, when it stays small and close to the people it governs. A representative who lives among the people he represents, and who must return to ordinary life among them, will govern differently than one who sits in a distant capital, overseeing a vast and diverse population he can never fully know.

📝 AP Tip
A common exam trap is treating Brutus No. 1 as simply "anti-government" in a generic sense. It is more precise, and will earn more credit, to say Brutus specifically opposed a large, centralized national government, not government in general; Brutus was entirely comfortable with strong state governments, which he saw as small enough to remain accountable.

Comparing the Two Arguments

The clearest way to hold both essays in your head at once is through a single shared question: how big should a republic be, and why? Madison answers: as large as possible, because size itself is the safeguard against faction. Brutus answers: as small as possible, because only smallness keeps representatives accountable and prevents the national government from swallowing the states.

  • Madison trusted structural complexity, many factions, many branches, many levels of government, to check power. Brutus trusted direct accountability, representatives who personally know and live among their constituents, to check power.
  • Madison saw a large, diverse republic as a strength that prevented any single faction from dominating. Brutus saw the same size and diversity as a weakness that made representation shallow and unreliable.
  • Both essays ultimately shaped the finished Constitution: Madison's structural arguments are reflected in federalism and separation of powers, while Anti-Federalist pressure like Brutus's is widely credited as a major reason the Bill of Rights was added in 1791.

History did not clearly vindicate either writer. The federal government has grown far larger than Brutus feared, partly through the necessary and proper clause and Supremacy Clause he warned about. Whether Madison's solution actually worked is a genuinely open question, not a settled one. Reasonable people looking at American politics today disagree sharply about whether a large, diverse republic has kept any single faction from dominating everyone else, or whether deep partisan division shows Madison's confidence was, at least in part, misplaced.

Develop an argument that explains whether a large republic or a small republic is a better safeguard of liberty in the United States.

Use at least one of these foundational documents: Federalist No. 10, Brutus No. 1, The U.S. Constitution.

A large republic is a better safeguard of liberty, because as Madison argues in Federalist No. 10, extending the sphere makes it harder for any single faction to capture the whole government, a danger Brutus underestimates when he treats size itself as the threat.

  • Thesis: Take Madison's side or Brutus's side. Do not only summarize both essays.
  • Evidence from a foundational document: Quote or closely paraphrase Federalist No. 10 (extend the sphere; representatives refine public views) or Brutus No. 1 (necessary and proper clause, supremacy clause, representatives too distant).
  • Additional evidence: Use a constitutional feature (House elections, Senate, Bill of Rights) or a later example of federal power versus local control.
  • Alternate perspective: If you side with Madison, answer Brutus's fear of a distant national government. If you side with Brutus, answer Madison's claim that a large republic controls faction better than a small one.
Model answer: A strong answer engages directly with Madison's logic: since faction arises from differing opinions and interests, which are a natural byproduct of free thought and free association, suppressing faction would require suppressing those freedoms too, though a student could reasonably argue other tools, like campaign finance limits, attempt to reduce faction's harms without eliminating liberty outright.
Model answer: A strong answer could argue either direction: that modern communication (social media, email, town halls) lets representatives stay closely informed about constituent views despite distance, weakening Brutus's original concern, or that the sheer scale of modern districts and the influence of money in politics have made representatives even more distant from ordinary constituents than Brutus feared.
Model answer: Answers will vary, but a strong response cites specific claims from both essays (not just general impressions) and explains clearly why the chosen argument's underlying assumption about human nature or government power is more convincing.
Model answer: It suggests that losing a specific political fight does not mean losing all influence over the outcome; sustained public pressure and credible arguments can still reshape the winning side's final product, even without changing the ultimate result.
Model answer: Answers will vary and should be evaluated on the quality of the argument, not which side is taken. A student arguing Madison was right might point to the sheer number and variety of interest groups and factions still actively competing today, with no single one achieving lasting dominance. A student arguing Madison was wrong, or only partly right, might point to intense two-party polarization as evidence that factions have consolidated into two large, entrenched blocs rather than staying too numerous and divided to coordinate, which is closer to the exact scenario Madison believed a large republic would prevent.
