1.3 Challenges of the Articles of Confederation

🏛️ Political
  • A government designed to be weak: The Articles of Confederation deliberately created a national government with almost no independent power, no executive, no national courts, no power to tax, and no power to regulate trade, because the states feared repeating British-style tyranny.
  • The weaknesses became a crisis: Without tax revenue, the national government could not pay its war debts or fund an army. Without power to regulate interstate commerce, states taxed each other's goods and undercut the national economy.
  • Shays' Rebellion forced the issue: An armed uprising of indebted farmers in Massachusetts in 1786 to 1787, which the national government proved powerless to respond to effectively, convinced many leaders that a stronger national government was necessary, directly leading to the Constitutional Convention.

Try each question before revealing the answer.

1. Why did the authors of the Articles of Confederation deliberately create a national government with such limited power?
Having just fought a revolution against what they saw as British tyranny, the authors feared recreating a powerful, potentially abusive central government, so they intentionally kept most governing power with the individual states.
2. Explain one specific way the lack of a national taxing power weakened the government under the Articles.
Without the power to directly tax citizens, Congress could only request funds from the states, which frequently refused or delayed payment, leaving the national government unable to pay its war debts or adequately fund an army.
3. How did Shays' Rebellion change the political conversation about the Articles of Confederation?
The national government's inability to respond effectively to the armed uprising convinced many previously skeptical leaders that the Articles were too weak to maintain order and protect property, turning "revise the Articles" into "replace the Articles" as the dominant political position.
Learning Objectives
  • Explain the challenges faced by the government under the Articles of Confederation.
  • Explain how the weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation led to a call for a stronger national government.
  • Explain how Shays' Rebellion is used as evidence for the need for constitutional reform.
Key Vocabulary

Tap a term for its definition.

  • Definition: The first constitution of the United States, ratified in 1781, which created a national government with very limited powers and left most authority with the individual states. Significance: Its practical failures directly motivated the Constitutional Convention of 1787 and shaped many design choices in the Constitution that replaced it. Example: Under the Articles, Congress could request money from the states but had no power to actually collect taxes directly from citizens.
  • Definition: A political system in which independent states or governments form a loose alliance, with the central authority holding only the specific powers the member states choose to delegate to it. Significance: Describes the basic structure of government under the Articles, where states retained the vast majority of governing power. Example: Under the Articles, each state retained "its sovereignty, freedom, and independence."
  • Definition: An armed uprising of indebted Massachusetts farmers in 1786 to 1787, led by Daniel Shays, protesting high taxes and aggressive debt collection during a severe economic depression. Significance: The national government's inability to respond effectively became the single most cited piece of evidence that the Articles of Confederation needed to be replaced, not just revised. Example: Massachusetts had to rely on a privately funded militia to put down the rebellion because the national government under the Articles had no standing army of its own.
  • Definition: The requirement under the Articles of Confederation that all thirteen state legislatures agree before the Articles themselves could be amended. Significance: Made it nearly impossible to fix even widely recognized flaws in the Articles, since a single dissenting state could block any reform. Example: A 1781 proposal to let Congress collect a small tax on imported goods failed because Rhode Island alone refused to approve it.
  • Definition: Trade and economic activity that crosses state lines. Significance: Congress under the Articles had no power to regulate interstate commerce, so states taxed each other's goods and pursued conflicting trade policies, fragmenting the national economy. Example: New York taxed goods shipped from New Jersey and Connecticut, provoking retaliation and economic tension between neighboring states.
  • Definition: An 1787 law passed under the Articles of Confederation establishing the process for admitting new states from the Northwest Territory and banning slavery in that territory. Significance: Widely considered the single major legislative success of the Confederation Congress, showing the government could still function even as it approached collapse. Example: The Northwest Ordinance created the basic template, an organized territory maturing into full statehood, that the United States would use to admit most future states.

A Government Built to Be Weak, On Purpose

The Articles of Confederation, ratified in 1781, were the first constitution of the United States. To understand why they turned out so weak, it helps to remember what the states had just finished fighting. Americans had just fought a war against a distant, powerful central government they believed had trampled their rights. When it came time to design their own national government, the last thing most state leaders wanted was to recreate that same danger. The result was a confederation: a loose alliance of sovereign states that delegated only a narrow, carefully limited set of powers to a national Congress.

On paper, this looked like a reasonable safeguard against tyranny. In practice, it left the national government too weak to function even in ordinary circumstances, let alone a crisis.

The Specific Weaknesses

Under the Articles, the national government was just a single-chamber Congress. There was no separate branch of any kind: no president or executive branch to enforce the laws Congress passed, and no national court system to resolve disputes between states or interpret national law consistently. On top of that missing structure, Congress itself had almost no real power: no power to tax directly, no power to regulate interstate commerce, and no way to fix any of it, since amending the Articles required unanimous consent from all thirteen state legislatures.

These weaknesses were not five separate problems. They fed each other. Because Congress had no power to tax, it could not raise its own revenue, it could only request money from the states, which routinely refused, delayed, or paid only a fraction of what was asked. Because it had no independent revenue, it could not reliably pay the young nation's Revolutionary War debts, and it could not fund a real standing army of its own. That specific gap, no tax power leading directly to no army, is exactly what left the national government powerless a few years later when it actually needed force to respond to a crisis.

📝 AP Tip
When an exam question asks you to "explain a challenge faced by the government under the Articles of Confederation," always name the SPECIFIC weakness (no taxing power, no executive, no national courts, unanimous consent to amend, no power over interstate commerce) rather than vaguely saying "the government was too weak." For full credit on a question that asks you to explain a CONSEQUENCE of a weakness, trace the chain: no tax power meant no revenue, which meant no funded army, not just "the government was weak so bad things happened."

The lack of commerce power caused a similar chain reaction. Because Congress could not regulate trade between states, individual states were free to tax goods coming from their neighbors as though those goods were foreign imports, and many did. New York, for example, taxed goods shipped in from New Jersey and Connecticut, provoking retaliation and deepening economic fragmentation at exactly the moment the young nation needed unified economic policy to recover from the war. And because fixing any of this required unanimous consent, a single state with something to lose could block the fix. In 1781, a proposal to let Congress collect a small tax on imported goods, which would have addressed the taxing-power problem directly, failed for exactly this reason: every state but Rhode Island approved it.

💡 Fun Fact
The Confederation Congress was not a complete failure. Its most significant legislative achievement, the Northwest Ordinance of 1787, established the process for turning federally organized territories into new states and banned slavery in the Northwest Territory. That basic template, organized territory growing into full statehood, became the model the United States used to admit most states that joined the union afterward.

Shays' Rebellion: The Weakness Becomes a Crisis

The abstract weaknesses of the Articles became an urgent, concrete crisis in 1786 and 1787, during Shays' Rebellion. Massachusetts was in the middle of a severe economic depression. Many farmers, veterans of the Revolutionary War among them, had fallen deep into debt, and the state was aggressively seizing farms and jailing debtors to collect what was owed. Led by Daniel Shays, a former Continental Army captain, groups of armed farmers shut down courts to prevent further debt collection and eventually marched on a federal armory in Springfield, Massachusetts.

The national government's response revealed exactly how weak it had become. Congress had no standing army it could send. Massachusetts had to rely on a privately funded state militia, paid for largely by wealthy merchants, to finally put down the uprising. For many political leaders who had until then defended the Articles, this was the breaking point: if the national government could not even guarantee basic order and protect property within its own borders, revising the Articles piecemeal was no longer enough.

📝 AP Tip
Shays' Rebellion is one of the single most cited pieces of evidence on the AP exam for why the Constitutional Convention happened. If an FRQ prompt asks for evidence that the Articles of Confederation needed to be replaced, Shays' Rebellion is almost always a safe, strong, specific example, especially paired with the detail that the national government had no army of its own to respond.

From Crisis to Constitutional Convention

Shays' Rebellion did not single-handedly cause the Constitutional Convention. Concerns about interstate trade disputes and the national government's financial weakness had already led to smaller meetings, including the Annapolis Convention of 1786, which called for a larger gathering to revise the Articles. But the rebellion supplied urgent, vivid, undeniable proof that those abstract concerns had real, dangerous consequences. When delegates gathered in Philadelphia in May 1787, they were officially instructed only to revise the Articles of Confederation. Instead, convinced by experiences like Shays' Rebellion that revision was not enough, they wrote an entirely new constitution, one that gave the national government the power to tax, created an executive branch and a national judiciary, and gave Congress explicit authority to regulate interstate commerce, addressing, point by point, nearly every major weakness the Articles had exposed.

Congress had no power to regulate interstate commerce, so states could tax each other's goods as if they were foreign imports.

B

Interstate tariffs and retaliation made trade harder and the economy worse, which convinced many leaders that only a national government with real commerce power could stop states from undermining each other.

The commerce clause gave Congress power to regulate trade among the states, so a state could no longer treat a neighboring state's goods like foreign imports.

Develop an argument that explains whether the structural weaknesses of the Articles of Confederation or a single event such as Shays' Rebellion was more responsible for the calling of the Constitutional Convention.

The Articles' structural weaknesses were more responsible than Shays' Rebellion, because the lack of taxing power, commerce power, and a workable amendment rule had already produced years of crisis before the Massachusetts uprising made those failures impossible to ignore.

  • Thesis: Pick a primary cause: long-term structural failure, or the rebellion as the decisive trigger. A thesis that treats them as equally important without weighing them is weaker.
  • Evidence from a foundational document: Use the Articles (no tax power, unanimous amendment rule, weak executive) or the Constitution (commerce clause, taxing power, Article V) as the attempted fix.
  • Additional evidence: Unpaid war debts, interstate tariffs, the failed 1781 impost, or Shays' Rebellion and Congress's inability to respond.
  • Alternate perspective: If you emphasize structure, explain why the rebellion still mattered as a catalyst. If you emphasize the rebellion, explain why the same uprising would have been less decisive if the Articles had already been able to tax, borrow, and keep order.
Model answer: A strong answer engages with the trade-off directly: the Articles' weaknesses (no taxing power, no commerce power, no ability to maintain order during Shays' Rebellion) suggest a real overcorrection, though a student could also argue the Articles served a necessary transitional purpose by proving, through painful experience, exactly how much national power was actually needed.
Model answer: A strong answer holds two things at once: that the farmers' economic grievances were genuine and sympathetic, and that the rebellion's political effect, exposing the national government's powerlessness, was significant regardless of how one judges the farmers' cause on its own merits.
Model answer: Answers will vary, but a strong response considers which specific weaknesses (taxing power, commerce power, executive branch) could realistically have been fixed through amendment, and whether a lower threshold would have been enough to overcome the same state-level self-interest that blocked reform under unanimous consent.
Model answer: It suggests that a government can fail structurally, be unable to tax, defend itself, or regulate commerce, while still producing important, lasting policy in narrower areas, meaning a fair historical judgment has to weigh specific achievements against systemic weaknesses rather than treating the outcome as all-or-nothing.
